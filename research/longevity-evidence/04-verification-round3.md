## R1 — contradiction-search · 裁决:restricted-use

### 允许/禁止的用法

USABLE, WITH THE CLAIM NARROWED TO BLOOD AND THE CORROBORATION NAMED.

THE ARTICLE MAY SAY:
- That whole-blood NAD+ has now been measured against age by at least two fully independent groups — Trętowicz et al. 2026 (Nature Metabolism, seven cohorts, UHPLC-HRMS, Netherlands/Finland/Spain) and Yang et al. 2022 (Frontiers in Endocrinology, n=1,518, enzymatic cycling assay cross-validated against LC-MS/MS, China) — and that neither finds a consistent decline across the adult age range. This is genuine independent replication: different teams, cohorts, countries, and assay chemistries.
- That there is no meta-analysis or systematic review of NAD+ versus age in humans, anywhere. (Verified null search.)
- That no one has ever measured NAD+ twice in the same person across ageing — every study on both sides is cross-sectional. (Verified null search.)
- That the human evidence FOR an age-related decline is thinner and less independent than the field's confidence implies: the two most-cited datasets, Massudi 2012 (skin) and Clement 2019 (plasma), share an author (Braidy) and an institution (UNSW Sydney) and so do not corroborate each other; and in brain 31P-MRS the larger, independent, Nestlé-funded study (Cuenoud 2020, n=50) reached only p≈0.06 where the small original (Zhu 2015, n=17) reported a clear decline.
- That reviews published in the same year as the target still assert the decline as settled background without appraising the human evidence.

THE ARTICLE MAY NOT SAY:
- That the paper shows NAD+ does not decline with age in humans, full stop. It measured whole blood. Its own abstract concedes the claim it is testing was made about blood AND TISSUES.
- That the paper refutes the NAD+ supplement industry's founding premise. An independent team using an independent tissue and an independent assay — Massudi et al. 2012, human skin, n=49 — reports strong age-correlated decline (r=-0.77 in males, -0.54 in females). That measurement stands unrebutted by a blood study and points the other way.
- That the finding is undisputed or that nothing contests it. Two independent whole-blood datasets (Yang 2022; Breton 2020) each report a sex-dependent pattern — modest decline in men, none in women, with a significant sex interaction in Yang (p=0.003). I could not verify whether the target stratified by sex, because its full text is paywalled with no PMC deposit and no locatable preprint. Until that is checked, the article must not present the target's null as covering men specifically.
- Any specific beta, confidence interval, or correlation coefficient from Yang 2022 without first re-reading them off the PDF. Those numbers came to me through a summarizer rendering of the open-access page, not from the document itself, and this pipeline has previously caught a summarizer fabricating quotes.
- Anything about the target's seven cohorts — their names, sizes, age ranges, independence from one another, or the study's statistical power — sourced from me. I could not open the paper. Every one of those is UNVERIFIED and belongs to the methods seat.

REQUIRED HEDGE IF THE FINDING CARRIES A SECTION: pair it with the sentence that the null is established in blood and that blood may simply be the wrong compartment — which is an argument against blood NAD+ as a biomarker (what the paper actually claims) rather than an argument against tissue NAD+ decline (what the industry premise actually asserts).

### 论证

WHAT QUANTITY IS ACTUALLY CLAIMED. The target measures one thing: NAD+ concentration in whole blood, cross-sectionally, against chronological age, by UHPLC-HRMS. The article wants to use it for something wider: that "NAD+ declines with age" is not established in humans. Those are not the same quantity, and the gap between them decides this ruling.

THE INDEPENDENT SECOND OPINION ON THE NARROW CLAIM EXISTS AND CORROBORATES. Yang et al. 2022 is the strongest single result I found in either direction and it has been sitting in the literature for four years. n=1,518 healthy Chinese adults — larger than the target's largest plausible single cohort — with two orthogonal assays (enzymatic cycling, cross-checked against conventional LC-MS/MS) and zero author, cohort, funder, or upstream-dataset overlap with the Amsterdam/Helsinki/Leiden/Valencia consortium. Its overall unstratified result is a null. That is a real upgrade: independent team, independent data, same quantity, same direction. Breton 2020 (France, third assay chemistry, again no overlap) adds a third independent blood dataset. On the narrow blood claim the target is NOT a single source, and the article can say so with confidence.

WHY I DID NOT RULE upgrade-multisource. Two things stop it.

First, the corroborators are not clean nulls. Yang and Breton — independently of each other, in different countries with different methods — both report the same sex asymmetry: a modest decline in men, none in women, with a significant interaction term in Yang (p=0.003). Two independent teams converging on the same structured deviation from a flat null is exactly the signal pattern one is supposed to take seriously, and it is small enough (~6% of the male mean) that a sex-pooled analysis would wash it out. I could not open the target's full text — no PMC deposit, Nature redirects to an auth wall, Europe PMC's article pages were down, and bioRxiv returned 403 — so I cannot say whether the target stratified by sex. If it did not, then the target and Yang agree at the pooled level while both may be concealing a male-specific effect, and "does not vary with age" is stronger than either dataset licenses.

Second, and decisive: an independent measurement in a different compartment points the other way. Massudi 2012 measured NAD+ in human skin biopsies (n=49, cycling assay, UNSW) and found r=-0.77 in males and -0.54 in females against age. That does not contradict the blood result — it contradicts the extrapolation. Since the article's intended use IS the extrapolation, the contrary measurement bites.

WHY I DID NOT RULE direction-contested EITHER. The contrary evidence is real but weaker than the field's confidence suggests, and the article deserves to know that too. The two most-cited pro-decline human datasets, Massudi 2012 and Clement 2019, share Nady Braidy and the UNSW cluster — so the pro-decline side commits the very independence failure already flagged against Vinten/Houtkooper on the pro-null side. This is the symmetry the article should report: BOTH camps' apparent replication is partly one lab twice. And in brain 31P-MRS the pattern is a classic shrinking effect — Zhu 2015 (n=17, split 7/4/6) reports a clear decline; the independent, larger Cuenoud 2020 (n=50) gets p≈0.06. Cuenoud's Nestlé funding, with the funder involved in design and analysis, would bias TOWARD confirming a decline, and it still failed to clear significance. So the tissue side is one unreplicated small brain study, one near-null larger brain study, and one skin study from a lab cluster that also produced the plasma study.

THE TWO DOCUMENTED ABSENCES ARE THE MOST QUOTABLE THING I FOUND. No meta-analysis or systematic review of NAD+ versus age in humans exists — the only hits are supplementation-trial meta-analyses. And no one has ever measured the same person's NAD+ twice across ageing; every dataset on both sides is cross-sectional. A trillion-dollar-adjacent supplement premise rests on a handful of small cross-sectional studies, several of which are not independent of each other, with no synthesis and no longitudinal data. That is a stronger and more defensible story than "one paper debunked NAD+ decline," and it does not depend on the target carrying weight it cannot carry.

RESTRICTED-USE is therefore the honest call. The finding survives, it is genuinely multi-source on blood, and it can anchor the section — but only with the claim narrowed to blood, the corroborating study named rather than left implicit, the sex-stratification question flagged as open, and Massudi's skin result acknowledged rather than omitted. The article should also note that the target has been cited exactly once as of today (Picca & Ferrucci, a review) and carries no published challenge — it is too new for the field to have responded.

PROVENANCE DISCIPLINE. Everything above traces to PubMed records, the PLoS One and Frontiers open-access pages, and the Semantic Scholar Graph API. I never filled a gap from memory. Three things I could not open (the target's full text, the Picca review, a possible bioRxiv preprint) are marked UNVERIFIED rather than assumed. And the Yang coefficients reached me through a WebFetch summarizer, not the PDF, so I have flagged them for re-verification before print rather than passing them along as solid.

## R1 — methods-audit · 裁决:restricted-use

### 允许/禁止的用法

MAY SAY, with these exact scope limits:
- That a 2026 Nature Metabolism study (Trętowicz et al., 8(6):1282-1290) measured NAD+ by validated UHPLC-HRMS across seven human cohorts (total N approximately 303) and found whole-blood NAD+ levels stable across the age range sampled.
- That the study's own stated conclusion is that it "challenge[s] the utility of blood NAD+ levels as a biomarker of ageing or lifestyle factors" — a biomarker claim about blood.
- That the paper's abstract concedes prior human blood evidence was already "inconsistent," so the blood literature was contested before this paper and this paper is a better-instrumented entrant, not a settlement.
- That the result excludes only LARGE age differences in whole blood — the authors report 80% power to detect differences greater than 7 nmol/mL at n=20 per group, roughly 2 SD. If the article cites the null at all, it should cite this power limit in the same breath.
- That whole-blood NAD+ is predominantly erythrocyte-derived and that erythrocytes lack mitochondria and nuclei — quoting the authors' own limitations — and that this makes whole blood a poor proxy for the tissue NAD+ pool the supplement premise refers to.

MUST NOT SAY:
- "NAD+ does not decline with age" without the "in whole blood" qualifier. The paper does not test tissue and explicitly disclaims it.
- That the study "refutes," "debunks," "disproves," or "overturns" the NAD+-decline premise, or that it is the strongest evidence against it. It is a bounded, underpowered null in the wrong compartment.
- That "no decline was found" is evidence that no decline exists. No equivalence test was performed; this is a failure to reject.
- Any numeric effect estimate, confidence interval, p value, or percent-change figure for the age-NAD+ association. I could not retrieve one from any accessible source. Do not invent, estimate, or infer one — and note that a summarizer-derived "median ~17.5 nmol/mL" surfaced during this audit is an arithmetic hallucination from the SD and must not be used.
- That seven cohorts constitute seven independent corroborations. One assay, one lab.
- That the finding generalizes to the general population. The cohorts include a longevity-enriched family study, a cardiovascular-risk group, and elite athletes.
- That the twin NR positive control proves the assay could have detected an age effect. It proves detection of a 5-month 250 mg-to-1 g pharmacological dose, an effect far larger than any plausible age effect.
- That industry funding undermines this result. It does not; the direction is wrong. Mention Nestlé Health Science funding alongside the "no competing interests" statement only if disclosure practice is itself the subject.

REQUIRED PAIRING: this source may not stand alone as the NAD+ section's spine. It must be presented alongside the contradiction seat's tissue-level evidence (muscle, brain, PBMC), and the section's claim must be rewritten from "the premise is refuted" to something like "the premise is weakly evidenced in humans and the best-instrumented blood measurement finds no large decline." If the contradiction seat returns independent evidence of a tissue-level decline, this paper's role shrinks further, to a statement about blood biomarkers only.

### 论证

The paper is genuine, peer-reviewed in a strong journal, methodologically careful about analytical variability, and its assay work is a real contribution. It is not an artifact, so VETO is not warranted. But it cannot bear the weight the article wants to put on it — "the strongest single piece of evidence that the founding premise of the NAD+ supplement industry is not established in humans" — for three independent reasons, any one of which would be sufficient to restrict it.

First, the null is underpowered on the paper's own arithmetic. The age-designed cell is 20 vs 20, and Extended Data Fig. 4b concedes an 80%-power MDE of 7 nmol/mL against a stated SD of 3.5 nmol/mL. That is a minimum detectable effect of 2 SD, roughly Cohen's d of 2.0, where a normally-powered n=20/group design detects d of about 0.91. The study is blind to anything smaller than a very large difference, and the declines claimed in the prior literature plausibly sit entirely inside that blind spot. Worse, the MDE is inflated by the study's own freeze-thaw and multi-batch noise on reused biobank samples. There is no equivalence test, so the result is a failure to reject rather than a demonstration of negligibility — the textbook null-as-absence-of-evidence error, and exactly the failure mode the article would be committing by leaning on it.

Second, and independently fatal to the intended use: the measured compartment is the wrong one, and the authors say so themselves. Their Discussion states that whole-blood NAD+ is predominantly erythrocyte-derived and that erythrocytes lack mitochondria and nuclei. The NAD+-decline hypothesis is mechanistically about PARP/CD38/mitochondrial consumption in nucleated cells. Analytical validation of the assay in whole blood is not construct validation of whole blood as a proxy for the tissue pool. This goes beyond the already-flagged issue (i): it is not merely that the abstract concedes a blood/tissue scope mismatch, it is that the paper's own stated biology explains why the blood compartment should be expected to be insensitive to the hypothesized mechanism. A null there is close to uninformative about the premise.

Third, selection runs toward the finding. The largest age-relevant cohort is the Leiden Longevity Study, genetically enriched for exceptional survival — the population where decline would be most attenuated. The others are a cardiovascular-risk group, elite athletes, and screened-healthy over-60 survivors. Add an unadjusted hematocrit confound (the authors demonstrate hematocrit correlates with the signal, then model age with adjustment for sex only) and the age coefficient is uninterpretable rather than reassuring.

Two structural points the article should not overstate: "seven independent cohorts" is seven sample sets run through one assay in one lab, so systematic artifacts propagate rather than cancel; and the NR positive control validates sensitivity to a pharmacological megadose, not to effects in the size range the age question occupies.

On conflicts, I want to be fair rather than merely hostile. Nestlé Health Science funding sits oddly beside "no competing interests," but a null on age-related decline is against supplement-industry interest, so the funding does not explain the finding and must not be used to impeach it.

Net: this survives as one carefully-measured source supporting a narrow, bounded claim about whole blood as a biomarker. It does not survive as the spine of a section arguing the industry premise is refuted. The article's NAD+ section needs either the tissue-level evidence the contradiction seat is chartered to find, or a rewritten claim scoped to what this paper actually shows.

So the minimum detectable effect is 7 nmol/mL = 2.0 x SD, i.e. Cohen's d of approximately 2.0. A two-sample design at n=20/group with 80% power at two-sided alpha 0.05 normally detects d of about 0.91; quoting an MDE of 2 SD means the study is powered ONLY for an enormous effect. Any true age-related difference smaller than 7 nmol/mL — which plausibly covers the entire range of declines claimed in the prior literature — would have been missed. This is a bounded null, not an absent effect.

- THE POWER CALCULATION IS INFLATED BY THE STUDY'S OWN PRE-ANALYTICAL NOISE. The 3.5 nmol/mL SD comes from a fresh-vs-frozen comparison (n=15), and Extended Data Fig. 4a models 'biological variation alone, biological plus analytical variation (single-batch analysis), biological plus analytical variation across multiple batches, and the combined effects of biological, analytical, and freeze-thaw variation.' The 7 nmol/mL MDE therefore reflects a noise budget dominated by banked-sample handling, not by biology. The cohorts are reused, previously-described biobank collections whose pre-analytical histories the authors did not control. A noisy instrument returning a null is weak evidence of absence — the authors deserve credit for quantifying the noise honestly, but the quantification is precisely what disqualifies the null from carrying a strong claim.

- NO EQUIVALENCE TEST; NULL REPORTED AS ABSENCE. I found no TOST or any formal equivalence procedure anywhere in the accessible text. 'Remain remarkably stable with age' is a failure to reject, not a demonstration that the effect is negligible. Compounding this, I could not retrieve a single effect estimate for the age-NAD+ association — no beta, no R-squared, no p value, no confidence interval — from the abstract, figure legends, or accessible Results. I state this as a limit of my audit, not as proof of omission (full text is paywalled and there is no PMC deposit), but the article MUST NOT assert a quantified null it cannot cite. If the section is written, someone must obtain the CI from the full text; without it the claim rests on the authors' adjective.

- THE MEASURED COMPARTMENT IS THE WRONG ONE, AND THE AUTHORS SAY SO. Verbatim Discussion limitations: 'First, we measured NAD+ in whole blood rather than in specific tissue types or cell compartments.' 'Second, whole-blood NAD+ is predominantly derived from erythrocytes, which lack mitochondria and nuclei.' 'Third, our findings may not generalize to other populations, tissues, or disease states.' This is a construct-validity failure for the article's intended use. The mechanistic NAD+-decline hypothesis runs through CD38 upregulation, PARP activation on nuclear DNA damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction — none of which operate in an anucleate, amitochondrial red cell. The assay is analytically validated for measuring NAD+ in whole blood; that is NOT the same as whole blood being a valid proxy for the tissue pool the industry premise refers to.

- HEMATOCRIT IS A LIVE CONFOUND LEFT OUT OF THE MODEL. Extended Data Fig. 7 plots 'Whole-blood NAD+ concentrations (nmol/mL) plotted against hematocrit in a subset of individuals from the CardioHT cohort (n = 20)' — the authors themselves establish that the RBC fraction drives the signal. Yet the stated model is 'linear regression adjusted for sex' only. No adjustment for hematocrit, hemoglobin, anemia, kidney function, medication, BMI, fasting status, or time of sampling is evident. Since hematocrit falls with age in many older adults, an RBC-dominated measure should if anything show a spurious age decline; recovering a null in an unadjusted model means the age coefficient is uninterpretable rather than reassuring.

- SELECTION BIAS RUNS IN THE DIRECTION OF THE FINDING. The Leiden Longevity Study (n=70, the largest age-relevant cohort) is a family-based collection genetically enriched for exceptional survival — the single population in which age-related decline would most plausibly be attenuated. CardioHT is a cardiovascular-risk group, ELITE includes elite athletes, and the '>60 healthy' recruits are by construction survivors. None of these are general-population samples. Using them jointly to support 'NAD+ does not decline with age in humans' is a generalization failure independent of the power problem.

- 'SEVEN INDEPENDENT COHORTS' IS SEVEN SAMPLE SETS, ONE MEASUREMENT SYSTEM. The cohorts are independently recruited, but every sample was run on a single UHPLC-HRMS platform in one laboratory (the Amsterdam UMC group — Schomakers, van Weeghel). Any systematic compartment or pre-analytical artifact propagates identically across all seven. The plural does not multiply the evidence the way the phrase implies, and the article must not present 'seven cohorts' as seven corroborations.

- THE POSITIVE CONTROL DOES NOT LICENSE THE NULL. The authors demonstrate assay sensitivity via the twin-pair NR study — 5 months of escalating nicotinamide riboside, 250 mg to 1 g daily, n=24. That is a pharmacological megadose perturbation whose effect size is far larger than any plausible gradual physiological age effect. Detecting it establishes that the assay is not broken; it does NOT establish sensitivity in the effect-size range where the age question actually lives (below the stated 7 nmol/mL MDE).

- DISCLOSURE INCONSISTENCY, BUT IT DOES NOT CUT AGAINST THE NULL. The paper states 'The authors declare no competing interests' while the funding section lists Nestlé Health Science (funder of the MEJNES2019 cohort), alongside EU Horizon Europe NADIS (101073251), Academy of Finland, NWO, and Spanish Ministerio de Ciencia grants. Flagging the mismatch for completeness — but note the direction: a null on age-related decline is contrary to supplement-industry interest, so industry funding does not explain the finding. The NR-raises-NAD+ result is the industry-favorable part. Do not use the COI to impeach the null.

- THE PAPER'S OWN CLAIM IS NARROWER THAN THE ARTICLE'S INTENDED USE. Verbatim abstract: 'NAD+ levels in blood and tissues are widely proposed to decline with age, yet evidence in human blood is inconsistent... Our results challenge the utility of blood NAD+ levels as a biomarker of ageing or lifestyle factors.' The paper claims a biomarker-utility conclusion about blood. It does not claim, and its design cannot support, that tissue NAD+ is stable with age. It also concedes the prior human blood literature is already 'inconsistent' — so this is a better-instrumented entrant into a mixed literature, not a decisive refutation of it.

## R2 — contradiction-search · 裁决:restricted-use

### 允许/禁止的用法

MAY SAY: (1) That RAPA-EX-01 (Stanfield et al., J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle 2026, PMID 41985884, ACTRN12624000790549) is the first and, as of this search, ONLY randomised placebo-controlled human trial testing weekly rapamycin against a physical-function endpoint in a non-transplant population — a 1,203-record sweep found no second one. (2) That its primary ITT endpoint was null (-2.13 reps, 95% CI -4.61 to 0.34, p=0.089) and numerically favoured placebo. (3) That the trial's prespecified complete-case (-2.46, p=0.045) and per-protocol (-3.44, p=0.007) sensitivity analyses were significant in favour of placebo — this MUST accompany any 'null' framing; omitting it understates what the authors reported. (4) That an HbA1c rise of +1.74 mmol/mol (95% CI 0.20-3.27, p=0.030) was observed. (5) That this HbA1c direction is CORROBORATED by two independent human datasets — PEARL's significant HbA1c increase in males on 5 mg weekly (p=0.024, n=114 completers, 48 weeks) and Gonzales 2025's reported HbA1c increase on daily 1 mg — and by an independent mouse study in which once-weekly rapamycin still impaired glucose tolerance during exercise training. (6) That there is NO human evidence that intermittent dosing spares mTORC2. This is now more than an absence: RAPA-EX-01 cites the PEARL trial (its reference 28) as the basis for the claim, and PEARL measured no pharmacodynamic markers whatsoever. A literal search for 'mTORC2-sparing'-type phrasing tied to intermittent dosing returned zero records. (7) That both trials were funded by the same longevity-community crowdfunding source (Lifespan.io) and neither by industry. MUST NOT SAY: (a) That the functional result is replicated or corroborated — it is not, and the closest design analogue (Elliehausen 2025, once-weekly rapamycin plus progressive wheel running, Aging Cell, PMID 40704394, from the fully independent Konopka/Lamming/Hornberger group) found NO compromise of exercise-induced strength, capacity or hypertrophy. That contradiction must be reported wherever the functional harm signal is asserted. (b) That the trial 'showed rapamycin harms function' — it was powered only for a large effect (Cohen's d ~0.80-0.90), the authors label it exploratory, and it cannot exclude a modest benefit. (c) That the HbA1c contrast is a clean safety finding on its own — it is one contrast in an unadjusted panel of at least eight lab families, and the paper states verbatim that no multiplicity adjustment was made. Its weight comes from the independent corroboration, not from p=0.030. (d) That RAPA-EX-01 and PEARL are two independent sources: they share the Lifespan.io funder, RAPA-EX-01 co-author Kaeberlein co-published with three of PEARL's seven AgelessRx authors, and RAPA-EX-01 takes its dose rationale from PEARL. Call them 'separate cohorts from an interlinked community' — not 'independent replication'. (e) That Kraig 2018 showed rapamycin raises HbA1c: the Hands 2025 review says so, but Kraig's own abstract reports no OGTT change in glucose, insulin secretion or insulin sensitivity. Cite the primary or drop it. (f) Any claim that this question is settled: two genuinely independent replications with ~3.5x the sample (RapaLoad NCT06789900, n=136, Active-not-recruiting; StrongBone NCT07191353, n=148, Recruiting — both weekly everolimus 5 mg plus structured exercise, Odense University Hospital) are running and unpublished. The article should say so. (g) Anything about whether the registered primary endpoint matches the published one — the ANZCTR record returned HTTP 403 and could not be checked. Flag as unverified for the methods seat rather than asserting a match.

### 论证

I treated the finding as three separable quantities and they do not get the same verdict, which is why this is restricted-use rather than a single clean upgrade or contest.

THE FUNCTIONAL QUANTITY (change in 30-s chair-stands under weekly 6 mg sirolimus) is single-source and will stay that way for at least a year. A 1,203-record Europe PMC sweep for randomised placebo-controlled rapamycin trials with any physical-function endpoint in non-transplant populations returned exactly one hit: this trial. The nearest independent measurement of the same construct is Elliehausen 2025 (Aging Cell, PMID 40704394) — once-weekly 2 mg/kg rapamycin plus eight weeks of progressive weighted wheel running — and it points the OTHER WAY: no compromise of maximal exercise capacity, grip strength or myofiber hypertrophy. That team (Konopka, Lamming, Hornberger, Wisconsin) has no author, funder or dataset overlap with Stanfield or Kaeberlein. It is mice, so it does not measure the same quantity and cannot demote the human trial — but the article cannot assert a functional harm direction while a genuinely independent, closely-matched preclinical test says the opposite. Two independent human replications of almost exactly this design (RapaLoad, StrongBone; weekly everolimus plus exercise in postmenopausal women, n=136 and n=148, Odense) are registered and unpublished, so the honest statement is 'one small exploratory trial, replications pending.'

THE METABOLIC QUANTITY genuinely upgrades. PEARL is a separate cohort, separate investigators, separate country of enrolment, 48 weeks instead of 13, 5-10 mg instead of 6 mg — and its full text reports a significant HbA1c increase in males on 5 mg (p=0.024), which the abstract's cheerful 'changes in blood biomarkers remained within normal ranges' obscures. Gonzales 2025 (daily 1 mg, early AD, UT Health San Antonio) independently reports HbA1c rising. Elliehausen's mice on INTERMITTENT dosing still showed impaired glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity. Three independent teams, three independent datasets, one direction. Kraig 2018 is the honest dissent (no OGTT change on daily 1 mg), and I flagged that the Hands review misreports it — which is exactly the kind of laundering that manufactures false consensus. On its own the RAPA-EX-01 HbA1c contrast is weak (unadjusted, one of eight lab families, the paper says so verbatim); with the corroboration it is reportable.

THE mTORC2 PREMISE is where I found the strongest result, and it goes the article's way harder than the article expected. I attacked the claimed absence from seven angles — Mannick's three elderly RCTs including the 5 mg and 20 mg weekly arms, muscle-biopsy searches, PBMC/phospho-AKT-Ser473 searches, the Konopka/Lamming programme, the Karolinska PK study, and a literal phrase search — and every one came back empty, the last with hitCount zero. Then the trial's own full text handed me the mechanism of the illusion: it asserts that 'prior human studies confirm... mTORC2 remains unaffected' and cites reference 28, which resolves to PEARL, which I read and which measured no pharmacodynamic marker of any kind. The field's central pharmacological premise is supported by a citation to a paper that does not contain the measurement. The article should say that, not merely that evidence is 'lacking.'

On independence hygiene: the two headline human trials share the Lifespan.io crowdfunding source, RAPA-EX-01's Kaeberlein co-published with three of PEARL's seven AgelessRx authors, and RAPA-EX-01 derives its dosing from PEARL. Cohorts and data are truly separate, so the metabolic corroboration survives; but presenting them as two independent trials would be the exact failure mode this seat exists to catch.

I tried to show this finding is an artifact and largely failed on the questions that would kill it: the trial is real, prospectively registered before first enrolment, has a publicly pre-published protocol, and its published primary endpoint matches the registered one exactly. Conduct was contracted to an independent trials unit and the statistician is independent of the sponsor. A negative result from a pro-drug team is the hardest kind to explain away, so I will not veto it.

What I did break is the article's intended framing, on four counts.

First, "null on its pre-specified primary endpoint" is wrong. The published protocol sets the nominal significance level at 0.1, and I verified this independently by arithmetic — the protocol's stated 80%/90% power for d=0.8/0.9 at n=20/arm reproduces only at alpha=0.10 (81%/89%), not alpha=0.05 (72%/81%). At the registered threshold, p=0.089 crosses. Compounding this, the p-value comes from a 999-replicate wild bootstrap whose Monte Carlo band is roughly 0.071-0.107, so it cannot adjudicate any threshold at all. The article must describe an estimate favouring placebo that did not reach conventional significance, and must not build a rhetorical beat on "null."

Second, the trial cannot support "rapamycin blunts exercise adaptation." The implied SE of 1.263 reps gives a minimum detectable effect of ~3.5 repetitions at conventional alpha; the observed -2.13 sits at ~60% of that, with ~39% post-hoc power. The protocol itself says the objective was feasibility and variance estimation. The significant per-protocol result (p=0.007) is the least trustworthy number in the paper, not the most: the effect grows monotonically as the analysis set narrows (ITT -2.13, complete-case -2.46, per-protocol -3.44) while dropout is differential (5 vs 3) and the per-protocol filter conditions on adherence in the arm with 57% more adverse events. That gradient is the signature of informative selection.

Third, the HbA1c signal fails multiplicity outright. Six of ~28 safety parameters were nominally significant with, in the paper's own words, no multiplicity adjustment. Bonferroni's threshold is 0.0018; under Benjamini-Hochberg FDR only MCV survives — HbA1c, total cholesterol, LDL, ALP and platelets all fail. And PEARL — longer and higher-dose — found blood biomarkers within normal ranges, so the external evidence does not back it up.

### 论证

The finding qualifies for upgrade-multisource, and the case is unusually clean for observational epidemiology.

WHAT QUANTITY IS AT STAKE. All-cause mortality of incident T2D patients initiated on metformin monotherapy, compared with matched individuals who do not have diabetes. The comparator is the key discriminator, and it is what separates the contested longevity claim from the uncontested active-comparator literature.

THE SECOND OPINION EXISTS AND IT AGREES WITH KEYS. Stevenson-Hoare, Leonenko and Escott-Price (BMC Public Health 2023, SAIL Databank, all-Wales, 1999-2018, 129,140 metformin patients) found metformin-treated patients survived a fraction 0.819 of matched non-diabetic controls' survival time over twenty years. Same contrast, same direction as Keys, opposite to Bannister.

INDEPENDENCE SURVIVES EVERY TEST I APPLIED. The brief warned that shared authors, cohort, funder or upstream dataset are how a field fakes replication, so I checked each. Author overlap across the three papers is zero — not one name recurs. The datasets are three different resources (CPRD, Danish national registers, SAIL). The funders are unrelated: Stevenson-Hoare's money comes from UK DRI/MRC/Alzheimer's Society, a dementia-genetics stream with no position on diabetes pharmacotherapy, and the authors declare no competing interests. Most tellingly, conception was independent: Stevenson-Hoare's reference list does not contain Keys, and its Research Square preprint predates Keys' October 2022 publication. Two teams reached the same reversal without seeing each other's work.

One honest caveat I want on the record: Bannister and Stevenson-Hoare are both at Cardiff University, though in unrelated departments (Currie's clinical-epidemiology/Pharmatelligence orbit vs Escott-Price's neuropsychiatric genetics centre), and CPRD historically included some Welsh practices, so partial patient overlap with SAIL cannot be excluded. Neither weakens the finding. Shared institution with zero shared authors and opposite conclusions is not a captured replication, and partial data overlap producing an opposite result makes the contradiction more striking, not less — if anything it removes 'different population' as an escape hatch.

THE REPLICATION DOES SOMETHING BETTER THAN CONTRADICT. By varying the simulated observation window, Stevenson-Hoare recovers Bannister's benefit at under three years and watches it invert after five. That converts a bare disagreement into a diagnosis: the founding result is a short-follow-up artefact, and the specific analytic choice that produces it is identifiable. Combined with Keys' forensic localisation — three of four crude mortality cells matching across countries to within a few percent while the metformin-treated cell differs 24.9 vs 14.4 — the article now has two structurally different lines of evidence converging on the same conclusion: the anomaly lives in Bannister's metformin arm, not in some general Denmark-vs-UK or Wales-vs-England difference.

THE ABSENCES ARE AS INFORMATIVE AS THE PRESENCES. No letter, comment, erratum, or reply to Keys exists anywhere; I enumerated all 11 citing works and classified each. The originating group has not returned to the comparison in the twelve years since — I enumerated all 14 Currie papers on metformin and mortality. No rebuttal to Stevenson-Hoare either. The reversal is not contested in print; it is simply unanswered.

WHAT I COULD NOT CLOSE. Campbell 2017's pooled HR 0.93 (0.88-0.99) for metformin users vs non-diabetics is the one apparent corroborator of Bannister still standing, and ScienceDirect blocked me (403) from seeing which studies carry that pool or how heavily Bannister weighs in it. This is the audit's real gap and I have flagged it as such rather than papering over it. Three observations constrain it even unopened: it is a 2017 synthesis and cannot contain the 2022 and 2023 reversals; it pools rather than independently measures; and its upper CI bound of 0.99 means the signal was marginal on arrival. It cannot be used as a second source against Keys, but neither can I declare it dead.

WHY NOT direction-contested. The only measurement pointing the other way is Bannister itself — the study under challenge — and it has now failed replication twice. Nothing independent and post-hoc points toward a metformin survival advantage over non-diabetics. Shadyab points a different direction on a different question and is disqualified by comparator. Campbell is unresolved but structurally incapable of adjudicating.

WHY NOT restricted-use, despite real limits. The limits here are about scope, not about whether the finding can bear weight: the reversal is Danish-plus-Welsh rather than global, no target trial emulation of this contrast has ever been attempted, and the deeper question of whether the metformin-vs-non-diabetic contrast is interpretable in either direction belongs to the methods seat. Those are boundaries on the claim, which I have written into permittedUse, not disqualifications of it. The specific thing the article needs — an independent team with independent data reversing the founding claim — is exactly what exists, twice over.

STRUCTURAL FINDING WORTH MORE THAN THE RULING. Assembling the four verified abstracts shows the literature is not in disarray at all. Bannister, Keys, Stevenson-Hoare and Shadyab all agree metformin outperforms sulfonylurea. They disagree only on metformin vs no diabetes. There is a stable replicated active-comparator result and a separate twice-refuted claim, and only the second is the longevity-drug hypothesis. If the article makes that separation explicit it will do more to clarify the field than the Keys citation alone does.

## R3 — methods-audit · 裁决:restricted-use

### 允许/禁止的用法

MAY SAY:
- That an independent Danish team, using complete national registers and a design that excludes prevalent users and avoids immortal time, found metformin monotherapy initiators had HIGHER all-cause mortality than matched people without diabetes (IRR 1.52, 95% CI 1.37-1.68 unadjusted; 1.32, 1.16-1.50 adjusted), the opposite direction from Bannister et al. 2014.
- That the finding is reproduced across two analytic designs within the same study (matched-cohort HR 1.48 unadjusted, 1.33 adjusted).
- That the Danish result is what one would expect given that Type 2 diabetes costs roughly six years of life expectancy — and that this makes Bannister's "survival advantage" the anomaly requiring explanation, not the Danish result.
- That Keys' own Discussion observes that three of four crude mortality cells match closely across the two countries (metformin-matched controls 16.9 Denmark vs 15.2 UK; sulfonylurea-matched controls 28.4 vs 28.7; sulfonylurea initiators 49.0 vs 50.9) while the metformin-treated cell does not (24.9 vs 14.4) — PROVIDED it is attributed to Keys' Discussion, described as crude unstandardized rates, and framed as localizing where the disagreement sits rather than proving why.
- That the study was funded by the Velux Foundation with no industry involvement and no declared conflicts.
- That the twin analysis points the same way, IF stated as directional support only and accompanied by its size (105 exposed cases vs 67 exposed controls; ~976 discordant pairs).

MAY NOT SAY:
- That Keys shows metformin increases mortality, shortens life, is harmful, or lacks a geroprotective effect. The contrast confounds the drug with the disease and identifies no drug effect in either direction. The authors explicitly cannot rule out a smaller protective effect.
- That Keys "settles," "refutes," "debunks," or "answers" the metformin-longevity question. It refutes one specific anomalous comparison, not the hypothesis.
- That the twin design controls for genetics or that it independently confirms the result. No zygosity-stratified analysis is reported, ~60% of pairs are dizygotic, and both adjusted twin estimates barely exclude the null.
- Any twin-arm number without its N attached, and no citation of the cumulative-use dose-response as an established finding (it is exploratory, unadjusted for multiplicity, and the twin cohort was explicitly too small for it).
- Any mechanism for why the UK metformin cell is low (censoring at intensification, death-ascertainment gaps, model extrapolation) — none of these was verifiable at source.
- That Keys' 2025 Ageing Res Rev review corroborates the 2022 study. Same group, same argument; it is one source, not two.
- That Shadyab/Kim 2025 (WHI, HR 0.70) supports or contradicts Keys or Bannister. It is an active-comparator study (metformin vs sulfonylurea) testing a different contrast entirely.

REQUIRED HEDGE if the number appears at all: the article must state, in the same passage, that comparing people with treated diabetes to people without diabetes cannot isolate a metformin effect — that this is the flaw in Bannister's design, and that Keys inherits it. The Danish study's value is as a demonstration that the founding observation does not reproduce, not as a measurement of what metformin does.

### 论证

The number itself is real and the study is, on the specific biases that wreck this literature, better built than the study it contradicts: complete national registers rather than primary-care records, prevalent users excluded back to the registry's first year, controls given the exposed person's index date so there is no immortal time, intention-to-treat so no censoring at intensification, and two designs (nested case-control IRR 1.52 and matched cohort HR 1.48) that converge. The singleton limb has the events to support the estimate. Funding is non-industry and the team is genuinely independent of Bannister. So I cannot veto it as an artifact of power, immortal time, or sponsorship.

What I can do is refuse to let it mean what the article wants it to mean. Three limits bind.

First, the twin limb is thin. The headline "discordant twin pairs" figure rests on 105 exposed cases versus 67 exposed controls; the matched-cohort twin limb has roughly 126 deaths across 976 pairs; both adjusted estimates barely clear the null; and there is no zygosity-stratified analysis despite 60% of pairs being dizygotic, so the genetic-control framing is not earned. On top of that the twin arm carries two selection problems that both push the same way as the result: 25.8% of twin deaths are dropped because the co-twin had already died, and co-twin controls die at 10.08 per 1000 py against 16.86 for singleton controls, a 40% gap the paper never explains and which the intact-pair requirement plausibly manufactures. The twin arm corroborates; it does not stand alone.

Second, and decisively: the contrast is confounded by diabetes itself. Metformin initiators differ from their controls by 45.6% vs 15.4% on lipid-lowering drugs and 63.5% vs 31.1% on antihypertensives, and the adjustment set is prescription proxies plus education and marital status with no BMI, smoking, HbA1c or renal function. Given that Type 2 diabetes costs roughly six years of life expectancy, an IRR of 1.3 to 1.5 is approximately the expected value of having treated diabetes. Keys did not discover that metformin is harmful. Keys found the boring, expected answer, which is precisely what makes Bannister's answer anomalous. The authors say so themselves when they concede they cannot rule out a smaller protective effect. If the article treats Keys as the correct answer to Bannister's question rather than as evidence that the question is malformed, it repeats the original error with the sign flipped.

Third, the forensic cell comparison. I verified the sentence word for word, and the numbers in the brief are right. But these are crude unstandardized rates across different calendar windows and follow-up durations, with no CIs on the UK side, no heterogeneity test, and the comparison drawn by one of the two studies' own authors. It localizes the disagreement; it does not explain it and does not establish that the UK metformin cell is the erroneous one. I could not verify any candidate mechanism because both the Bannister full text and the Keys supplement (where all nine sensitivity analyses, including the attempted replication of Bannister's never-exposed control definition, actually live) are paywalled.

Restricted-use rather than single-source-verified, because the risk here is not that the number is wrong but that it will be asked to carry a causal claim it cannot carry.

### 发现

- PROVENANCE VERIFIED. Keys MT, Thinggaard M, Larsen LA, Pedersen DA, Hallas J, Christensen K, Int J Epidemiol 2022;51(6):1886-1898, DOI 10.1093/ije/dyac200, PMID 36287641. Paywalled at Oxford Academic; not in PMC, no open-access PDF (Unpaywall/Semantic Scholar both return none). I reached the publisher full text and verified the abstract, Tables 1-4 values and the key Discussion quotes, but COULD NOT read the supplementary material (Sections S2-S3), where all nine sensitivity analyses live. Every sensitivity-analysis result below is therefore the authors' own main-text assertion, unverified at source. Disclose this if the article leans on any sensitivity result.

- HEADLINE N AND POWER (singletons) - SURVIVES. Source cohort 216,916 (5% random sample of the Danish population, alive and antidiabetic-naive on 1 Jan 1996). 7,842 metformin monotherapy initiators, matched 1:1 to non-diabetic controls on birth year and sex. Nested case-control drew from 44,629 deaths in the singleton cohort. Back-calculating from the published crude rates and CIs (24.93, 23.23-26.64; 16.86, 15.46-18.27) gives roughly 825 deaths among metformin initiators and roughly 553 among controls over ~33,000 person-years each. That is comfortably powered for the reported IRR 1.52. Two independent designs converge: nested case-control IRR 1.52 (1.37-1.68) unadjusted, 1.32 (1.16-1.50) adjusted; matched cohort HR 1.48 (1.32-1.64) unadjusted, 1.33 (1.16-1.54) adjusted. The singleton limb is not an artifact of small numbers.

- TWIN LIMB IS THE WEAK LIMB - do not let the article lean on it. The 'discordant twin pairs' headline IRR 1.90 (1.35-2.67) rests on 105 metformin-exposed cases (2.46%) versus 67 metformin-exposed controls (1.57%) among 4,264 case-control sets - i.e. ~172 exposed individuals carry the entire estimate. The matched-cohort twin limb has 976 discordant pairs and, back-calculated from the rate CIs (21.68, 17.09-26.26 and 10.08, 6.96-13.21), roughly 126 deaths total. Adjusted twin estimates barely clear the null: IRR 1.64 (1.10-2.46), HR 1.80 (1.11-2.91). The authors concede the twin cohort was 'insufficient to conduct subgroup analyses of cumulative metformin use.' The twin arm is powered only for a large effect; it corroborates direction, it does not independently establish it.

- TWIN DESIGN DOES NOT DELIVER THE GENETIC CONTROL IT IMPLIES. Zygosity is reported in Table 1 (31.1% MZ, 60.1% DZ, 8.7% unspecified) but NO zygosity-stratified analysis is presented. With ~60% dizygotic pairs, the 'matching on unobservables relating to shared early-life environment and genetic risk factors' the authors claim is, in the majority of pairs, sibling-level not genotype-level control. The article may not say the twin result 'controls for genetics.'

- INFORMATIVE SELECTION IN THE TWIN CASE-CONTROL, DIRECTIONALLY ALIGNED WITH THE FINDING. Of 5,744 twin deaths, only 4,264 (74.2%) could be used - 1,480 (25.8%) were dropped 'due to their co-twin having previously died.' Only the first death within a pair can serve as a case. Because the diabetic twin is more likely to die first, the retained case series is enriched for exposed and the surviving-co-twin control series is enriched for unexposed. This is inherent to the design rather than an error, but it means the twin IRR is closer to a within-pair 'who dies first' statistic than to a population rate ratio.

- UNEXPLAINED CONTROL-GROUP ANOMALY. Co-twin control mortality is 10.08 per 1000 py versus 16.86 for singleton controls - roughly 40% lower - and the paper offers no explanation (they only note that metformin initiators are similar across the two samples once age-standardized). Requiring an intact same-sex pair with both members alive and resident at index selects a healthier-than-average control set. An artificially healthy comparator inflates the IRR in exactly the reported direction. This is an unresolved threat to the twin estimate specifically.

- INFORMATIVE CENSORING OF CONTROLS. Non-diabetic controls were censored at incident diabetes. This removes exactly the person-time in which a control's health is deteriorating, depresses the comparator rate, and biases the IRR upward - again in the direction of the reported result. Defensible as a design choice (it preserves a clean 'no diabetes' comparator) but it is a directional bias, not a neutral one, and the article should not present the Danish IRR as a conservative estimate.

- IMMORTAL TIME AND PREVALENT USER: CLEAN. Prevalent users (any antidiabetic prescription before 1996) were excluded; cohort entry 1 Jan 1996, one year after the Danish National Prescription Registry began. Controls are assigned the exposed person's index date and followed from that shared date, so there is no immortal time in the exposed arm. Main analysis is intention-to-treat (initiators stay classified as metformin after regimen change), which avoids the informative-censoring-on-intensification trap. On the specific biases that plague this literature, Keys is the cleaner design.

- RESIDUAL CONFOUNDING IS ENORMOUS AND ONLY CRUDELY ADDRESSED. Baseline imbalance between metformin initiators and matched controls: lipid-lowering 45.6% vs 15.4%, antihypertensive 63.5% vs 31.1%, antiplatelet 30.6% vs 14.7%. Adjustment used only prescription proxies for morbidity (lipid-lowering, antiplatelet, antihypertensive, pulmonary, psychiatric, dementia) plus education and marital status. No BMI, no smoking, no HbA1c, no renal function, no diabetes duration or severity. The adjusted 1.32 is therefore not a de-confounded anything.

- THE CONTRAST IS UNINTERPRETABLE AS A DRUG EFFECT IN EITHER DIRECTION. This is the decisive audit point. 'Metformin-treated T2D versus no diabetes' confounds the drug with the disease. The authors themselves anchor the expected value: Type 2 diabetes is associated with a reduction in life expectancy of about 6 years, and they write that a 'survival advantage or equalization in a population presenting with substantial co-morbidity... would be unlikely.' An IRR of 1.3-1.5 is roughly what having treated diabetes costs; it is the expected result, not a discovery about metformin. Keys did not find that metformin shortens life - they found that Bannister's anomaly does not reproduce. Keys' own limitation sentence is explicit: the study 'is unable to rule out a protective effect of smaller magnitude that may also extend to ageing cohorts without Type 2 diabetes.' The article must not treat Keys as the correct answer to Bannister's question; the honest framing is that the question itself is malformed.

- MULTIPLICITY AND PRE-SPECIFICATION. No protocol registration, no pre-specification statement, no STROBE declaration found in the paper. The analysis space is large: 2 designs x 2 populations (singleton/twin) x unadjusted/adjusted x cumulative-use strata x 9 sensitivity analyses x a sulfonylurea comparison. No multiplicity correction. For the singleton headline the effect is far too large and too consistent across designs for this to matter. It DOES matter for the secondary claims - the cumulative-use dose-response ('increased mortality across all levels of cumulative use') and the twin subgroup results are exploratory and should be labelled as such.

- GENERALIZATION LIMITS. Median age at index 61 (singletons) / 60 (twins). Mean follow-up 4.20 years (singletons, SD 3.47) and 4.07 years (twins); median 3.27 and 3.31 years. This is a four-year mortality study in middle-aged people with incident treated diabetes, in Denmark, 1996-2012. It carries no information about metformin in healthy non-diabetic adults, about lifespan or maximum lifespan, or about long-duration use.

- CONFLICTS AND INDEPENDENCE. Funded by a Velux Foundation grant (Velux 31205) to K. Christensen at the Danish Ageing Research Center; no industry funding, no declared conflicts, no product supplier, no patent holder. Independence from Bannister et al. is genuine: different investigators, different country, different data system (complete national registers vs UK CPRD primary-care records). IMPORTANT CAVEAT for the article's source-counting: Keys MT, Hallas J, Miller RA, Suissa S, Christensen K, 'Emerging uncertainty on the anti-aging potential of metformin,' Ageing Res Rev 2025 (PMID 40582648) is the SAME group extending the same argument. It is a continuation, not corroboration, and must not be counted as a second independent source. The group also has a declared intellectual position, which is a prior, not a disqualifying conflict.

- FORENSIC CROSS-COUNTRY OBSERVATION: QUOTE VERIFIED, INFERENCE RESTRICTED. The Discussion reads, verbatim: 'Interestingly, crude mortality rates per 1000 person-years for the matched cohorts without diabetes were almost identical (metformin matched cohort without diabetes, 16.9 vs 15.2; sulfonylurea matched cohort without diabetes, 28.4 vs 28.7), as well as for sulfonylurea monotherapy initiators (49.0 vs 50.9). Differences in crude mortality rates were only observed in those initiating metformin monotherapy (24.9 in our study vs 14.4 in Bannister et al.).' The numbers in the article's brief are accurate. But every one of these is a CRUDE, unstandardized rate; no confidence intervals are attached to the UK figures; the two studies span different calendar windows and different mean follow-up; no formal test of heterogeneity is performed; and the comparison is made by the authors of one of the two studies, not by a third party. It is a descriptive localization, not a demonstration.

- WHAT THE FORENSIC POINT CANNOT DO. It shows WHERE the disagreement sits; it cannot show WHY, and it cannot adjudicate which country's metformin cell is wrong. Candidate explanations for the low UK metformin rate (14.4) - differential censoring at treatment intensification in the CPRD design, incomplete death ascertainment in primary-care records versus Denmark's complete Civil Registration System, or Bannister's use of a parametric survival-time-ratio model that extrapolates median survival well beyond a mean follow-up of roughly 2.8 years (503,384 person-years, 7,498 deaths, ~180,900 people) - are plausible but I could NOT verify any of them against the Bannister full text, which is also paywalled. Do not let the article assert a mechanism for the discrepancy.

- CONTRAST BOOKKEEPING (flagged for the article, since conflation is the failure mode here). Bannister 2014 and Keys 2022 test contrast A: metformin initiators vs matched people WITHOUT diabetes - confounded by diabetes itself, uninterpretable as a drug effect. Shadyab/Kim et al. 2025 (J Gerontol A, PMID 40388602, Women's Health Initiative target trial emulation, HR 0.70 for death before age 90) tests contrast B: metformin vs sulfonylurea - an active-comparator design that is interpretable as a relative drug effect but says nothing about metformin versus not having diabetes, and is confounded by sulfonylurea's own established harm signal. These are different questions with different biases. The article must never present Shadyab as replicating or rebutting either Bannister or Keys.

WHAT THE ARTICLE MAY SAY. (1) It may state the trial-level result as fact, with the correct scope: in a post-hoc analysis of banked CALERIE-2 specimens (N=197 of 220 randomised), ten epigenetic clocks were null at both 12 and 24 months and only DunedinPACE moved (12-month ITT d = -0.29, 95% CI -0.45 to -0.13; 24-month d = -0.25, -0.41 to -0.09). The authors' own words for the analysis are 'a post hoc analysis'; use them. (2) It may state -- and this is the strongest sourced claim in this seat -- that as of July 2026 NO independent team using an independent randomized caloric-restriction or fasting trial has ever measured DunedinPACE as an outcome. The finding is unreplicated, and the two nearest independent diet-RCT analyses (DIRECT PLUS, n=256, 18 months; MACRO, n=144, 12 months) found no between-arm difference in any clock including DunedinPACE. (3) It may state that the trial's own data have been publicly available via the Aging Research Biobank since the 2025 CALERIE Genomic Data Resource release, and that no independent re-derivation of the eleven-measure result has been published. (4) It may state that DunedinPACE is not a discriminating signal: across 41 human interventional studies it was the most-tested next-generation clock and responded in 36% of them, essentially tied with GrimAge (38.1%) and PhenoAge (33.3%); a 32-week semaglutide RCT moved it 9% versus CALERIE's 2-3% over two years. (5) It may quote the authors' own hedge verbatim -- 'effect-size estimates imply close to 90% overlap of DunedinPACE trajectories between the two groups' -- which sits in the same paragraph as the smoking-cessation line. (6) It may note that COSMOS (n=958, prespecified, independent) produced the mirror-image pattern, PC clocks moving and DunedinPACE not, which undercuts the paper's post-hoc explanation that DunedinPACE is categorically 'more sensitive' than the clocks. WHAT THE ARTICLE MAY NOT SAY. (A) It may NOT carry the mortality implication forward in any form -- not as the authors' claim, not as a hedged 'the authors suggest,' not as a 'could correspond to.' The chain requires that a treatment-induced CHANGE in DunedinPACE carries mortality information. HUNT measured exactly that in an independent cohort and got a null (change-in-DunedinPACE HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.63-1.55) while change in GrimAge2 was significant in the same table. The translation's two supporting citations are the authors' own DunedinPACE development paper and a same-network dementia paper. And the underlying per-SD hazard ratio varies from 1.23 (Finland) to 1.99 (HUNT2) to null (HUNT3) across cohorts. If the article mentions the smoking-cessation comparison at all, it must appear only as an example of what the paper's Discussion asserted and the independent evidence does not support -- never as a quantity. (B) It may NOT call the result a multiple-comparisons artifact or say multiplicity was ignored. The paper pre-committed to alpha = 0.005 citing Benjamin et al. 2018; that is approximately Bonferroni for eleven tests (0.0045), and both timepoints clear it. The sound criticism is non-prespecification of the measure, not a loose threshold. (C) It may NOT present DO-HEALTH as independent corroboration -- Belsky and Ryan are co-authors. (D) It may NOT present the Fudan organ-age reanalysis (Clin Nutr 2026) as replication of the DunedinPACE finding: independent team, but the SAME CALERIE dataset and a different class of measure. (E) It may NOT present the Speakman graded-CR mouse result as human replication. (F) It may NOT assert that critics have challenged the paper: I found no letter, comment, Matters Arising or published re-analysis among its 180 citing works. The honest statement is that the result has been widely cited, never replicated, and never formally challenged.

### 论证

The finding actually contains two claims with different evidentiary standing, and the seat's job is to keep them apart. CLAIM 1, the trial-level effect (randomized CR moved DunedinPACE and nothing else): I could not find a single independent measurement in either direction. Every search angle -- CR trials, fasting trials, TRE trials, CALERIE-specific queries, ClinicalTrials.gov -- returned nulls. That alone would be single-source-verified. What sharpens it is that independence fails for the apparent corroborators in exactly the way the brief warned about: DO-HEALTH shares Belsky and Ryan; every other CALERIE clock paper is the same consortium; and the surrogate itself was built by the senior author, then applied to CALERIE in September 2021, four months before the measure was published. The two genuinely independent diet RCTs that did test DunedinPACE (DIRECT PLUS, MACRO) found nothing between arms. CLAIM 2, the mortality translation, is what forces the ruling. This is the piece the article most wants and the piece that is most exposed. The translation requires a specific quantity -- does a change in DunedinPACE predict death? -- and an independent Norwegian team measured that exact quantity with repeated methylation eleven years apart and got a null across all three model specifications, while change in GrimAge2 in the same table was significantly associated. The same cohort's baseline association flipped from significant to null between waves. The per-SD hazard ratio the arithmetic rests on ranges from 1.23 to 1.99 across cohorts. And the paper's own two citations for it are its own measure-development paper and a same-network dementia study. So there is an independent measurement pointing the other way on the load-bearing extrapolation. That is direction-contested, not merely single-source. Two further things changed my read. First, the discriminator argument: I initially expected to show DunedinPACE moves far more readily than other clocks, which would make one-of-eleven look like noise-mining. The systematic review shows something subtler and more useful -- 36% responsiveness, statistically tied with GrimAge and PhenoAge. So DunedinPACE is not uniquely twitchy; it is one of three roughly equally promiscuous markers, and COSMOS produced the exact opposite dissociation with a larger, prespecified design. That kills the paper's post-hoc taxonomy defense more cleanly than a 'it moves for everything' claim would have. Second, I have to correct the brief's framing on multiplicity in the paper's favor. The full text shows a pre-committed alpha of 0.005, which is essentially Bonferroni for eleven tests, and both timepoints clear it. If the article attacks this result as an uncorrected fishing expedition, it will be wrong on a checkable fact and will hand the paper's defenders an easy rebuttal. The real vulnerabilities are non-prespecification, the self-described post-hoc design, the six-to-eleven measure expansion between preprint and publication, an unvalidated surrogate, and a mortality extrapolation that independent data contradict. Those are enough. One caveat on my own confidence: my citation enumeration ran through Europe PMC, whose citation graph is less complete than Scopus or Web of Science, so a critique published in a low-indexed venue could have been missed. The absence of a critique is the weakest of my findings and I have flagged it as such in permittedUse -- I state it as 'none found,' not 'none exists.'

- DUNEDINPACE MOVES EASILY AND IS NOT A DISCRIMINATING SIGNAL. Johnson AA & Sinclair DA, Front Genet 2026 (PMID 42294499, PMC13258529), systematic review of 41 human interventional studies: DunedinPACE is the most-tested next-generation clock (25 interventions) and responded in 36% of them -- statistically indistinguishable from GrimAge (38.1%) and DNAm PhenoAge (33.3%). Tallying their intervention table, DunedinPACE moved down 7 times, up 2 times, and did not move 16 times. Interventions that moved it include a 12-week antiretroviral (emtricitabine-tenofovir alafenamide), pitavastatin, semaglutide, omega-3, ketamine and a plant-rich diet. The same review warns, verbatim: 'a change in an epigenetic aging clock in response to an intervention is not necessarily indicative of responsiveness or an improved clinical outcome. It could, for example, be a result of technical and/or biological noise.'

- CORROBORATING META-EVIDENCE THAT THE MARKER CLASS IS THE MOST PROMISCUOUS ONE. Sehgal R et al. (Yale + TruDiagnostic), TranslAGE-Response, bioRxiv 10.1101/2024.10.22.619522, 51 longitudinal interventional studies, 16 clocks: 'clocks trained to predict mortality or pace of aging have the strongest response across all interventions.' COSMOS (Li S, Sesso HD et al., Nat Med 2026, PMID 41803341): n=958, 2-year randomized, PRESPECIFIED ancillary study, five measures (PCHannum, PCHorvath, PCPhenoAge, PCGrimAge, DunedinPACE). ## R4 — methods-audit · 裁决:restricted-use

### 允许/禁止的用法

WHAT THE ARTICLE MAY SAY.

1. It may state the result with these exact attributions: in a post-hoc analysis of banked CALERIE-2 specimens (N=197 of 220 randomized), eleven DNA-methylation measures of aging were tested; ten epigenetic clocks showed no treatment effect at either 12 or 24 months, and DunedinPACE alone moved (12-month ITT Cohen's d = -0.29, 95% CI -0.45 to -0.13, p = 4.83E-04; 24-month d = -0.25, 95% CI -0.41 to -0.09, p = 0.002). Source: Waziry et al., Nature Aging 2023;3:248-257, Table S4.

2. It may state that the 12-month effect survives Bonferroni correction across all 22 ITT tests and across all 44 ITT+TOT tests reported, even though the paper performed no multiplicity correction and uses only a blanket P<0.005 threshold. It MUST NOT present the finding as a multiple-comparisons artifact — that claim is false and I will not let it stand.

3. It may state that the 24-month effect (p=0.002) does not survive Bonferroni across the full set of reported tests and should not be treated as independent confirmation.

4. It may state that this analysis was not pre-registered: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00427193 registers core body temperature and resting metabolic rate as primary outcomes and TNF-alpha as the sole secondary; no methylation or epigenetic outcome is registered. It may state that the three-measure "primary analysis" set (PC PhenoAge, PC GrimAge, DunedinPACE) was designated within the post-hoc paper itself.

5. It MUST disclose, wherever the result is given weight, that two authors including the senior author are listed inventors on DunedinPACE, licensed to a commercial entity, per the paper's own competing-interests statement.

6. It may state that DunedinPACE covaries strongly with cardiometabolic and adiposity measures — citing the paper's own Figure S4A (baseline associations with waist circumference, insulin, HOMA-IR, CRP, HDL, triglycerides, blood pressure, metabolic syndrome score) and the independent FDR-corrected MACRO trial — and that the trial never tested whether change in weight or body composition accounts for the DunedinPACE movement. It may therefore state that a cardiometabolic-improvement explanation has not been excluded.

7. It may state the achieved dose as 11.9% (s.e.m. 0.7) against a prescribed 25%, citing the Waziry paper's own text. If it wants to cite the 2016 erratum or the ~19.5%/~9.1% front-loading split, another seat must verify those documents — I did not reach them.

8. It may report the dose-response and TOT figures (>10% CR: d=-0.33; 20% CR IV estimate: d=-0.43 at 12 months) ONLY with the label that these are non-randomized comparisons or instrumental-variable estimates resting on untestable assumptions, and that 20% CR was above the 75th percentile of achieved dose.

WHAT THE ARTICLE MAY NOT SAY.

A. VETOED: the mortality translation. The article may NOT state, in its own voice or as an unqualified report, that the CALERIE effect "corresponds to a 10-15% reduction in mortality risk" or that it is "similar in magnitude to smoking cessation." If the sentence appears at all it must appear as a quoted claim BY THE AUTHORS, immediately accompanied by: (i) that it extrapolates an observational level-mortality gradient onto an intervention-induced change, which no evidence licenses; (ii) that the DunedinPACE developers, same senior author, wrote in eLife 2022 that establishing DunedinPACE as a surrogate endpoint "will require evidence that it is modifiable by intervention and that intervention-induced changes result in changes in healthy-lifespan phenotypes" — a condition CALERIE does not satisfy; (iii) that the underlying hazard ratios (Framingham Offspring 1.65/SD, Normative Aging Study 1.26/SD) span roughly 7-15% for this shift and come from older cohorts, while CALERIE enrolled healthy adults aged 21-50; (iv) that Anthonisen 2005 counted actual deaths in 5,887 people over 14.5 years (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.02-1.37), a hard endpoint not comparable to a two-year surrogate in 197 people. No mortality number derived from this finding may appear in a headline, subhead, pull-quote, abstract, or summary.

B. The article may NOT say caloric restriction "slows aging" or "makes people biologically younger" on the strength of this result. It may say the trial reduced a methylation-based pace-of-aging index whose construct validity as an aging measure, as distinct from a cardiometabolic-state measure, is unsettled.

C. The article may NOT present "ten of eleven measures were null" as evidence that the DunedinPACE result is a fluke or was cherry-picked. ### 论证

I could not break the 12-month result on statistical grounds, and I tried. The two attacks the article seems to want to lead with both fail on inspection. First, "one significant out of eleven tested" implies undisclosed multiplicity, but I computed the corrections myself: at 22 ITT tests (alpha 0.00227) and even at all 44 ITT+TOT tests (alpha 0.00114), the 12-month p of 4.83E-04 survives. The authors did not perform a correction and the words for it appear nowhere in the paper, but their blanket 0.005 threshold happens to land near a Bonferroni for this family. That is luck, not method, and the 24-month p of 0.002 does not survive the widest family — so the correct statement is that the 12-month effect is multiplicity-robust and the 24-month effect is fragile, not that the whole thing is a multiple-comparisons artifact.

Second, and more important, the "ten clocks were null" framing is a power error waiting to happen. Using the supplement's own scaling SDs, a 2.6% slower pace accrues about nine days of biological age over twelve months, against clock measurement SDs of 2.76 to 5.04 years. The implied clock effect is d ≈ 0.009 against a minimum detectable effect of d ≈ 0.21. The trial is roughly twenty-fold underpowered to see on a cumulative clock what DunedinPACE reports. The nulls are therefore consistent with the DunedinPACE result, not evidence against it. The pace-versus-level taxonomy is a real distinction with a quantitative consequence, and I have to say so even though it helps the paper — a hostile review that gets the arithmetic wrong is worthless. Where the taxonomy IS convenient rather than principled is the fact that if pace-versus-level were genuinely load-bearing a priori, DunedinPACE would have been named as the outcome in advance. It was not.

What does not survive is everything downstream of the measurement. The registration is decisive: NCT00427193 registers core body temperature and resting metabolic rate as primary outcomes and TNF-alpha as the lone secondary. Nothing epigenetic is registered at all. The three-measure "primary analysis" set is stamped on the supplementary tables but was chosen after the data existed. So this is a genuinely unplanned analysis with an author-designated winner, and two of those authors — including the senior author — invented and commercially license the winning instrument. That does not make the number wrong; it makes the framing non-negotiable.

The construct problem is the one I would put highest, and the evidence for it is in the authors' own supplement. Figure S4A shows DunedinPACE correlating at baseline with waist circumference, insulin, HOMA-IR, CRP, HDL, triglycerides, blood pressure and metabolic syndrome score — precisely the panel an 11.9% caloric restriction with sustained weight loss moves. Its training criterion includes cardiorespiratory fitness and HDL. The MACRO trial, FDR-corrected and independent, shows DunedinPACE tracking cardiometabolic markers where the PC clocks do not, and a randomized semaglutide trial moves it 9%. Meanwhile the paper adjusts only for baseline BMI stratum and never asks whether change in weight or adiposity accounts for the DunedinPACE movement. The mediation analysis it did run points the opposite way and came back null for almost every outcome. So the honest reading is that CALERIE moved a composite that is heavily loaded on cardiometabolic and adiposity state, in participants who lost substantial weight. Whether that constitutes slowed aging is the open question, not the finding.

On the mortality translation I am ruling against the authors flatly, and the strongest argument is theirs. The eLife paper that introduced DunedinPACE — same senior author, two years earlier — states that establishing it as a surrogate endpoint will require evidence that intervention-induced changes produce changes in healthy-lifespan phenotypes. The 2023 Discussion asserts a mortality magnitude that its own instrument's introduction paper said had first to be demonstrated. Add that the 10-15% figure takes the top of a 7-15% range spanning Framingham Offspring (HR 1.65/SD) and the Normative Aging Study (HR 1.26/SD), both older cohorts, applied to healthy people aged 21-50; and that the smoking-cessation benchmark is a 5,887-person trial counting actual deaths over 14.5 years (HR 1.18) being set beside a two-year, 197-person modeled surrogate. That sentence is a modeled extrapolation wearing the clothes of a measured result, and the article must not repeat it in its own voice.

Hence restricted-use rather than VETO on the finding as a whole, with a targeted veto on the mortality sentence. The randomized 12-month effect on DunedinPACE is real, correctly analyzed, multiplicity-robust, cell-count-insensitive and publicly checkable via the Duke biorepository and the authors' GitHub. It just cannot carry a mortality claim, and it cannot be called "slowed aging" without the adiposity caveat attached.

### 论证

I looked for an independent team measuring the same quantity and found that none exists, which is the decisive result here. The quantity is percent change in mouse median lifespan under astaxanthin, meclizine or mitoglitazone. Every mammalian survival number for all three agents comes from one program: the NIA ITP, one mouse stock, one protocol executed at its own three sites, with overlapping author lists across the C2019, C2021 and C2022 reports. The ITP's own C2019 paper states its astaxanthin arm was the first mammalian lifespan evaluation of the compound, and I could not find a single non-ITP mouse survival study of any of the three, before or since. The ITP is also the only multi-site mouse lifespan program in existence, so the absence is structural rather than accidental. Every candidate corroborator I tested broke on independence or on relevance. The astaxanthin invertebrate positives are a different organism and are the reason the compound was nominated in the first place, and two analyses (Bene & Salmon 2023; the npj Aging DrugAge appraisal 2025) quantify how poorly invertebrate lifespan predicts mouse lifespan. The rosiglitazone +11% result is genuinely independent of the ITP but is a PPAR-gamma agonist, whereas mitoglitazone was deliberately engineered to lose PPAR-gamma activity, and the study is n=34 vs 31 at one site in one sex. The Gehan-test reanalysis shares both the ITP dataset and an ITP site PI as co-author. So the finding is single-source in both directions: the hit was single-source and so is the null. That alone would put this at single-source-verified, but two further problems push it to restricted-use, because the article's intended claim is stronger than the evidence and would mislead if stated as drafted. First, power. The ITP's design point is 80% power for a 10% change in mean lifespan with 44 males and 36 females per treatment group per site; independent protocol guidance asks roughly 176 males per group for the same target. The originals were 8 to 12 percent in median. Meclizine's 8 percent is below the program's own detection point, so a non-significant re-test is an expected outcome even under a fully real effect, and the Gehan-test paper independently shows that ITP null verdicts flip under a different survival test. Second, only mitoglitazone was re-run at its original dose and start age, and its reported outcome is same-direction and similar-magnitude but non-significant, which is what an underpowered true effect looks like rather than a contradiction. The other two were given at different doses or later start ages, so no direct replication was attempted at all, and the C2019 achieved doses were only 46 and 68 percent of target, meaning the original dose is not a fixed quantity that a different-dose null could refute. The ITP's dose-sensitivity framing is therefore not a dodge; it is largely accurate. What does survive, and survives strongly, is a reframed version of the article's point. The ITP's own prior dose-robustness record was 5 of 6 (rapamycin, acarbose, 17-alpha-estradiol, NDGA, canagliflozin replicated; only aspirin failed, and only at higher doses) — established from a verbatim sentence in the primary source, Strong et al. Aging Cell 2022. Three new failures take that to roughly 5 or 6 of 9, which is a real and citable deterioration. The JAX female exclusion is post-hoc on the abstract's own wording, has no precedent I could find in earlier ITP reports, and lands in a literature that disagrees with itself about whether ITP female lifespan varies by site (Cheng 2019 says no, the 2025 protocol reanalysis says yes) — though both of those reached me only through a summarizer and need verbatim checking. Independent work by Pabis and colleagues explains why long-lived controls can manufacture apparent harm, which partly vindicates the reanalysis while simultaneously exposing the original +12% to the same artefact. And the strongest of the three hits carries a commercial-supplier conflict on the original report, not on the null. Finally, I could not obtain the C2022 full text and the cohort's raw data is not yet in the public Mouse Phenome Database, so neither I nor anyone else outside the program can currently verify the per-arm numbers the finding cites. The honest thing the article can say is narrower and sharper than what it planned: the field's best evidence sits barely above its own detection threshold, is almost never re-run under identical conditions, and has never once been checked by an outside laboratory.

Searches for a Japanese/Chinese/European equivalent, a private replication consortium, or a mouse arm of Rejuvenome/Astera capable of retesting these agents returned nothing. - APPARENT CORROBORATOR #1 FAILS — WRONG ORGANISM. Astaxanthin has independent positive lifespan data in C. elegans (Food & Function 2025, doi 10.1039/d4fo03490b; and PMC9687064) and in Drosophila SOD/catalase mutants fed Haematococcus pluvialis. These are the results that MOTIVATED the ITP nomination; they cannot corroborate or refute a mouse median-lifespan number. Independent quantification of exactly this gap: Bene & Salmon, GeroScience 2023 — across ITP compounds, worm and fly results had essentially no predictive power for mouse lifespan; and the independent DrugAge appraisal (Nature npj Aging 2025, s41514-025-00287-0; 667 studies) reports only about one third of non-mammal findings translating to mammals.

- APPARENT CORROBORATOR #2 FAILS — WRONG TARGET, AND IT POINTS THE OTHER WAY. Xu et al., Aging Cell 2020;19(11):e13267 (PMC7681041, NIDDK intramural) is genuinely independent of the ITP and found rosiglitazone extended median lifespan 11% (777→864 d, p<0.05) in male C57BL/6J started at 14 months — but n=34 treated vs 31 control, one site, one sex, and they explicitly did NOT run a pioglitazone or mitoglitazone lifespan arm. It is a PPARγ agonist, whereas mitoglitazone (MSDC-0160) was deliberately medicinal-chemistry-engineered to LOSE PPARγ binding and act on the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier / mTOT. So it cannot corroborate mitoglitazone. It does, however, constitute a small independent positive for the TZD class that points opposite to C2022's pioglitazone null-plus-female-harm.

- APPARENT CORROBORATOR #3 FAILS — SAME DATA, SHARED AUTHOR. Jiang, Gelfond, Liu, Strong & Nelson, GeroScience 2024;46(5):4533-4541 reanalysed the ITP dataset with the Gehan test and found five compounds the log-rank test had scored as null (metformin, enalapril, 17-DMAG, CAPE, green tea extract) were in fact significant. Randy Strong is an ITP site PI and a co-author of the C2022 report, and the data are the ITP's own — not independent on either axis. Its substantive implication cuts AGAINST the article: ITP 'not significant' verdicts are demonstrably test-dependent.

- BASE RATE, FROM THE PRIMARY SOURCE, VERBATIM. Strong et al., Aging Cell 2022;21(12):e13724 (PMC9741502): "Five of the agents that increase lifespan (NDGA, aspirin, rapamycin, 17⟨E2, and acarbose) have been re‐examined in later cohorts with different dosages and treatment durations. Of these, only aspirin did not replicate, although it was only tested at higher doses." (the mangled character is 17-alpha-E2). Add canagliflozin: the C2016 male hit was re-tested with a 16-month late start and replicated (+14%, p=0.004 in males; GeroScience 2024, doi 10.1007/s11357-024-01176-2 — note this paper carries a 2025 correction, PMID 39004654, whose content I could not retrieve). NDGA specifically replicated at three doses — 800 ppm +8% p=0.04, 2500 ppm +10% p=0.0053, 5000 ppm +9% p=0.0048 (PMC3954939). So PRE-C2022 the ITP's own dose/schedule-robustness record was 5 of 6, with aspirin (higher dose only) the single documented self-non-replication. C2022 adds three, taking it to roughly 5-6 of 9. That is the defensible number; 'does not consistently reproduce its own hits' is a fair gloss on 5-6/9 but NOT on the C2022 paper alone.

- POWER: THE ARMS ARE AT OR BELOW THEIR OWN DETECTION THRESHOLD. ITP design (NIA/MPD protocol; eBioMedicine 2016 description): 44 males + 36 females per treatment group PER SITE (~132 M / ~108 F per arm pooled), controls doubled to 88 M + 72 F per site; the stated design point is 80% power to detect a 10% change in MEAN lifespan pooling as few as two sites (also stated verbatim in PMC3954939). Independent protocol guidance (A Standardized Protocol for Mouse Longevity Studies, Aging & Disease 2025, PMC13061545) puts >=176 males per group for 10% at 80% power in a two-group design, and 71/group only for a 15% effect. The originals were +8% to +12% MEDIAN. So meclizine's 8% sits BELOW the design's own 80%-power point, and a true 9-12% effect leaves a single re-test non-significant an appreciable fraction of the time. 'No significant effect' in these arms is weak evidence of absence.

- TWO OF THE THREE ARE NOT REPLICATION ATTEMPTS AT ALL. Per the C2022 abstract (verbatim, PubMed 41843349), the three were "administered at different doses or starting at later ages" — different intervention, so no direct replication was attempted for astaxanthin or meclizine. Worse for astaxanthin: the C2019 paper reports the achieved dose was 46% ± 13 of the 4000 ppm target (~1840 ± 520 ppm), and meclizine 68% ± 6 of the 800 ppm target (~544 ± 48 ppm) — the 'original dose' is itself only loosely defined, so a different-dose null cannot refute it. Mitoglitazone is the only agent whose original protocol (300 ppm from 7 months — confirmed independently in the Mouse Phenome Database C2021 listing) was repeated, and the reported outcome is same-direction and similar-magnitude but non-significant, which is precisely what an underpowered true effect looks like.

'Prompting' concedes the sequence: result first, exclusion second. I found no precedent in prior ITP reports for dropping a site. ## R5 — methods-audit · 裁决:restricted-use

### 允许/禁止的用法

THE ARTICLE MAY SAY, sourced to Korstanje et al., GeroScience 2026;48(3):3821-3830 (PMID 41843349): (1) That in ITP Cohort 2022, eleven intervention arms built from eight agents/combinations produced no statistically significant lifespan increase in either sex on the program's primary endpoint (site-stratified log-rank, nominal unadjusted p). (2) The specific male numbers, provided they are labelled as non-significant point estimates: Asta_low +3.69% (p=0.214), Asta_late +2.21% (p=0.377), M160_late +7.75% (p=0.176), Mec_high +9.23% (p=0.396), Mec_late -1.66% (p=0.557); male control n=300, treated arms n=102-210. (3) That pioglitazone significantly REDUCED female lifespan (-9.41% median, p<0.0001 pooled; -6.2%, p=0.0047 excluding The Jackson Laboratory) — this is the only result in the paper that survives correction for the ~40 nominal tests reported. (4) That the ITP itself, in print, raises chance as an explanation for its own earlier hits, quoting the Discussion: the prior gains of 9%, 8% and 12% "were at or near the lower level of what is expected to be able to discover at 80% power, and none of them showed a change in the Wang-Allison statistic. Furthermore, the p-values are nominal, and chance effects can be expected in any series of studies for truly ineffective agents." (5) That none of the three earlier hits survives multiplicity correction within its own original cohort (astaxanthin p=0.003 and meclizine p=0.03 among ~28 nominal tests in C2019; mitoglitazone p=0.015 among ~24 in C2021), and none showed a 90th-percentile (Wang-Allison) effect. (6) That in C2021 itself, forskolin (+8.3% male median, p=0.17) and mitoglitazone (+8.7%, p=0.015) delivered near-identical median gains against the same control group but landed on opposite sides of p=0.05 — the single cleanest demonstration that ~8-9% median gains are boundary calls in this design.

THE ARTICLE MAY NOT SAY: (a) That the ITP "re-tested" its own hits and they "failed to replicate" or "did not reproduce" — NOT ONE of the three agents was re-tested at its original dose-and-start-age combination. Astaxanthin was given at roughly half the originally effective delivered dose (880 ppm vs ~1,840 ppm achieved in C2019); meclizine at ~3.2x (1,727 ppm) or a fraction of it, and four months later (16 vs 12 mo); mitoglitazone eleven months later (18 vs 7 mo) at 126 ppm achieved against a 300 ppm target. The ITP's "different doses or later starting ages" framing is not spin; it is literally what Table 3 shows, and it defeats the replication reading. (b) That mitoglitazone was "re-tested at its original dose (300 ppm)" — 300 ppm was the target; 126 ppm was achieved; and the start age changed. (c) That these agents "do not extend lifespan" or that the null is "evidence of absence." The paper reports no hazard ratio, no confidence interval and no power calculation. My derived 95% CIs from its own numbers do not exclude the original effects (M160_late HR~0.87, CI ~0.72-1.06 vs original ~0.78; Mec_high HR~0.92, CI ~0.75-1.11 vs original ~0.80). Per-arm power to detect the original point estimates was only ~60-90%, and ~24% for a plausible shrunk true effect of ~5%. (d) That this is an independent replication or an independent check — same program, same authors, same three sites, same breeding scheme; independence is zero. (e) That astaxanthin shortened female lifespan: p=0.021 does not survive multiplicity and the effect disappears once the anomalous JAX site is removed. (f) That the JAX site was "excluded to rescue the result" — the exclusion was post-hoc but was cross-validated against the C2023 cohort, all six control Z-scores were reported, and it moved the harm findings toward the null while making female benefit EASIER to detect. It cannot have manufactured the headline. (g) It may not cite "68 ppm" for Mec_late as an established fact; that figure is unexplained, plausibly a percent/ppm transcription error, and unresolvable from the published record.

REQUIRED HEDGE IF THE LOAD-BEARING CLAIM IS KEPT: the article's intended thesis — "even the field's gold-standard program does not consistently reproduce its own hits" — must be rewritten as the claim this evidence actually supports: that the ITP's positive findings in the 8-12% median range sit at or below its own design's 80%-power detection floor, are reported without multiplicity correction, are not corroborated by the program's maximum-lifespan statistic, and do not persist when dose or start age is shifted — with the ITP itself now saying in print that chance may explain them. Any sentence asserting non-replication must carry, in the same sentence or the next, the fact that the doses and/or start ages differed and that the arms were underpowered for the original effect sizes.

### 论证

Not a VETO: the document is real, primary, reached in full, NIA-funded across three independent sites with data publicly deposited at the Mouse Phenome Database, no competing interests declared, and no commercial incentive toward a null. Its own stated conclusion — that no arm in Cohort 2022 significantly increased lifespan — is accurately reported and usable. But it will not carry the weight the article wants to put on it, for two independent and individually sufficient reasons.

First, the replication premise is factually false. I reached Table 3 twice and cross-checked it against both original reports. The C2019 astaxanthin hit was delivered at ~1,840 ppm achieved (46% of a 4,000 ppm target) from 12 months; C2022 gave 880 ppm from 11 and 16 months. The C2019 meclizine hit was ~544 ppm achieved from 12 months; C2022 gave 1,727 ppm from 16 months and a second arm whose achieved dose is printed as 68 ppm. The C2021 mitoglitazone hit started at 7 months; C2022 started at 18 months at 126 ppm against a 300 ppm target. Zero arms match on dose and age together. The ITP's own Discussion describes each arm in exactly these terms and never claims a replication. So the paper contains no replication attempt to have failed, and the ITP's counter-framing is not resistance to the article's claim — it is the accurate description of the design.

Second, even granting a replication reading, the design cannot support an absence claim. The program's stated power is 80% for a 10% lifespan gain; two of the three original effects (+8%, +9%) fall below that floor. The paper reports no power analysis, no hazard ratios and no confidence intervals — a structural deficiency for a null-result paper. Inverting the reported log-rank p-values against the reported N's, the minimum detectable hazard ratio at 80% power is ~0.73-0.78 across these arms, while the original effects correspond to HR ~0.75-0.80; per-arm power to detect them was ~60-90%, and only ~24% for the ~5% effect one would expect after shrinking estimates that were selected at p=0.003-0.03 with no multiplicity control. Decisively, the derived confidence intervals from the C2022 data contain the original effect estimates. "Not significant" here means "not resolvable," not "not there."

Multiplicity cuts symmetrically and is the most defensible thing the article can take from this paper. C2022 reports ~40 nominal unadjusted tests, of which only pioglitazone's female harm survives correction. But none of the three original hits survives correction within its own cohort either, and none showed a 90th-percentile effect. The strongest single datum for the article's underlying point is not in C2022 at all: in C2021, forskolin at +8.3% (p=0.17) and mitoglitazone at +8.7% (p=0.015) produced near-identical median gains against the same controls yet were classified as a miss and a hit. That is an internal, dose-independent demonstration that hits in this range are noise-dominated boundary calls, and it survives every objection I raised above.

On the JAX anomaly, the hostile reading does not hold. The exclusion was post-hoc and outcome-triggered, which I flag, but the authors reported all six control Z-scores rather than only the extreme one, validated the anomaly against an out-of-sample C2023 cohort, and checked husbandry confounders. More importantly the bias runs the wrong way for an accusation of manufacture: unusually long-lived control females bias the pooled analysis against benefit and toward harm, so removing that site should have made female benefit easier to see, and none appeared. The exclusion weakened the paper's harm findings and left the headline null intact. The male nulls are untouched, since control males at all three sites were within historical range.

Residual gaps I could not close and have flagged rather than papered over: the Mec_late achieved dose is unexplained and plausibly a percent/ppm transcription error (the C2019 paper reports meclizine recovery as exactly "68%"), and the C2021 mitoglitazone achieved dose sits in unretrievable supplementary tables, so dose comparability for that arm is unverified in both directions. Neither gap changes the ruling, because the start-age mismatch alone defeats the replication reading for mitoglitazone and meclizine.

Hence restricted-use: the cohort-level null, the specific arm statistics, the pioglitazone female harm, the ITP's own printed chance concession, and the multiplicity fragility of the original hits are all usable. Any sentence framing this as the ITP failing to reproduce its own hits must be rewritten, and must carry the dose/start-age mismatch and the underpowering in the immediate vicinity.

DECISIVE: my derived 95% CIs from the C2022 data DO NOT EXCLUDE the original effects — M160_late HR~0.87 (95% CI ~0.72-1.06) contains the original mitoglitazone HR~0.78; Mec_high HR~0.92 (95% CI ~0.75-1.11) contains the original meclizine HR~0.80; even the best-powered arm, Asta_low HR~0.89 (95% CI ~0.75-1.07), only barely reaches the original astaxanthin HR~0.75. The C2022 results are statistically COMPATIBLE with the original effect sizes.

- MULTIPLICITY IS UNCONTROLLED IN BOTH DIRECTIONS, AND THE ORIGINAL HITS DO NOT SURVIVE IT EITHER. C2022 Methods verbatim: 'P-values presented are nominal, i.e., they are not adjusted for multiple comparisons, and readers should evaluate the evidence for our conclusions with this in mind.' C2022 ran 11 male + 9 female arms x 2 tests (log-rank + Wang-Allison Fisher exact) = ~40 nominal tests; Bonferroni alpha ~0.00125. The ONLY result in the entire paper that survives correction is pioglitazone reducing FEMALE lifespan (-9.41% median, p<0.0001 pooled; -6.2%, p=0.0047 after excluding TJL). Astaxanthin female harm (p=0.021) does not survive and vanishes entirely on TJL exclusion. The nominally significant male HARM signal (Mec_late Wang-Allison p=0.009, p90 -3.07%) also does not survive. Symmetrically, none of the three original 'hits' survives correction within its own cohort: C2019 tested 7 agents (~28 tests, Bonferroni alpha ~0.0018) — astaxanthin p=0.003 fails, meclizine p=0.03 fails; C2021 tested 6 agents (~24 tests, alpha ~0.002) — mitoglitazone p=0.015 fails. And none of the three showed a Wang-Allison (90th-percentile) effect in its original report.

- THE CLEANEST DEMONSTRATION OF BOUNDARY FRAGILITY IS INSIDE THE ORIGINAL COHORT, NOT IN C2022. C2021 Table 1 (males, control n=298): forskolin +8.3% median, log-rank p=0.17, WA p=0.32 — reported as a MISS; mitoglitazone +8.7% median, log-rank p=0.015, WA p=0.19 — reported as a HIT. Two essentially identical median gains in the same cohort against the same controls, one crossing p=0.05 and one not. This is a far better-anchored illustration of the article's point (that ~8-9% median gains are noise-dominated boundary calls in this design) than the C2022 non-replication framing, and it does not depend on any dose/start-age argument.

- MEDIAN %CHANGE AND THE LOG-RANK TEST ARE COMPUTED ON DIFFERENT MOUSE SETS, SO CROSS-PAPER %-MEDIAN COMPARISONS ARE UNSOUND. Methods: mice removed for fighting or unrelated reasons (chip failure, water leakage, escape) were treated as alive at removal and lost to follow-up; these were '4.1% of males and 0.7% of females, included in Kaplan-Meier survival statistics but excluded from median or 90th percentile calculations.' So the headline descriptive statistic (%change in median) and the inferential statistic (stratified log-rank) do not describe the same denominator. This partly explains the striking dissociations the article wants to lean on: Mec_high shows the largest male median gain in the cohort (+9.23%) at p=0.396, and after TJL exclusion M160_late females show a median change of only -0.70% yet log-rank p=0.0374. A large median shift with a large p-value means the survival curves cross, not that there is a suppressed positive trend. Per-arm censoring/removal rates are NOT reported, so differential attrition by arm cannot be excluded — a reporting gap, though at 4.1% male removal it is unlikely to generate a 9% median shift.

- JAX CONTROL-FEMALE ANOMALY AND THE SITE EXCLUSION: POST-HOC, BUT WELL-DIAGNOSED, AND CONSERVATIVE IN THE DIRECTION THAT MATTERS. The anomaly was detected during analysis, not pre-specified. Verbatim: 'TJL control females in C2022 were 3.13 standard deviations (i.e. Z = 3.13, two-tailed p = 0.0017) above the mean survival median in the C2004 - C2021 cohorts.' The authors then state: 'We therefore conducted two analyses of drug effects on female mice, one in which all three sites were considered, and one in which only UM and UT data were included.' Mitigations that hold up: (a) the comparison cells were all reported, not just the extreme one — 'Z scores for females were -1.5 for UM and -0.4 for UT, and Z scores for control males at TJL, UM, and UT were respectively 0.25, -0.49, and 0.9 (all non-significant)'; correcting Z=3.13 for selection across those 6 control cells still leaves p~0.01; (b) it was independently cross-checked against an out-of-sample cohort — the C2023 TJL control females 'are far enough along to see that their median lifespan is in line with previous cohorts (data not shown)'; (c) confounders were checked (same room, water, bedding, diet, overlapping cohorts). CRITICAL DIRECTIONAL POINT FOR THE ARTICLE: an anomalously LONG-LIVED control group biases the pooled female analysis AGAINST detecting benefit and TOWARD detecting harm. Removing TJL therefore made the harm signals WEAKER (astaxanthin female harm disappeared; M160_late female median change went from -5.76% to -0.70%) while making benefit EASIER to detect — and no female benefit appeared under either analysis. So the female null survives the site question, and the exclusion decision cannot be accused of manufacturing the headline. The exclusion is only a problem for the HARM claims, which is where the article must be careful. The male nulls are untouched by this issue: control males at all three sites were within normal range (Z = 0.25, -0.49, 0.9).

- REPORTING DEFICIENCIES relevant to weighting this source: no power calculation or minimum detectable effect is stated; no hazard ratios; no confidence intervals on any estimate; sidedness of the log-rank test is not stated in C2022 (the C2021 companion specifies 'a two-sided 5% significance level', so two-sided is the reasonable assumption); per-site and per-arm N are not given (only pooled: male control n=300, treated arms n=102-210; female control n=272, treated arms n=130-132); the large target-vs-achieved dose shortfalls (AKG 20,000 -> 3,262; mitoglitazone 300 -> 126; meclizine 800 -> 68; atorvastatin/telmisartan 120/30 -> 48/27) are tabulated but never discussed or explained. A paper whose entire headline is a null and which reports no confidence interval on any effect cannot, on its own, support an assertion of no effect. I also could not verify the C2021 mitoglitazone achieved dose from the accessible primary record (the C2021 dose/recovery data sit in unretrievable Supplementary Tables S2/S3), so the claim that C2022's M160_late was dose-comparable to C2021 is UNVERIFIED in either direction.

- TWO ARMS WERE MALE-ONLY (Asta_late and Mec_late appear in the male half of Tables 1 and 3 but not the female half), with no stated rationale. Asta_late is also the smallest arm in the study (n=102 males), giving it the weakest power in the cohort (minimum detectable HR ~0.73). The article should not present '11 arms, both sexes' as if all arms were run in both sexes.

### 论证

The core structural claim survives independent challenge and is genuinely upgraded. Bruellman et al. (Epigenetics 2026) is a clean independent replication — different team, different cohort (CATSLife1), different funder, no author overlap, its own multiplicity correction — and it reproduces not just the direction but the specific shape of the Apsley result: oral tissue reads much older than blood across seven clocks (+3.77 to +19.72 y, all p<0.001), while PBMC vs buffy coat is indistinguishable from zero for every clock. That second half matters more than the first, because it is the part that could most easily have been an artifact of one lab's assay or one cohort's handling, and it held. A third independent line (Zarandooz & Raffington, Max Planck, public GEO data) shows only moderate saliva-blood ICCs for second/third-generation clocks and as low as ~0.19-0.25 for first-generation acceleration measures, consistent with poor transfer. And the GrimAge2 authors' own 2022 paper concedes a systematic saliva offset, reporting a mean GrimAge2 of 61.6 y in NGHS saliva from mothers aged 36-43 — about +20 y, essentially the same number Apsley got for saliva vs DBS. What did NOT replicate is the headline magnitude. Nobody independent has measured buccal-vs-blood GrimAge2 at all; the independent oral-vs-blood figures for the GrimAge family are ~9 y (PCGrimAge) and the largest independently reproduced single-clock gap is PhenoAge at ~19.7 y. So 35.78 y is a single-source number sitting on a multi-source phenomenon, and the article should carry the phenomenon rather than the number. I also found one real direction contest, confined to DunedinPACE, which was tissue-robust in the independent cohort. Independence audit turned up the field's usual fake-replication pattern: three of the most citable-looking corroborators — the Innovations in Aging abstract, the Epigenomics review, and the widely syndicated Conversation explainer — are all Apsley and Shalev restating the same sample. Anyone assembling citations quickly would mistake that for four sources. On the consumer-vendor question, the article's belief holds up: a targeted PubMed query returned zero records, and neither the authors' own reviews nor the ELSI literature cite such a study. But the grey literature that fills the gap is not usable as a substitute — the 13.1-year spread most often quoted mixes epigenetic, glycan and blood-panel modalities and comes from an affiliate site. Finally, the bridge from this finding to consumer tests is weaker than the finding itself. CheekAge is trained on >8,000 buccal samples and Elysium's Index is saliva-based, so the tissue-mismatch mechanism cannot be assumed for named vendors. The one clean documented case of a blood-trained clock deployed on oral tissue commercially is FOXO's 2021 UCLA license of GrimAge/PhenoAge for saliva-based life-insurance underwriting — which is underwriting, not a consumer kit. I rule upgrade-multisource on the phenomenon, with the magnitude and the consumer inference fenced off in permitted use.

(A) is now multi-source. (B) is still single-source. They must not be conflated in the article.

Different discipline, different labs, different assays (SNaPshot/pyrosequencing/NGS rather than EPIC arrays), same direction. This supports the 'predictable, not mysterious' reading: oral tissue is a mixture of buccal epithelium and leukocytes in individually variable proportions.

- A NON-CLOCK COUNTERWEIGHT WORTH KNOWING. Marmiroli G, Candelora F, Fiano V, Richiardi L, Popovic M, 'Comparison of DNA methylation measured in saliva and peripheral blood as a marker of exposure and outcome: a scoping review', Clinical Epigenetics 2026, PMID 41957674. The instability is in the CLOCK ALGORITHM's tissue transfer, not in DNA methylation measurement per se.

- INDEPENDENT SUPPORT FOR THE ARTICLE'S WIDER 'UNSTABLE MEASUREMENT LAYER' THESIS, from sources unconnected to Apsley: technical noise between replicates of the same blood sample reaching up to ~9 y for prominent clocks, fixed to within ~1.5 y by principal-component retraining (Higgins-Chen et al. 2022, PMC9586209); array-vs-sequencing platform replicate differences of ~1.8–6.2 y depending on clock (PMC12741009); and a 2025 preprint (bioRxiv 2025.10.13.682176) finding technical reliability does not predict biological stability. These are better load-bearing citations for 'the measurement layer is unstable' than the cross-tissue number is, because they do not depend on deliberately using a clock outside its training tissue.

## R6 — methods-audit · 裁决:restricted-use

### 允许/禁止的用法

MAY SAY:

1. That a peer-reviewed within-person study (Apsley et al., Aging Cell 2025, 83 individuals, 284 samples across five tissues) found that epigenetic clocks give wildly different answers depending on which tissue you sample — with oral-vs-blood gaps reaching tens of years for second-generation clocks, GrimAge2 differing by roughly 36 years between cheek cells and dried blood spots from the same person.
2. That this is specifically a failure of transportability, not a general tissue effect, and the article MUST say so in the same breath: within blood, the same clocks agree closely (GrimAge2 dried blood spot vs. buffy coat, -1.11 +/- 0.81 years, non-significant — and non-significant for all seven clocks tested). The gap appears when a blood-trained clock is read off oral tissue.
3. That the same pattern appears on a rate measure, not just year-scale clocks: DunedinPACE differs by 0.65 buccal-vs-blood (a 65% difference in apparent pace of aging) while being flat between blood fractions.
4. That absolute "biological age" in years is the least portable output of these assays — dependent on tissue, on array version, and on which clock is run — and that the direction of this finding is independently corroborated (a separate Max Planck analysis of 107 saliva/blood pairs found first-generation cross-tissue ICCs of just 0.19-0.25).
5. That no published study splits one person's sample across multiple commercial biological-age vendors and reports the disagreement. This gap may be stated affirmatively as a gap.
6. That the study's provenance is clean: NIH-funded, no declared conflicts, no vendor involvement.

MAY NOT SAY:

1. Not "35.78 years" or "35.78 +/- 0.68." Write "roughly 36 years" or "more than three decades." The paper never reports how many pairs are in that cell (at most 64, likely nearer 55), so the decimals are unearned, and every value was computed on EPIC v2 — an array on which none of these clocks was built and where GrimAge is independently documented to degrade badly (r < 0.6) and Hannum to return negative ages.
2. Not "+/- 0.68" as a margin of error on an individual's result. It is a standard error on a group mean; the implied person-to-person SD is about 5 years.
3. Not "this shows biological-age measurement is unstable or noisy." The opposite: a ~36-year offset with a ~0.7-year standard error is a highly CONSISTENT systematic bias. The article may call it a systematic, calibratable bias — a clock read outside its training tissue — and may not convert it into a claim about random error, test-retest noise, or instability. For the instability thesis the article needs a within-tissue reliability source, which this is not.
4. Not "consumer vendors sampling different tissues will therefore report biological ages ~36 years apart," and no numeric version of that claim. Tally Health's TallyAge is built on CheekAge, trained on 8,045 buccal samples; Elysium's Index is a saliva assay on the principal-component method. The vendors that use oral tissue do not apply blood-trained clocks to it. The most the article may say is: results from vendors using different tissues are not on a common scale and are not interchangeable; a number from a cheek swab and a number from a blood draw are not comparable quantities, and no published study has measured how far apart real vendor outputs land.
5. Not "the authors warn against consumer epigenetic testing," or any implication that the paper is an indictment of the industry. The authors argue the reverse — that DTC companies "may prefer to use oral-based tissues" and that this "could expand the applications of epigenetic clocks." Cite them as calling for tissue-specific calibration.
6. Not any use of Eric Verdin's Medscape anecdote (results "40 to 67") as evidence or as a study. If mentioned at all, label it explicitly as one researcher's uncontrolled self-report.
7. Not a claim that the magnitude generalizes to a general adult population. 36 of 83 subjects were children aged 9-14 drawn from a high-risk maltreatment cohort, pooled into the same paired t-tests as adults, for clocks the authors concede were trained only on adults; the adult sample was a university-community convenience sample (77 recruited, 47 analyzed).
8. If the article wants the "25 years apart on repeated processing of one sample" figure for its instability thesis, it may not use it on the strength of Elysium's marketing page. Verify it in Higgins-Chen/Levine, Nature Aging 2022 (PMID 36277076) first, and disclose that those authors have commercial ties to Elysium Health.

### 论证

The number is real and correctly transcribed; I verified it against Table 2 at PMC and it survives on provenance (NIH-funded, no conflicts, no vendor involvement), on internal coherence (seven clocks move together, and the DBS-vs-buffy-coat null holds for all seven), and on power (t ~ 52). It is also corroborated in DIRECTION by two independent teams: a Max Planck pooled analysis of 107 saliva/blood pairs (PMC12016411) finding first-generation cross-tissue ICCs of only 0.19-0.25, and a buffy-coat/PBMC/saliva preprint reporting higher DNAm ages in saliva than buffy coat. So this is not VETO territory.

But it cannot carry the weight the article wants, for three independent reasons.

First, the statistic argues against the thesis it is being recruited for. The +/- 0.68 is a standard error, implying a person-to-person SD near 5 years around a 35.8-year offset. A bias that consistent and that one-directional is the definition of a calibration constant — stable, predictable, and correctable by fitting the clock in the target tissue. The article wants to say the measurement layer is unstable; this number says the measurement layer is very stably wrong when you use it outside its training tissue. Those are different claims, and only the second is supported.

Second, the magnitude specifically is not trustworthy, even though the direction is. Three defects stack on the point estimate: the cell's N is never reported anywhere in the paper (it is at most 64 pairs and probably nearer 55, inferred from tissue availability); children aged 9-14 from a high-risk maltreatment cohort are pooled into the same paired t-test as adults, for a clock the authors themselves note was trained only on adults; and every value comes off EPIC v2, an array on which no clock in the table was built, with ~249 of ~2070 clock probes imputed from training-set means. Independent array work shows GrimAge correlation collapsing below 0.6 on EPIC v2 and Hannum returning negative ages, and shows that it is absolute epigenetic age — precisely what Table 2 reports — that degrades, while age acceleration survives. The within-person paired design does defend against a constant platform offset, which is why I do not veto; it does not defend against a tissue-dependent one, and substituting blood-training-set means for missing probes is exactly a tissue-dependent operation. The honest reading is "tens of years," not "35.78."

Third, and decisive for the consumer-test argument, the inference the article wants to draw is broken at the premise. The measured quantity is "blood-trained GrimAge2 applied to buccal DNA." The vendors that sample oral tissue do not do that: Tally Health's TallyAge comes from CheekAge, trained on 8,045 buccal samples aged 18-100; Elysium's Index is a saliva assay built on the principal-component reliability method. The discordance here is predictable rather than meaningful — it is what the paper was designed to demonstrate, and it is the error a competent vendor avoids by construction. The paper's authors say so themselves, arguing that DTC companies "may prefer to use oral-based tissues" and that this "could expand the applications of epigenetic clocks." Using their paper as an indictment of consumer testing reverses their conclusion.

I also searched hard for the head-to-head DTC vendor study and did not find one. The only artifacts are an N=1 anecdote in a Medscape interview (Verdin, results ranging 40 to 67) and affiliate blog comparisons. The article's belief that no such study exists holds, and it should say so as a documented gap rather than gesture at the anecdote.

Restricted-use rather than single-source-verified because the permitted claim is materially narrower than the claim the article set out to make, and the restriction is not optional: quoting 35.78 to the decimal, or attaching it to vendor comparability, would each be a defect a hostile reader could land.

### 发现

- NUMBER VERIFIED AT PRIMARY. Table 2 of Apsley et al., Aging Cell 2025 (PMC11984668, tbl-0002) reads, verbatim, GrimAge2 buccal vs. DBS = 35.78 +/- 0.68***; DBS vs. buffy coat = -1.11 +/- 0.81 (n.s.); DBS vs. PBMC = 5.16 +/- 0.56***. The crucial limit the orchestrator already identified is confirmed and is stronger than stated: DBS vs. buffy coat is non-significant for ALL SEVEN clocks (Horvath -0.27+/-0.53, Hannum 0.02+/-0.76, Skin&Blood -0.29+/-0.18, PedBE 0.04+/-0.07, PhenoAge -1.33+/-0.75, GrimAge2 -1.11+/-0.81, DunedinPACE -0.02+/-0.01). The tens-of-years effect is exclusively an oral-vs-blood phenomenon.

- N PER CELL IS NEVER REPORTED — the headline's denominator is undisclosed. The paper gives only 83 individuals / 284 samples and a coarse breakdown (N with 2 tissues = 8, 3 tissues = 32, 4 tissues = 43). Buccal was collected in 81 people (46 adults + 35 children) and DBS in 64 (35 + 29), so the buccal-vs-DBS cell is at MOST 64 pairs and plausibly nearer 55. No table footnote states it. Power is not the problem (35.78/0.68 gives t ~ 52); the problem is that a number quoted to two decimals has no stated N anywhere in the source.

- '+/- 0.68' IS STANDARD ERROR, NOT SD — and this inverts the article's intended meaning. Per the Table 2 caption the values are beta +/- SE. Back-solving with n ~ 60 gives an implied SD of roughly 5 years. So the finding is a 35.8-year offset that is remarkably CONSISTENT from person to person. That is the signature of a systematic, one-directional, calibratable bias, not of an unstable measurement. Citing +/- 0.68 as if it were the spread of individual results would be a factual error, and citing a tight-SE systematic offset as proof of 'instability' argues the opposite of what the statistic shows.

- ADULTS AND CHILDREN WERE POOLED IN THE SAME PAIRED t-TESTS, AND THE HEADLINE CLOCK IS NOT VALID IN CHILDREN. 36 of 83 subjects are aged 9-14; the authors' own fourth limitation states that 'specific algorithms such as the Hannum, PhenoAge, GrimAge2, and DunedinPACE clocks were trained using only data from adult samples.' The primary analyses combine the two age groups with no age covariate ('Because all comparisons were made within-person, sex, and race/ethnicity were not included as covariates'). Age-stratified versions exist only in unexamined supplementary tables S7/S8. The 35.78 figure is therefore a blend of adults and of children in whom GrimAge2 is out of training range on BOTH axes (age and tissue).

- NO MULTIPLICITY CORRECTION ACROSS ~59 COMPARISONS (7 clocks x 9 tissue pairs, minus 4 empty cells); the paper acknowledges no correction. This does NOT threaten the headline (t ~ 52 survives any correction). It is noted for completeness, and because the uncorrected-null direction is conservative, the DBS-vs-buffy-coat null is if anything easier to trust: -1.11 +/- 0.81 implies a 95% CI of roughly [-2.7, +0.5] years, which excludes anything remotely like a 30-year gap. The 'confined to oral-vs-blood' limit is well-bounded, not a bare failure to reject.

- BATCH IS PARTIALLY CONFOUNDED WITH TISSUE IN THE PRIMARY ANALYSIS. Four array plates were used (96/96/96/8) and an individual's tissues were not necessarily processed together. DNAm batch entered only SENSITIVITY analyses, not the Table 2 paired t-tests that produce 35.78. The sensitivity work is reassuring on direction (residualizing for cellular composition and batch moved within-person correlations by only an average of delta r = -0.04, and PC-clock versions left 'the direction of effects and significance levels comparable'), but the specific point estimate 35.78 is a raw, unadjusted difference.

- PLATFORM PROBLEM, INDEPENDENTLY DOCUMENTED: every value in Table 2 was produced on EPIC v2, and the authors concede 'no clocks investigated here were constructed using this array' and that 'imputations for missing probes were made over the entire sample.' Independent work (Clinical Epigenetics 2024, PMC11583407; N=30 whole-blood samples run on 450K, EPICv1 and EPICv2) found that on the SAME DNA, epigenetic-age correlation between EPICv1 and EPICv2 was only r = 0.71, that GrimAge fell to r < 0.6 on EPICv2 versus 0.88 for 450K-EPICv1, and that Hannum 'consistently estimated negative ages for EPICv2 samples' — Hannum being the clock Apsley reports at 26.18 years buccal-vs-DBS. Roughly 249 of the ~2070 clock CpGs are absent from EPICv2, imputed with training-set (blood) means, and the array literature reports that age ACCELERATION survives probe reduction while ABSOLUTE epigenetic age does not. Table 2 reports absolute-year differences, i.e. the least platform-robust quantity available.

- THE PAPER'S OWN FRAMING IS THE OPPOSITE OF THE ARTICLE'S INTENDED USE. Apsley et al. write that 'commercial companies which offer epigenetic clock estimates for direct-to-consumer and health-care uses may prefer to use oral-based tissues' and that doing so 'could expand the applications of epigenetic clocks to more use cases.' The paper is a call for tissue-specific calibration and an argument FOR oral sampling, not an indictment of consumer testing. Presenting it as the latter misrepresents the source's stated conclusion.

- VENDORS SAMPLING ORAL TISSUE DO NOT USE BLOOD-TRAINED CLOCKS, WHICH BREAKS THE INTENDED INFERENCE. Tally Health's TallyAge derives from CheekAge, trained on 8,045 buccal samples from adults aged 18-100 (GeroScience; mortality validation in Frontiers in Aging, PMC11473594). Elysium's Index is a saliva assay on a custom Illumina chip built on the principal-component/reliability method. Apsley's 35.78 measures what happens when blood-trained GrimAge2 is applied to buccal DNA — a thing the major oral-sample vendors specifically do not do. The number cannot be transposed into 'vendor A and vendor B will differ by ~35 years.'

- SELECTION LIMITS MAGNITUDE GENERALIZATION (though not internal validity, since the design is within-person). Adults were a Penn State community convenience sample, 77 recruited and 47 analyzed (~39% not included, reasons not enumerated in the text I could reach). Children came from the Child Health Study, an explicitly high-risk pediatric maltreatment cohort — the authors flag this as their third limitation. Overall 78% White, 60% female, mean age 30.25. Each subject is their own control, so the direction of the effect is protected; the magnitude should not be quoted as a population parameter.

- PROVENANCE IS CLEAN — this is the finding's strongest feature. Funding is NIH (U01ES030949, P50HD089922, UL1TR002014); conflicts of interest are declared as 'None'; no vendor supplied product, funding, or assay. There is no commercial incentive shaping the result in either direction.

- NULL RESULT ON THE VENDOR QUESTION (relevant to my ruling on consumer claims): I found no peer-reviewed study splitting one person's sample across multiple commercial DTC biological-age vendors and reporting the disagreement. The closest items are (a) an anecdote attributed to Buck Institute CEO Eric Verdin in a 2025 Medscape interview that his own results across commercial methylation clocks ranged from 40 to 67, and (b) affiliate-driven vendor-comparison blog posts. Neither is a study; the Verdin item is N=1, self-reported, non-peer-reviewed, with no stated protocol. The article must not cite either as evidence, and must not imply a head-to-head vendor study exists.

- A BETTER SOURCE EXISTS FOR THE ARTICLE'S ACTUAL THESIS. The claim 'the measurement layer under longevity claims is unstable' is about within-tissue technical noise, which Apsley does not measure. Two candidates do: the array study above (same DNA, EPICv1 vs EPICv2, r = 0.71) and the principal-component-clock paper (Higgins-Chen/Levine, Nature Aging 2022, PMID 36277076), which Elysium's own science page paraphrases as a single sample processed repeatedly yielding estimates 'up to 25 years apart.' CAUTION: I could not reach the Nature Aging primary (paywall redirect), so that 25-year figure is currently sourced only to a vendor marketing page describing its own licensed method, and the PC-clock authors have Elysium ties. It must be verified in the primary before use.

- SECONDARY OBSERVATION WORTH KEEPING: the oral-vs-blood problem is not confined to year-scale clocks. DunedinPACE, a rate measure where 1.0 is one year of biological aging per calendar year, differs by 0.65 +/- 0.01 buccal-vs-DBS — a 65% difference in apparent pace of aging — while DBS-vs-buffy-coat is -0.02 +/- 0.01 (n.s.). Same pattern, different scale, and it rules out 'this is just an artifact of anchoring clocks to a chronological-age intercept.'
