# L2 — Rapamycin / rapalogs: human and companion-animal evidence

Working notes. Compiled 2026-07-28. All numbers traced to primary sources (paper, registry, SEC filing, FDA label)
unless explicitly flagged as HYPE or SECONDARY.

Convention used throughout:
- **T1** = animal evidence
- **T2** = human surrogate/biomarker endpoint
- **T3** = human clinical-outcome endpoint
The recurring failure mode in this field is silent promotion T1 → T3.

---

## 0. Mechanism (one paragraph, primary citations)

- Heitman J, Movva NR, Hall MN. "Targets for cell cycle arrest by the immunosuppressant rapamycin in yeast."
  *Science* 1991;253(5022):905–909. Genetic screen in *S. cerevisiae* for rapamycin-resistant mutants identified
  FKBP12 as the direct binding partner and two novel genes TOR1/TOR2 as required for rapamycin toxicity.
  This is the origin of the name TOR.
- Rapamycin does not inhibit mTOR directly: the rapamycin–FKBP12 complex is the inhibitory species, and it
  inhibits mTORC1 acutely; mTORC2 inhibition requires chronic exposure and is cell-type dependent. This
  distinction is the entire pharmacological premise of the "intermittent dosing" hypothesis and of the
  next-generation rapalog companies (Tornado's TOR-101 is explicitly pitched as "free of rapamycin's
  longer-term effect on mTORC2").
- Why the field expected translation: mTORC1 is the nutrient/growth-factor sensor whose inhibition is the
  proximate mechanism of dietary-restriction-like longevity in yeast, worms, flies; and rapamycin is the only
  compound the NIA Interventions Testing Program has robustly and repeatedly shown to extend lifespan in
  genetically heterogeneous mice, including when started at 20 months of age (Harrison et al, *Nature* 2009;
  460:392–395 — L1 owns these numbers; one sentence of context only).

**Key structural point for the article:** the mouse evidence is unusually strong *and* the human
clinical-outcome evidence is essentially zero. That gap is the story.

## 1. PEARL trial — the headline "first long-term human longevity trial of rapamycin"

**Primary source:** Moel M, Harinath G, Lee V, Nyquist A, Morgan SL, Isman A, Zalzala S.
"Influence of rapamycin on safety and healthspan metrics after one year: PEARL trial results."
*Aging (Albany NY)* 2025;17(4):908–936. doi:10.18632/aging.206235. Published 2025-04-04.
Preprint: medRxiv 2024.08.21.24312372 (2024-08-22).
Registry: NCT04488601. Sponsor: **AgelessRx** (industry). Collaborator: UCLA.

### Design
- 48-week decentralised, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled.
- Arms: compounded rapamycin 5 mg/week (n=40), 10 mg/week (n=35), placebo (n=39) — **completers**.
- Ages 50–85, "relatively good health", well-managed chronic conditions permitted.
  Exclusions incl. premenopausal females, HbA1c >7%, impaired hepatic/renal function.
- Registry actual enrollment **129**. Paper: 114 completed + 11 discontinued = **125** accounted for.
  → 4-participant discrepancy between registry and paper unexplained.
- Analysis was of **completers only** (114), not intention-to-treat. Discontinuations: placebo 6, 10 mg 3, 5 mg 2.
- Study dates per registry: start 2020-01-01, primary completion 2023-12-30.
- **Results have NOT been posted to ClinicalTrials.gov** (hasResults = No) as of this writing.

### Primary endpoint — NOT MET
Registered primary outcome: "Changes in visceral fat as measured by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan."
Result, verbatim: **"Visceral adiposity did not change significantly (ηp2 = 0.001, p = 0.942)."**
ηp2 = 0.001 is not a near-miss; it is an effect size indistinguishable from zero.

### Secondary / exploratory findings claimed
- Lean tissue mass, women, 10 mg arm: 48 wk md = 6.194 (95% CI 0.8773–11.5105), p = 0.018;
  24 wk md = 3.60472 (95% CI 0.0913–7.1182), p = 0.043. Note the lower CI bounds nearly touch zero at both
  timepoints — these are fragile.
- Self-reported pain, women, 10 mg: md = 8.071 (95% CI 3.044–13.098), p < 0.001.
- General health (5 mg, both sexes) p = 0.004; emotional well-being (5 mg) p = 0.023.
- Bone outcomes: **no significant changes.** (NB: press coverage claiming "improves bone mass in men" is not
  supported by the published paper — see cross-caliber issues.)

### The caliber problem with those secondaries
- Women were **35.1% of the whole cohort and only 20% of the 10 mg arm**. The load-bearing positive finding
  therefore rests on roughly **7 women**. The paper does not report the exact per-cell n for this subgroup.
- Stated statistics method, verbatim: *"Data were analyzed using tests as described in the text, with relevant
  corrections for sphericity (Greenhouse-Geisser), homogeneity of variance (Welch's test), and multiple
  comparisons (Bonferroni or Games-Howell correction), unless otherwise noted."* — these are **within-model
  post-hoc corrections**, NOT a family-wise correction across the dozens of secondary outcomes × 2 doses ×
  2 sexes × 2 timepoints that were examined. There is no stated global multiplicity control and no
  pre-registration of the sex-stratified analyses.

### The exposure problem (this is the strongest single criticism, and it comes from the sponsor's own later paper)
- Rapamycin was **compounded**: *"Rapamycin used in this study was a compounded formulation of 5 mg or 10 mg,
  received from Belmar Pharma Solutions (Golden, CO, USA)."*
- **PEARL did not measure blood rapamycin levels.** The paper instead cites a separate later cohort finding that
  *"compounded rapamycin did indeed have approximately ⅓ the concentration in blood after 24 hrs relative to
  commercial"*.
- That later paper is Harinath G, Lee V, Nyquist A, et al. "The bioavailability and blood levels of low-dose
  rapamycin for longevity in real-world cohorts of normative aging individuals." *GeroScience* 2025;47(4):5681–5694.
  doi:10.1007/s11357-025-01532-w. Verbatim: *"compounded rapamycin (mean = 0.27 ng/mL per 1 mg) is 31.03% (3 ×)
  less bioavailable per milligram than commercial formulations (mean = 0.87 ng/mL per 1 mg)."*
  Compounded n=23 (5/10/15 mg), commercial n=44 (2/4/6/8 mg), 24 h post-dose; plus observational cohort n=316.
  Same AgelessRx author group. COI verbatim: *"GH, VL, AN, MM, BV, CT, SN, SLM, AI, and SZ are employees and
  shareholders of AgelessRx."*
- **Implication:** PEARL's top dose of 10 mg/week compounded corresponds to roughly 3 mg/week commercial-equivalent
  systemic exposure. So PEARL is not a clean test of "low-dose weekly rapamycin"; it may be a test of a
  substantially lower exposure than the off-label community actually takes (median 6 mg/week, mostly commercial).
  A null primary endpoint under those conditions is only weakly informative in either direction.

### Funding & COI, verbatim
*"Funding for the study came primarily from donors via a Lifespan.io crowdfunding effort, with significant
donations from Vitalik Buterin, Micah Zoltu, Brad Armstrong, and anonymous gifts. Administrative and trial
support, article publishing charges, and other fees were provided by AgelessRx."*
*"GH, VL, AN, MM, SM, AI, and SZ are employees and shareholders of AgelessRx."*
→ AgelessRx is a telehealth company that **sells off-label rapamycin** (~$65/month entry price per its own 2026
marketing). The sponsor of the trial is the vendor of the drug. Note also that the primary outcome, the analysis
plan flexibility, and the publication venue (*Aging*, where the journal also ran a promotional news item) all sit
inside a fairly closed loop.

### Limitations, authors' own words, verbatim
*"First, adherence to the once-weekly dosing schedule was based largely on self-report; missed doses or irregular
dosing could have impacted treatment effect. Second, our cohort demographics showed relatively few women and
predominantly health-conscious participants, which could mask larger effects in populations with higher baseline
adiposity or different lifestyle patterns. Third, only broad measures of diet and activity were captured in
self-reports, leaving these factors as a plausible source of unexplained variance in outcomes."*

### Abstract conclusion, verbatim
*"Low-dose, intermittent rapamycin administration over 48 weeks is relatively safe in healthy, normative-aging
adults, and was associated with significant improvements in lean tissue mass and pain in women."*
→ Note what is absent from the conclusion sentence: the primary endpoint.

### Sponsor's public framing (HYPE, documented)
AgelessRx's own explainer page (agelessrx.com/results-of-the-pearl-trial/) does not state that the primary
endpoint was missed. It writes: *"Though there were no statistically significant differences to visceral fat by
the end of the trial, those who took Rapamycin were more likely to lose visceral fat than those in the placebo
group."* and leads with *"The PEARL trial successfully showed that Rapamycin is a safe and well-tolerated drug
when taken at low doses."* It also calls rapamycin *"the gold standard for pharmacological interventions"* —
a claim true only of T1 mouse data, presented without that qualifier.

### Safety in PEARL
AEs and SAEs "similar across all groups." SAEs: 1 (10 mg), 2 (5 mg), 3 (placebo). GI symptoms more frequent on
drug (10 mg = 8, 5 mg = 7, placebo = 4). One anemia case (5 mg), resolved. This is a real but weak safety signal
set: n≈38/arm for 48 weeks cannot exclude anything with an incidence below a few percent.

## 2. Mannick lineage: Novartis → resTORbio → Life Biosciences → Tornado → Altos

This is the cleanest natural experiment in the whole longevity field: the same investigator took the same
hypothesis from a positive T2 biomarker study, through a positive-ish T3 phase 2, to an unambiguous
**phase 3 failure at n=1024**.

### 2a. 2014 — RAD001 and influenza vaccine response (T2, surrogate)
Mannick JB, Del Giudice G, Lattanzi M, Valiante NM, Praestgaard J, Huang B, Lonetto MA, Maecker HT, Kovarik J,
Carson S, Glass DJ, Klickstein LB. "mTOR inhibition improves immune function in the elderly."
*Sci Transl Med* 2014 Dec 24;6(268):268ra179.
- **218 volunteers ≥65 y**, no unstable medical conditions, 9 sites, New Zealand + Australia, Dec 2011–Apr 2012.
  211 completed; **199 in the intent-to-treat population**.
- Randomisation 5:2 drug:placebo per arm. Arms: RAD001 0.5 mg daily (N=53), 5 mg weekly (N=53),
  20 mg weekly (N=53), pooled placebo (N=59). **6 weeks** of drug, then 2-week washout, then 2012 seasonal
  influenza vaccine; HI titres at baseline and 4 weeks post-vaccination.
- **Primary endpoint, verbatim:** *"The study was powered to be able to demonstrate that in at least two of three
  influenza vaccine strains, there was a ≥1.2-fold GMT ratio increase relative to placebo and a posterior
  probability of at least 80% that the placebo-corrected GMT ratio exceeded 1.0."*
- **Result, verbatim:** *"In the intent-to-treat population, the low-dose RAD001 (0.5 mg daily or 5 mg weekly)
  cohorts, but not the higher-dose (20 mg weekly) cohort, met the primary endpoint of the study."*
  → 2 of 3 dose arms met it; the highest dose did not. Non-monotonic dose response.
- **The "20% improvement" everyone quotes is exactly this ≥1.2-fold GMT ratio** — i.e. a ~20% relative increase in
  geometric-mean antibody titre versus placebo, on a log-scale immunological surrogate, 4 weeks after one
  vaccination. It is not a 20% reduction in influenza, illness, hospitalisation, or anything a patient feels.
- **Seroconversion (a harder surrogate) did NOT reach significance in the ITT population.** Verbatim:
  *"In the intent-to-treat population, seroconversion rates for the H3N2 and B influenza strains were increased in
  the RAD001-treated cohorts as compared to the placebo cohorts, although the increases did not meet statistical
  significance (Table 2)."* Significance appeared only in the subgroup with baseline titres ≤1:40, and only for the
  B strain in the 0.5 mg daily arm.
- **Authors' own caliber warning, verbatim:** *"Finally, this was an exploratory proof-of-concept study that used a
  liberal preplanned threshold for significance (80% posterior probability of an effect over placebo)."*
  The analysis was Bayesian with noninformative priors; the bar was an 80% posterior probability, **not** p<0.05.
  Immunophenotyping used FDR with a q<0.20 threshold across 76 tests (PD-1+ CD4 week 6: P=0.03, **q=0.13**).
- **Adverse events (Table 1):** subjects with AEs 41.5% (0.5 mg daily), 37.7% (5 mg weekly), 50.9% (20 mg weekly)
  vs **20.3% placebo**. Mouth ulceration 11.3% / 3.8% / 17.0% vs 5.1% placebo (20/218 = 9.2% overall).
  Increased blood cholesterol 3.8% in all three drug arms vs 0% placebo. Neutropenia 5.7% and
  thrombocytopenia 3.8% in the 20 mg arm only.
  Authors' own note: *"significant numbers of subjects in our study developed mouth ulcers even when dosed as low
  as 0.5 mg RAD001 daily."*
- **COI:** authors affiliated to Novartis Institutes for BioMedical Research, Novartis Vaccines and Diagnostics,
  Novartis Pharmaceuticals; corresponding author email joan.mannick@novartis.com. RAD001 = everolimus, a Novartis
  product. Novartis-run, Novartis-funded, Novartis-authored.

### 2b. 2018 — phase 2a, RTB101 + everolimus (T3 but soft endpoint, self-reported infection)
Mannick JB, Morris M, Hockey HUP, et al. "TORC1 inhibition enhances immune function and reduces infections in the
elderly." *Sci Transl Med* 2018 Jul 11;10(449):eaaq1564. PMID 29997249.
- **264 elderly subjects**, 6 weeks of a low-dose combination of BEZ235 (=RTB101) + RAD001.
- Reported *"a significant (P = 0.001) decrease in the rate of infections reported by elderly subjects for a year
  after study drug initiation."* Also upregulated antiviral gene expression and improved influenza vaccine response.
- Caliber flag: the infection endpoint here was **self-reported infections over 12 months following a 6-week
  course**, i.e. reported illness, not laboratory-confirmed and not adjudicated. This softness is exactly what the
  FDA later objected to in the reverse direction (see 2c).

### 2c. 2021 — phase 2b and phase 3 published together (the decisive result)
Mannick JB, Teo G, Bernardo P, et al. "Targeting the biology of ageing with mTOR inhibitors to improve immune
function in older adults: phase 2b and phase 3 randomised trials." *Lancet Healthy Longev* 2021;2(5):e250–e262.
doi:10.1016/S2666-7568(21)00062-3. PMID 33977284. Open access CC BY-NC-ND.
Funding, verbatim: **"resTORbio and National Institute on Aging."**

**Phase 2b** — 652 participants, aged 65–85 **with elevated RTI risk** (asthma, T2D, COPD, CHF, current smokers,
or recent RTI hospitalisation), enrolled 2017-05-16 to 2018-01-10, 54 sites NZ/AU/USA.
- Part 1 (n=179): RTB101 5 mg qd (61), RTB101 10 mg qd (58), placebo (60).
- Part 2 (n=473): RTB101 10 mg qd (118), 10 mg bid (120), 10 mg + everolimus 0.1 mg qd (115), placebo (120).
- Primary outcome: **laboratory-confirmed RTIs over 16 weeks.**
- **The FIRST prespecified efficacy analysis of part 2 — RTB101 10 mg + everolimus — did NOT reach statistical
  significance.**
- The famous positive number came from a **second prespecified analysis that pooled parts 1 and 2**:
  RTB101 10 mg qd **34/176 (19%)** vs pooled placebo **50/180 (28%)**; **OR 0.601, 90% CI 0.391–0.922, p=0.02.**
  → Note the **90% CI**, not 95%. At 95% the interval would cross 1. This is a materially lower bar and is almost
  never mentioned when this result is cited.

**Phase 3** — 1024 patients, aged ≥65, **non-smokers without COPD** (i.e. lower baseline risk), enrolled
2018-05-07 to 2019-07-19. RTB101 10 mg qd n=513 vs placebo n=510.
- Primary outcome **changed** to *clinically symptomatic respiratory illness* — symptoms consistent with RTI
  regardless of laboratory confirmation. Per the accompanying commentary: *"The US Food and Drug Administration
  requested a change in primary endpoint between the phase 2b and phase 3 trials because of concerns that
  laboratory confirmation of an infection was not relevant to how patients feel and function."*
- **Result: 134/511 (26%) RTB101 vs 125/510 (25%) placebo; OR 1.07, 90% CI 0.80–1.42, p=0.65. FAILED.**
  The point estimate is on the *wrong side of 1*.
- Both trials confirmed the biomarker: interferon-induced antiviral genes significantly upregulated on drug.
  **The mechanism worked and the clinical endpoint did not.** This is the single most instructive fact in L2.
- Interpretation, verbatim: *"However, clinical trial endpoint and patient population refinement may be necessary
  to determine whether interferon response upregulation by mTOR inhibitors consistently reduces viral infection
  incidence or severity in older populations."*

**Contemporaneous company disclosure** (resTORbio press release, 2019-11-15, GlobeNewswire): PROTECTOR 1,
1024 patients randomised 1:1, *"the risk of having a CSRI was 0.46 in subjects treated with RTB101 versus 0.44 in
those on placebo"*, and *"While we are disappointed in these results, there are extensive preclinical data
supporting the potential therapeutic benefit of TORC1 inhibition in multiple aging-related diseases."*
Company stopped development in the indication. **Stock fell ~74% on the news** (BioSpace headline).

**Independent commentary:** Dzankic PJ, Partridge L. "Clinical trials of mTOR inhibitors to boost immunity to
viral infection in older adults." *Lancet Healthy Longev* 2021;2(5):e232–e233. doi:10.1016/S2666-7568(21)00090-8.
Verbatim: *"Compared with the phase 2b trial, the phase 3 trial was thus done in patients who were at lower
overall risk of RTIs and with a primary endpoint that was less clearly linked to underlying immune function."*
COI: *"LP reports a grant from European Research Council, outside the submitted work. PJ-D declares no competing
interests."* — i.e. genuinely independent.
→ Note the two competing explanations for the phase 2→3 collapse: (a) the population and endpoint changed
[the charitable reading, which the authors and commentators offer]; (b) the phase 2b result was a pooled,
90%-CI, second-analysis finding that was never that robust [the sceptical reading, which nobody wrote down but
which the numbers support]. A good article should present both.

### 2d. What happened to resTORbio, and to Mannick
- 2020-04-03: enrollment in cohort 5 of the RTB101 Parkinson's phase 1b/2a postponed (COVID-19 level 4 alert,
  New Zealand); the study was then terminated without enrolling cohort 5.
- 2020-04-28: resTORbio entered a **reverse-merger agreement with Adicet Bio** (SEC 8-K, CIK 1720580).
  resTORbio ceased to exist as an aging company; the shell became Adicet Bio, a **gamma-delta T-cell oncology**
  company. RTB101 was abandoned. **There is no rapalog anywhere in Adicet's pipeline today.**
- Mannick's subsequent path: Life Biosciences → co-founder & CEO of **Tornado Therapeutics** (a Cambrian Bio
  "PipeCo"), developing selective mTORC1 inhibitors **licensed from Novartis**, lead asset TOR-101.
- **2025-08-04: Altos Labs announced Mannick as SVP, Chief Medical Officer and Head of Product Development**
  (altoslabs.com press release). She left Tornado. Altos is a cellular-reprogramming company — not a rapalog
  company.
- **TOR-101 status as of 2026: still preclinical.** Mannick said in January 2025 they expected to take TOR-101
  into trials in 2025; no phase 1 initiation has been announced. Per Synapse/PatSnap drug-status tracking, global
  highest R&D status remains Preclinical.
  → So: the single most experienced rapalog clinical developer in the world has, as of 2026, moved to a company
  that does not develop rapalogs, and the rapalog she was developing has not entered humans.

## 3. Companion dogs

### 3a. Urfer et al 2017 — the small cardiac study everyone cites
Urfer SR, Kaeberlein TL, Mailheau S, Bergman PJ, Creevy KE, Promislow DEL, Kaeberlein M.
"A randomized controlled trial to establish effects of short-term rapamycin treatment in 24 middle-aged
companion dogs." *GeroScience* 2017;39(2):117–127. doi:10.1007/s11357-017-9972-z. PMID 28374166.
- **24 dogs** (analysed: placebo 8, low-dose 0.05 mg/kg 5 completers, high-dose 0.1 mg/kg 10 completers),
  **three times weekly for 10 weeks**. Dogs ≥6 y, ≥18 kg, mean age 9.7 ± 1.84 y.
- Echocardiography, pooled rapamycin vs placebo:
  - Fractional shortening: F(2,20)=5.05, **P=0.036**
  - E/A ratio: Wilcoxon two-sided W=93.5, **P=0.029** — but **not significant in the linear model**
  - Ejection fraction: **P=0.058, not significant**
- **No correction for multiple comparisons.** Authors' own verbatim limitation: *"The low number of dogs and
  multiple comparisons performed limit our statistical power, and the relatively short duration did not allow us
  to determine whether the rapamycin-treated dogs had increased lifespan."*
- Safety: routine bloodwork within normal limits; MCV significantly decreased on drug; increased activity in the
  high-dose group (70% vs 25% placebo, **P=0.066**, i.e. not significant).
- Funding: partially NIH P30AG013280 (UW Nathan Shock Center).
- **Honest reading: this is a 15-dog-on-drug, 10-week, uncorrected, mixed-model-disagreeing echocardiographic
  signal.** It is hypothesis-generating and is routinely cited as though it showed rapamycin improves dog heart
  function. It did not establish that.

### 3b. TRIAD — the trial that actually matters
Coleman LA, Creevy KE, et al. "Test of Rapamycin in Aging Dogs (TRIAD): study design and rationale for a
prospective, parallel-group, double-masked, randomized, placebo-controlled, multicenter trial of rapamycin in
healthy middle-aged dogs from the Dog Aging Project." *GeroScience* 2025;47(3):2851–2877.
doi:10.1007/s11357-024-01484-7.
- **Primary objective: whether rapamycin extends LIFESPAN in healthy, middle-aged, medium-to-large companion
  dogs.** This is a genuine T3 clinical outcome — the *only* one in the entire rapamycin longevity literature.
  The authors' own claim: *"the first rigorous test of a pharmacologic intervention against biological aging with
  lifespan and healthspan metrics as endpoints to be performed outside of the laboratory in any species."*
- Design: rapamycin or identical placebo **by mouth once weekly (on Wednesday) for 1 year**; per-dog participation
  3 years in trial + 2 years post-trial monitoring (5 years total); recheck visits at 6/12/18/24/30/36 months,
  final visits at 48 and 60 months.
- Eligibility: dogs ≥7 years, ≥44 lb (20 kg), apparently healthy, examinable without sedation; excluded for
  diabetes, heart disease, cancer, infection.
- Secondary/healthspan: echocardiography + ECG, blood pressure, activity monitors, cognition battery, bloods,
  urine, hair and faecal samples, owner surveys.
- ~20 clinical sites (Texas A&M, Tufts, UGA, Arizona among them).
- **Status as of 2026: STILL RECRUITING. 235 of 580 target dogs enrolled (~41%).** Aim to finish enrolling by
  end of 2026, start medication spring 2026, **trial slated to conclude November 2029.**
- → **No TRIAD results have been reported, and none are expected before ~2029.** Any 2026 claim that "rapamycin
  extends lifespan in dogs" is unsupported.

### 3c. The 2024 Dog Aging Project funding crisis
- The DAP was funded by a **five-year, ~$29M NIA grant** (~$29M in federal funding since 2018). In December 2023
  Kaeberlein disclosed on social media that NIA had declined renewal; funding expired **June 2024**, threatening
  **up to 90% of the annual budget**.
- Kaeberlein to STAT (2024-01-24), verbatim: *"The trial would end only half-powered. So it is very unlikely that
  we would be able to actually detect an effect statistically, even if the clinical trial worked."*
  Founders said they needed to raise **$40–50M**. A petition drew ~10,000 signatures.
- **Rescue:** December 2024 (Texas A&M press release 2024-12-09) — a **$7 million NIH award** to expand TRIAD.
  The AVMA's 2025-02-26 story headlines "$7M grant" but its body says **"$5 million over five years"** from NIA,
  with only the **first-year portion received** and subsequent years described as uncertain. **These two figures
  are not reconciled in any source I could reach — flag as unresolved.**
- Enrollment trajectory across sources: ~50% of (then-)target Jan 2024 (STAT) → 170 dogs Dec 2024 (Texas A&M) →
  "over 180" Feb 2025 (AVMA) → 235 in 2026, target 580. The Jan-2024 "50%" cannot be reconciled with a 580 target
  and almost certainly refers to a smaller earlier target. **Flag as a cross-caliber issue.**
- Kaeberlein has since left UW and is CEO of **Optispan** (Seattle). Promislow remains a co-director.

## 4. Off-label human use

### 4a. The survey — the most-cited "safety" evidence, and the weakest
Kaeberlein TL, Green AS, Haddad G, Hudson J, Isman A, Nyquist A, Rosen BS, Suh Y, Zalzala S, Harinath G,
Kaeberlein M, et al. "Evaluation of off-label rapamycin use to promote healthspan in 333 adults."
*GeroScience* 2023 Oct;45(5):2757–2768. doi:10.1007/s11357-023-00818-1. PMID/PMC10187519.
- **333 rapamycin users vs 172 never-users; 505 completed the full survey. Survey run March–June 2022.**
- **Recruitment, verbatim:** *"Participants were recruited to the study by direct email from participating
  physicians, through social media posts, via targeted recruitment within rapamycin user groups such as the
  Rapamycin Users Facebook page and the Rapamycin News website (https://www.rapamycin.news/), word of mouth, and
  the study website."*
  → This is the definition of a self-selected enthusiast sample. The comparison group is a *different*,
  non-matched convenience sample (users mean age ~61, 78% male; non-users mean age 52–56, 63% male —
  **the users are ~5–9 years older and much more male**, and the analysis of "conditions less frequent in users"
  is therefore comparing non-comparable groups).
- Regimens: **88–92% once-weekly**, median weekly dose **6 mg** (range 1–20 mg), median duration **218 days**,
  only 4 participants on daily dosing.
- **Findings:** *"Only mouth sores was significantly more prevalent in rapamycin users compared to non-users"*
  (FDR-adjusted p<0.05). And *"Several conditions were significantly less frequent in rapamycin users compared to
  non-users: abdominal cramps, depression, abdominal pain, muscle tightness, anxiety, and eye pain."*
  → **The "less depression, less anxiety, less abdominal pain" result is the tell.** No plausible mechanism makes
  weekly rapamycin reduce eye pain and anxiety. These are healthy-user and reporting-bias artefacts, and they
  calibrate exactly how much confounding is present in the *same dataset* that produced the reassuring safety
  conclusion. If the design manufactures six spurious benefits, it can equally hide harms.
- **Authors' own limitations, verbatim:** *"This study has several limitations that make the data less reliable
  than what would be obtained from a double-blind, randomized clinical trial. The self-reported nature of the data
  and the possibility of unintended bias in the participant pool reduce confidence that these results would be
  recapitulated in a larger, more heterogenous population. In particular, we cannot rule out the possibility that
  the population of rapamycin users is self-selected against people who started taking rapamycin and stopped
  because of negative experiences."*
  → **Survivorship bias, admitted by the authors, in the study that is used to argue rapamycin is safe.**
- **COI, verbatim:** *"MK and GH are co-founders of Optispan Geroscience, which has no material interest in
  rapamycin or rapamycin derivative production or sales at this time. ASG and BSR are practicing physicians who
  may prescribe rapamycin off-label for certain patients when appropriate. AI, AN, and SZ are employees and/or
  shareholders of AgelessRX, a longevity-focused telemedicine platform that sponsors several clinical trials..."*
- Companion paper: "Evaluation of off-label rapamycin use on oral health" (PMID 38839644).

### 4b. How many people actually take it
- No registry, no prescription-database study, no published prevalence estimate could be located.
- The only quantified figure reachable is a **vendor self-report**: AgelessRx states it provides rapamycin
  off-label to **"more than 5,000 people"** in the US (and claims 200,000+ platform members overall, not
  rapamycin-specific). Marketing entry price ~$65/month. **Vendor self-report; treat as marketing, not data.**
- **This is a genuine gap: nobody knows the denominator.** Every statement of the form "thousands of biohackers
  take rapamycin" is uncounted.

## 5. Safety: transplant dose vs intermittent low dose

### 5a. What the label actually says (FDA, Rapamune/sirolimus)
Boxed warning covers **immunosuppression with increased susceptibility to infection and the possible development
of lymphoma and other malignancies**. Label also carries: increased risk of abnormal wound healing (explicitly
flagged for BMI >30), hyperlipidaemia requiring monitoring and NCEP-guideline intervention, hyperglycaemia and
diabetes mellitus as adverse reactions, and increased skin-cancer risk on immunosuppressive therapy.
Approved indication is **prophylaxis of organ rejection in renal transplant**, at **daily** dosing.
→ Everything in the longevity conversation is off-label, at a different dose, on a different schedule, in a
different population, for an unapproved purpose.

### 5b. Does intermittent dosing avoid the adverse effects? — the central open question
**The honest answer as of 2026: this is largely inference, and the human evidence that does exist is mixed and
weakly against the clean version of the hypothesis.**

Evidence *for* tolerability of intermittent/low dosing:
- Mannick 2014: 6 weeks, well tolerated, but **AE rate roughly double placebo (37.7–50.9% vs 20.3%)** and mouth
  ulcers even at 0.5 mg daily.
- PEARL 2025: 48 weeks, no group differences in safety bloods or serious AEs — but n≈38/arm and
  **~3× lower systemic exposure than intended** because of the compounded formulation.
- Kaeberlein 2023 survey: only mouth sores elevated — but self-selected, survivor-biased (see 4a).
- Lee DJW, Hodzic Kuerec A, Maier AB. "Targeting ageing with rapamycin and its derivatives in humans: a systematic
  review." *Lancet Healthy Longev* 2024;5(2):e152–e162. doi:10.1016/S2666-7568(23)00258-1. PMID 38310895.
  18,400 unique articles screened → **19 included studies**. Verbatim: *"No serious adverse events attributed to
  rapamycin and its derivatives were reported in healthy individuals; however, there were increased numbers of
  infections and increases in total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and triglycerides in individuals with
  ageing-related diseases."* Also: *"Overall, no significant effects on the endocrine, muscular, or neurological
  systems were found."* and *"The effects of rapamycin or its derivatives on the respiratory, digestive, renal,
  and reproductive systems were not assessed."*
  → 19 studies. Total. Across the whole field. And the safety reassurance is explicitly conditional on being
  *healthy* — i.e. it does not transfer to the older, comorbid people the drug is aimed at.

Evidence *against*:
- **Kraig et al 2018** (*Exp Gerontol* 105:53–69, doi:10.1016/j.exger.2017.12.026): 25 adults aged 70–95,
  **1 mg/day for 8 weeks** (continuous, not intermittent), mean circulating level 7.2 ng/mL. No significant
  adverse clinical outcomes, **but** significant decrease in plasma albumin, **increased triglycerides and
  increased HbA1c**, and a near-significant (p=0.06) increase in VLDL. So metabolic drift appears at *low* dose,
  not just transplant dose.
- **RAPA-EX-01 (2026)** found a **statistically significant increase in HbA1c** in the sirolimus arm at
  **6 mg once weekly** — i.e. under exactly the intermittent regimen the hypothesis says should be metabolically
  clean. See §6.
- No human study has ever measured wound healing, infection outcomes, or malignancy incidence on intermittent
  low-dose rapamycin over any meaningful horizon.

**Bottom line for the article:** the "intermittent dosing avoids the side effects" claim rests on
(i) mTORC1-vs-mTORC2 pharmacology, which is mechanistic inference; (ii) rodent data; (iii) short human trials
underpowered for the harms that matter. There is **no human trial designed or powered to detect
immunosuppression, impaired wound healing, or cancer incidence at intermittent low doses.** That is the
load-bearing gap.

## 6. 2025–2026 new results and trials

### 6a. RAPA-EX-01 — the most important new rapamycin result, and it is negative
Stanfield B, Kaeberlein M, Leroux B, et al. "Exercise and Weekly Sirolimus (Rapamycin) in Older Adults:
RAPA-EX-01 Randomised, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Trial." *J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle* 2026.
doi:10.1002/jcsm.70274. Published April 2026.
Protocol paper: *Trials* 2024;25:642, doi:10.1186/s13063-024-08490-2.
- **40 sedentary adults aged 65–85** (mean 72.2 y, 47.5% female), randomised 1:1 to **sirolimus 6 mg once weekly**
  (commercial 2 mg tablets ×3) or matched placebo, **13 weeks**. Dose taken on day 6 of the training week,
  ~24 h after the last exercise session — deliberately designed to test the "cycling" hypothesis.
- Both arms did a standardised home-based programme 3×/week (chair-stands + exercycle).
- **Primary outcome: change in 30-second chair-stand repetitions at 13 weeks.**
- **Result (prespecified ITT): adjusted mean difference (sirolimus − placebo) = −2.13 repetitions,
  95% CI −4.61 to 0.34, p = 0.089.** Not significant, **direction favours placebo**.
- **Sensitivity analyses were significant against the drug:** complete-case −2.46 reps, p = 0.045;
  per-protocol p = 0.007.
- All secondary outcomes trended toward placebo: 6-min walk −4.87 m (p=0.706), grip strength −1.19 kg (p=0.344),
  SF-36 slightly favouring placebo. **Every confidence interval spanned zero and the direction usually favoured
  placebo.**
- **Safety:** 17/20 (85%) in each arm had ≥1 AE, but total event burden **99 (sirolimus) vs 63 (placebo)**.
  **Statistically significant increase in HbA1c on sirolimus.** One pneumonia in the sirolimus arm.
- **Conclusion:** once-weekly 6 mg sirolimus **did not enhance and may have modestly attenuated** the short-term
  functional gains of a home exercise programme in older adults.
- **Funding/COI:** funded by **Dr Brad Stanfield Ltd and Vitasang Ltd** — i.e. a YouTube health communicator
  self-funding a trial of a hypothesis he had publicly promoted. Kaeberlein is senior collaborator.
  Stanfield stated no affiliation with Pfizer and no financial incentive in the outcome. **Notably this is one of
  the few trials in the field where the funder had a public reputational stake in a POSITIVE result and published
  a negative one.** That deserves credit and makes the null more credible, not less.
- Peter Attia's commentary (peterattiamd.com) is a fair critique from a rapamycin-sympathetic reader: the
  training stimulus was modest — *"not a rigorous hypertrophy stimulus"* — and 13 weeks of chair-stands cannot
  address the outcomes that matter: *"No set of biomarkers measured over that time horizon is going to
  meaningfully resolve whether rapamycin slows cardiovascular, metabolic, neurodegenerative, or oncologic
  disease—and those are the questions that actually determine whether the drug deserves a place in longevity
  medicine."* His position: *"I remain where I've been on rapamycin: curious, cautiously interested, and nowhere
  near ready to close the book."*

### 6b. UT Health San Antonio dose-optimisation trial — launched March 2026
UT Health San Antonio press release, 2026-03-16 (news.uthscsa.edu).
- **~84 older adults** in the main sub-study; arms = **daily rapamycin vs intermittent rapamycin vs placebo**;
  **6 months treatment + 6 months follow-up**; NIA-funded.
- PIs: **Ellen Kraig, PhD; Dean Kellogg Jr., MD PhD; Brett Ginsburg, PhD** (Kraig ran the 2018 8-week study).
- Explicitly designed to answer the dosing question — *"testing different dosing schedules to determine how much
  rapamycin is needed to reach biological targets without negative side effects."*
- Kraig, verbatim: *"Rapamycin is widely discussed in popular culture as a longevity drug. But there's a
  difference between something that is biologically plausible and something that has been rigorously tested in
  people."*
- **No NCT number, no specific doses, and no primary endpoint disclosed in the release.** Results years away.
  Endpoints described only as immune and metabolic markers — i.e. **T2 surrogate**, again.

### 6c. ERAP — rapamycin in early Alzheimer's (ongoing)
Svensson JE, Bolin M, Thor D, et al. "Evaluating the effect of rapamycin treatment in Alzheimer's disease and
aging using in vivo imaging: the ERAP phase IIa clinical study protocol." *BMC Neurol* 2024;24:111.
doi:10.1186/s12883-024-03596-1. NCT06022068. Karolinska Institutet.
- **n=15**, amyloid-positive MCI/mild AD, ages 55–80, **7 mg rapamycin weekly for 6 months**.
- Primary endpoint: **change in cerebral glucose uptake on [18F]FDG PET** — a surrogate imaging endpoint, and an
  ambiguous one (mTOR inhibition plausibly *lowers* glucose uptake).
- Secondary: MoCA, CSF Aβ/p-tau/t-tau, cerebral blood flow.
- **No results published as of this writing.** n=15, open design → will not settle anything.

## 7. Cross-caliber issues / suspected misquotes

1. **"Rapamycin improves bone mass in men" (PEARL).** Widely repeated in coverage of PEARL. The published paper
   reports **"Bone outcomes: no significant changes"** and no significant improvement in men in lean tissue or
   bone mineral content. This looks like a preprint-vs-published drift or a misreading of a directional trend.
   Anyone repeating it should be asked for the p-value.
2. **The "20% improvement" number is a shape-shifter.** It originates as the ≥1.2-fold influenza GMT ratio in
   Mannick 2014 (T2, everolimus, 6 weeks, ≥65 y, Bayesian 80%-posterior threshold). It is regularly re-attached to
   PEARL, to rapamycin generally, and to "immune rejuvenation." At least one AI-generated summary encountered
   during this research attributed "improved vaccine responses by 20 percent" to the PEARL trial. **PEARL did not
   measure vaccine response at all.**
3. **The RTB101 phase 2b result is quoted with a 90% CI, never labelled as such.** OR 0.601, 90% CI 0.391–0.922.
   At 95% it would cross 1. It also came from the *second* prespecified analysis after the first (the
   everolimus-combination arm) failed, and pooled two separately-designed trial parts.
4. **PEARL's dose is not the community's dose.** PEARL used compounded rapamycin at 5/10 mg/week; the sponsor's
   own later PK paper puts compounded at **31% of commercial bioavailability per mg**. PEARL's 10 mg/week
   ≈ 3 mg/week commercial equivalent. Off-label users take a median 6 mg/week, mostly commercial. **PEARL's null
   safety result and its null primary endpoint therefore both apply to a lower exposure than most users take.**
   This cuts both ways and is almost never stated.
5. **Dog Aging Project funding figure.** Texas A&M (2024-12-09) and the AVMA headline say **$7 million**;
   the AVMA body text says **$5 million over five years**, first year only received. Unreconciled.
6. **TRIAD enrollment fraction.** STAT (Jan 2024) reported the trial was "approximately 50% of its enrollment
   target"; against the 580-dog target, enrollment was 170 in Dec 2024 and 235 in 2026 (~41%). The Jan-2024 50%
   figure must refer to a smaller earlier target and should not be quoted against 580.
7. **"Rapamycin is the gold standard" (AgelessRx).** True of the ITP mouse literature; presented on a page selling
   rapamycin to humans without that qualifier. Textbook T1→T3 promotion.
8. **PEARL analysed completers only (114), not ITT (125 enrolled, 129 per registry).** With 11 discontinuations
   unevenly distributed (6 placebo vs 5 drug), a completers-only analysis is a real, if modest, bias risk, and it
   is not how the trial was registered to be read.

## 8. Gaps — searched for, does not exist

- **No human randomised trial of rapamycin with a clinical outcome (mortality, incident disease, disability) as a
  primary endpoint has ever been conducted or is currently registered.** Every human trial in this line uses a
  surrogate: antibody titre, self-reported infection, visceral fat on DXA, chair-stands, FDG-PET uptake,
  immune/metabolic markers. Not one has a hard outcome.
- **No human evidence at all on the central pharmacological claim** — that intermittent dosing spares mTORC2 and
  therefore avoids immunosuppression, impaired wound healing, and dyslipidaemia. No trial has ever measured wound
  healing on intermittent rapamycin in humans. No trial has measured infection *incidence* as a safety endpoint at
  weekly dosing over ≥1 year. No cancer-incidence data in healthy users at any duration.
- **PEARL results were never posted to ClinicalTrials.gov** (hasResults = No), despite the trial completing
  2023-12-30.
- **No prevalence data on off-label rapamycin use.** No prescription-database analysis, no claims-data study,
  no registry. The only number available is a vendor's self-report (>5,000 people, AgelessRx).
- **No long-term (>1 year) placebo-controlled human safety data of any kind.** The longest is PEARL at 48 weeks,
  n≈38/arm, at ~1/3 intended exposure.
- **No head-to-head of compounded vs commercial rapamycin on clinical endpoints**, despite a 3× bioavailability
  gap and the fact that most longevity-clinic prescriptions are compounded.
- **No TRIAD results, and none due before ~November 2029.**
- **No published rapamycin trial with an epigenetic-clock primary endpoint has reported.** The only clock-adjacent
  claim in the literature is a PhenoAge *modelling exercise applied to published group-level data* from the Kraig
  trial (Hands 2025), which the authors themselves call *"suggestive at best...limited by lack of subject level
  observations."*
- **No retraction or erratum on any of the major rapamycin longevity trials could be located.** The corrective
  literature here takes the form of failed replication (PROTECTOR 1) and null trials (RAPA-EX-01), not retraction.
  Worth stating explicitly: the field's problems are not fraud, they are endpoint softness, multiplicity,
  self-selected samples, and vendor-run trials.
- **No independent (non-vendor, non-advocate) randomised trial of rapamycin for healthy human ageing has been
  completed.** PEARL = AgelessRx. The RTB101 programme = resTORbio/Novartis. RAPA-EX-01 = a health influencer's
  own company. The UT Health San Antonio trial (NIA-funded, launched March 2026) will be the first, and it reports
  years from now.
