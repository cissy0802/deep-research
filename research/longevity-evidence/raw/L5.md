# L5 — Caloric restriction, fasting, time-restricted eating: human & primate evidence

Research date: 2026-07-28. All numbers verified against primary sources (papers, PMC full text,
ClinicalTrials.gov API) unless explicitly labelled as hype/secondhand.

---

## 1. CALERIE-2 — the headline finding is that it MISSED its own primary endpoints

### The registry entry is the most damning document (NCT00427193, ClinicalTrials.gov API v2)

Registered PRIMARY outcome measures — all four:
- Change in Core Body Temperature, Baseline to 12 Months
- Change in Core Temperature, Baseline to 24 Months
- Change in Resting Metabolic Rate (RMR) Corrected for Changes in Body Composition, Baseline to 12 Months
- Change in Resting Metabolic Rate (RMR) Corrected for Changes in Body Composition, Baseline to 24 Months

Sponsor: Duke University. Phase: NA. Actual enrollment: **238** (note: papers report 218 randomized;
Waziry 2023 reports 220 — see cross-caliber issues). Start 2007-05, completion 2012-11 (actual).
Eligibility: age 21–50 men / 21–47 women; BMI ≥22 and <28. Exclusions: any CVD, diabetes, abnormal ECG.
**Neither mortality nor incident disease appears as a primary OR secondary endpoint.**

These primary endpoints come from the "rate of living" and oxidative-damage theories of aging —
they are mechanistic surrogates, not health outcomes.

### Ravussin 2015 (J Gerontol A 70:1097–104, PMID 26187233) — the primary-outcome paper

Verbatim: "The CR group achieved 11.7±0.7 %CR (mean ± standard error) and maintained 10.4±0.4% weight
loss. Weight change in AL was negligible. RMR residual decreased significantly more in CR than AL at
12 months (p = .04) but not 24 months (M24). **Core temperature change differed little between groups.**"

Completion: 82% of CR and 95% of AL completed.

=> At the 24-month endpoint, BOTH registered primary outcomes were null. This is almost never
mentioned in coverage of CALERIE. Everything famous about the trial is downstream secondary work.

### Achieved vs prescribed restriction

Target 25%. Achieved **11.7 ± 0.7%** (Ravussin 2015) / **11.9 ± 0.7%** (Kraus 2019, Waziry 2023 —
slightly different analysis samples). So the trial tested ~12% CR, not 25%. Any statement of the form
"25% caloric restriction in humans caused X" is false on its face.

### Kraus 2019 Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol (PMID 31303390) — cardiometabolic

Title itself says "**exploratory outcomes** of a multicentre, phase 2, randomised controlled trial".
N=218 (143 CR : 75 AL, 2:1), 2 years. Intake 2,467 → 2,170 kcal/day.
LDL-C p<0.0001; TC:HDL ratio p<0.0001; SBP p<0.0011; DBP p<0.0001; CRP p=0.012 at 2y;
insulin sensitivity index p<0.0001; metabolic syndrome score p<0.0001.
Weight −7.5 kg CR vs −0.1 kg AL; 71% of weight lost was fat mass.
NOTE: abstract gives p-values, not effect sizes with CIs — poor reporting caliber for a flagship result.

### Waziry 2023 Nature Aging (PMC10148951) — the DunedinPACE paper

N randomized 220 (145 CR, 75 AL); DNAm analysis sample 197 (128 CR, 69 AL). This is a **post-hoc
analysis of banked specimens**, not a pre-registered endpoint.

WHAT MOVED:
- DunedinPACE 12mo: d = −0.29 (95% CI −0.45, −0.13), P<0.003
- DunedinPACE 24mo: d = −0.25 (95% CI −0.41, −0.09), P<0.003
- "2–3%" slowing in pace of aging

WHAT DID NOT MOVE:
- PhenoAge 12mo: d = −0.03 (95% CI −0.19, 0.12), P>0.50; 24mo: d = 0.05 (−0.11, 0.20), P>0.50
- GrimAge 12mo: d = −0.04 (−0.16, 0.07), P>0.40; 24mo: d = 0.05 (−0.07, 0.17), P>0.40
- First-generation Horvath and Hannum clocks: similarly non-significant

So 1 of ~5 clocks moved. The two clocks with the best mortality-prediction track record (PhenoAge,
GrimAge) were flat, and at 24 months both had point estimates in the WRONG direction (d = +0.05).

AUTHORS' OWN LIMITS (verbatim, all three worth quoting):
> "There is no gold standard measure of biological aging. We analyzed several measures which represent
> the current state-of-the-art in DNAm quantification of biological aging. Nevertheless, these measures
> are acknowledged to be incomplete summaries of biological changes that occur with aging and to have
> technical limitations."

> "The CALERIE Trial sample does not represent the general population and treatment effects may not
> generalize beyond the population of healthy volunteers recruited to participate. CALERIE follow-up is,
> so far, limited to the end of the intervention period. Whether treatment and any slowing in biological
> aging that resulted from it translated to long-term clinical benefit is currently unknown."

> "Moreover, changes in DunedinPACE over follow-up showed substantial overlap between the CR treatment
> group and the AL control group; **effect-size estimates imply close to 90% overlap of DunedinPACE
> trajectories between the two groups.**"

COI (verbatim): "D.L.C. and D.W.B. are listed as inventors on a Duke University and University of Otago
invention, **DunedinPACE, that was licensed to a commercial entity.** The other authors declare no
competing interests." Funding: NIA R01AG061378 to D.W.B.

=> The one clock that moved is the one two of the authors invented and commercially licensed. This is
not an accusation of misconduct, but it is exactly the COI the article should record.

Author Correction (PMC10275750) exists but is trivial — Belsky's first affiliation was wrong. Not a
scientific correction. Worth stating so nobody over-reads "there's a correction on that paper."

The widely circulated gloss "2–3% slowing ≈ 10–15% reduction in mortality risk, like smoking cessation"
is an EXTRAPOLATION from DunedinPACE's observational validation cohorts, not measured in CALERIE.

### CALERIE Legacy (NCT05651620) — the follow-up still avoids hard endpoints

Title: "Legacy Effects of CALERIE™, a 2-year Calorie Restriction Intervention, on Hallmarks of
Healthspan and Aging". Status ACTIVE_NOT_RECRUITING. Est. enrollment 216. Started 2023-04-06.
Primary completion 2027-01-31, completion 2028-01-31.
PRIMARY outcomes: "Biological age - Klemera-Doubal Method" and "Healthspan", at 11–16 years post-trial.
SECONDARY: Homeostatic Dysregulation, Levine Phenotypic Age, height, weight, waist, hip, BMI, BP,
pulse, respiration, fat mass, fat-free mass.
=> Even the 11–16 year follow-up of the only long-term human CR RCT does not have mortality or
incident disease as a primary endpoint. No results before 2027 at the earliest.

## 2. The cost side of CR in humans (systematically under-reported)

### Bone — Villareal 2016 JBMR 31(1):40 (PMID 26332798)

N=218 (143 CR, 75 AL), BMI 25.1±1.7, age 37.9±7.2, 24 months.
BMD change at 24 months, CR vs AL:
- Lumbar spine: −0.013±0.003 vs +0.007±0.004 g/cm², p<0.001
- Total hip: −0.017±0.002 vs +0.001±0.003 g/cm², p<0.001
- Femoral neck: −0.015±0.003 vs −0.005±0.004 g/cm², p=0.03
Bone turnover: at 12 mo CR had greater increases in C-telopeptide, TRAP, and BSAP; at 24 mo only BSAP
differed.
AUTHORS' CONCLUSION VERBATIM:
> "Therefore, bone loss at clinically important sites of osteoporotic fractures represents a potential
> limitation of prolonged CR for extending life span."

This is the trial's own investigators saying the intervention has a skeletal cost. It is essentially
absent from popular CR advocacy.

### Lean mass

Kraus 2019: 71% of weight lost was fat → ~29% was non-fat.
CALERIE MRI ancillary (n=43; 28 CR, 15 AL): ~13% CR over 24 months gave 78% of tissue loss as fat and
17.5% as lean tissue. Both framings agree that a meaningful minority of the loss is lean tissue.

### Eating-disorder risk

Ganson et al., Canadian Study of Adolescent Health Behaviors, N=2,762 aged 16–30.
IF in past 12 months: 47.7% of women, 38.4% of men, 52% of transgender/gender-nonconforming.
IF significantly associated with overeating, binge eating, vomiting, laxative use, compulsive exercise
in women; compulsive exercise and higher EDE-Q global score in men.
Caveat: cross-sectional/prospective observational, self-report, and reverse causation is very plausible
(people with disordered eating adopt IF as a socially acceptable label for restriction). Should be
recorded as a signal, not a causal claim. Still: it is the only sizeable harm literature on IF and it
is almost never mentioned alongside benefit claims.

## 3. The two primate studies — the best evidence-grading case study in the field

### Wisconsin (WNPRC), Colman 2009 Science 325:201 (PMC2812811)

76 monkeys total: "Initially the study included 30 males and the cohort was expanded in 1994 to include
an additional 30 females and 16 males." All ADULTS at onset: "All animals were adults (7 to 14 years
old) when introduced into the study." Restriction: "each CR animal's individually determined baseline
intake was reduced by 10% per month over a 3-month period to reach the desired 30% restriction."

THE HEADLINE: "37% (14/38) of the control animals died of age-related causes compared to only 13%
(5/38) of the CR group", p=0.03.

THE BURIED LINE (verbatim): "**The effect of CR on overall mortality is in the predicted direction, but
is currently not statistically significant (p=0.16).**"

THE EXCLUSION THAT MADE IT WORK (verbatim): "Seven control and 9 animals subjected to CR died of
non-age-related causes, which included complications of anesthesia, gastric bloat, endometriosis, and
injury." => MORE CR animals (9) than controls (7) died of non-age-related causes. Removing those deaths
from the analysis is what converts a null all-cause result into a significant age-related one. The
choice of "age-related deaths" as the headline endpoint is the entire result.

### Wisconsin follow-up, Colman 2014 Nat Commun 5:3557 (PMID 24691430)

With 5 more years of accrued deaths:
- Age-related mortality: HR 2.89 (95% CI 1.34–6.25), P=0.007
- All-cause mortality: HR 1.78 (95% CI 1.04–3.04), P=0.037
Abstract verbatim: "Here we show that CR significantly improves age-related and all-cause survival in
monkeys on a long-term ~30% restricted diet since young adulthood."
=> To their credit, the all-cause result did eventually firm up. The honest reading: in 2009 the
all-cause claim was not supported; by 2014 it was, at a modest HR whose CI nearly touches 1.
(Note the abstract says "since young adulthood" whereas the 2009 paper says animals were 7–14y adults —
mild internal inconsistency in how they describe onset age.)

### NIA, Mattison 2012 Nature 489:318 (PMID 22932268)

Abstract verbatim: "We report here that a CR regimen implemented in young and older age rhesus monkeys
at the National Institute on Aging (NIA) has not improved survival outcomes."
Young-onset: 81 monkeys (40 control, 41 CR). Old-onset: 34 monkeys (16 control, 18 CR).
Some metabolic benefits (lower triglycerides in older CR; lower cholesterol in older CR males; lower
glucose in CR males) but no survival improvement and no significant difference in age-related disease
incidence.

### Mattison 2017 Nat Commun 8:14063 (PMC5247583) — the joint reconciliation

The four design differences the authors identify:

(1) DIET COMPOSITION — the big one.
"A naturally sourced diet was employed at the NIA facility" vs "a semi-purified diet was employed at UW
to ensure that intake could be fully defined."
Sucrose: NIA "sucrose comprised less than 7% of total carbohydrates" vs UW **45% of total carbohydrates**.
Fat: NIA 5.0% vs UW 10.6%.
=> UW controls were eating a high-sucrose, higher-fat purified diet. The UW "control" is closer to a
Western-diet model than to a healthy baseline. CR vs a bad diet is a different question from CR vs a
good diet.

(2) HOW CONTROLS WERE FED — the other big one.
NIA controls: received measured portions based on "data published by the National Research Council to
provide approximate ad libitum intake."
UW controls: "Food was provided at levels approximating ad libitum to control animals" with baseline
intake measured over 3–6 months.
=> NIA controls were portion-controlled to a book value, and ended up leaner — i.e. NIA "controls" were
themselves mildly restricted. The NIA study compared CR against mild CR; UW compared CR against
genuine ad libitum on a sugary diet. The contrast was much larger at UW.

(3) AGE AT ONSET.
NIA young/adolescent-onset: no survival benefit; strikingly, "CR monkeys reached 80% mortality before
the controls." NIA old-onset: no difference vs its own controls, but survival comparable to UW CR.
UW adult-onset: "statistically significant effect...increasing survival", control animals having
"almost twice the rate of death."
=> Adult-onset CR looked beneficial; juvenile-onset CR did not, and if anything trended harmful.

(4) GENETIC BACKGROUND.
NIA: monkeys "sourced from several locations and included monkeys of both Indian and Chinese origin."
UW: "All of Indian origin."

N: NIA 121 monkeys total across onset ages; UW 76 (38 per group, adult-onset only).

WHY THIS IS THE KEY CASE STUDY: two ~25-year studies of the same intervention in the same species
reached opposite conclusions, and the resolution was not "one was wrong" but "the control groups were
eating different things and the animals started at different ages." The effect of CR is contingent on
what you're restricting FROM. Nearly every popular retelling flattens this into "the monkey studies
agree that CR works", which is a misreading of the reconciliation paper.

## 4. Time-restricted eating RCTs

### TREAT — Lowe 2020 JAMA Intern Med 180(11):1491 (PMC7522780)

141 randomized, 116 in analysis. TRE = eat ad libitum 12:00–20:00, fast 20:00–12:00 (16:8). 12 weeks.
Adults with overweight/obesity. Delivered remotely.
PRIMARY OUTCOME (weight): between-group difference **−0.26 kg (95% CI −1.30 to 0.78; P=0.63)** — null.
APPENDICULAR LEAN MASS: TRE −0.64 kg vs control −0.17 kg, P<0.001.
The much-quoted line: "Of this, 1.10 kg (approximately 65% of weight lost) was lean mass; only 0.51 kg
of weight loss was fat mass" — vs a typical expectation of 20–30% lean.
ENERGY INTAKE NOT MEASURED (verbatim): "We do not have self-reported measures of energy or macronutrient
intake." Also "We did not measure changes in protein intake", which "clearly warrants future study."

CORRECTION: JAMA Intern Med published a correction — "there was an error in Figure 2" (jamanetwork
article 2776274). Minor but should be recorded.

THE CRITIQUE OF THE LEAN-MASS FINDING:
Tinsley GM, Peterson CM, Horne BD. "Caution Against Overinterpreting Time-Restricted Eating Results."
JAMA Intern Med. 2021 Jun 1;181(6):877–878 (PMID 33616609). Author reply exists: Weiss EJ, Lowe DA,
Vittinghoff E, same issue.
COI OF THE CRITICS (recorded per rule 4): Tinsley consulted for an intermittent fasting app; Horne
leads intermittent-fasting research funded by the Intermountain Research and Medical Foundation. So the
critique comes from people with a stake in TRE looking good. Both directions have interests here.
Substantive grounds for caution on the lean-mass number: it was one of many secondary DXA outcomes;
DXA lean soft tissue includes water/glycogen and is sensitive to hydration and glycogen shifts, which
change with fasting; and protein intake was unmeasured. Independent replication of a specifically
large lean-mass penalty from TRE has not materialized — see TREATY below, where lean mass results
tracked the (null) primary outcome.

### TREATY — Liu 2022 NEJM 386:1495 (PMID 35443107) — the largest/longest TRE RCT

139 adults with obesity (BMI 28–45), China, **12 months**. BOTH arms calorie-restricted:
men 1500–1800 kcal/day, women 1200–1500 kcal/day. TRE arm ate 08:00–16:00; control arm same calories,
no time restriction. 118/139 (84.9%) completed.
Weight: TRE −8.0 kg (95% CI −9.6 to −6.4); CR-alone −6.3 kg (95% CI −7.8 to −4.7).
Between-group difference **−1.8 kg (95% CI −4.0 to 0.4; P=0.11)** — null.
"Results of analyses of waist circumferences, BMI, body fat, body lean mass, blood pressure, and
metabolic risk factors were consistent with the results of the primary outcome."
CONCLUSION VERBATIM: "Among patients with obesity, a regimen of time-restricted eating was not more
beneficial with regard to reduction in body weight, body fat, or metabolic risk factors than daily
calorie restriction."

### Maruthur 2024 Annals Intern Med 177(5):549 — the isocaloric test

41 adults with obesity + prediabetes or diet-controlled T2D. Mean age 59, 93% women, mean BMI 36.
12 weeks. Randomized ISOCALORIC feeding study: meals for both arms "had the same nutrient content and
were isocaloric with total calories determined at baseline."
TRE arm: 10-hour window, 80% of calories before 1 p.m. UEP arm: ≤16-hour window, ≥50% of calories after
5 p.m.
Weight: TRE −2.3 kg vs UEP −2.6 kg; difference **0.3 kg (95% CI −1.2 to 1.9)** — null.
Fasting glucose, HOMA-IR, glucose AUC, glycated albumin: all null.
CONCLUSION VERBATIM: "In the setting of isocaloric eating, TRE did not decrease weight or improve
glucose homeostasis relative to a UEP, suggesting that any effects of TRE on weight in prior studies
may be due to reductions in caloric intake."
Funding: American Heart Association.
Limitation to record: N=41 is small; 93% women; 12 weeks.

### Peters/Schwarz 2025 Sci Transl Med 17(822), doi 10.1126/scitranslmed.adv6787

31 women with overweight/obesity, randomized crossover, 2 weeks early TRE (08:00–16:00) vs 2 weeks late
TRE (13:00–21:00), intended isocaloric.
Insulin sensitivity did not differ between or within interventions.
Shifting the window 5 h later delayed monocyte-derived circadian phase by ~24 minutes vs eTRE.
=> Demonstrates the mechanism (peripheral clocks do entrain to meal timing) while explicitly
dissociating it from metabolic benefit. This is the cleanest available separation of "TRE does
something biological" from "TRE does something useful."
(Could not reach full text — science.org 403, medRxiv 403. Verified via search result metadata +
Semantic Scholar listing. Flag as partially verified; a verifier should reach the full text.)

### BMJ 2025 network meta-analysis (PMC12175170)

99 randomized trials, 6,582 adults. Median trial duration **12 weeks (range 3–52)**.
>80% of studies judged low risk of bias.
Body weight vs AD-LIBITUM:
- Alternate-day fasting (ADF): −3.40 kg (95% CI −4.14 to −2.67), HIGH certainty
- Whole-day fasting (WDF): −2.36 kg (−3.02 to −1.70), HIGH certainty
- Continuous energy restriction (CER): −2.11 kg (−2.73 to −1.50), MODERATE certainty
- Time-restricted eating (TRE): −1.72 kg (−2.21 to −1.22), MODERATE certainty
Head-to-head vs CER:
- ADF vs CER: −1.29 kg (−1.99 to −0.59), MODERATE certainty — the only fasting strategy that beat CER,
  and by ~1.3 kg
- WDF vs CER: no significant difference
- TRE vs CER: no separate pooled estimate reported in the abstract/results reachable
CONCLUSION VERBATIM: "The current evidence provides some indication that intermittent fasting diets
have similar benefits to continuous energy restriction for weight loss and cardiometabolic risk
factors. Longer duration trials are needed to further substantiate these findings."
Authors note: "estimates were similar among trials with less than 24 weeks follow-up (n=76); however,
moderate-to-long-term trials (≥24 weeks, n=17) only showed benefits in weight reduction in diet
strategies compared with ad-libitum."
=> Only 17 of 99 trials ran ≥24 weeks. No hard clinical endpoints anywhere in the evidence base.

SUMMARY OF THE TRE QUESTION: across a small isocaloric feeding trial (Maruthur), the largest and
longest TRE RCT (TREATY, 12 months), a mechanistic crossover (Peters 2025), and a 99-trial network
meta-analysis, TRE does not beat matched calorie intake. The benefit attributed to meal timing is
almost entirely a calorie effect. ADF may have a small genuine edge (−1.29 kg vs CER), plausibly also
via adherence/intake rather than timing.

## 5. The "91% higher cardiovascular mortality" episode — hype in BOTH directions

### What it actually was

AHA EPI|Lifestyle Scientific Sessions 2024, Chicago, March 18–21. **Abstract P192**, published only in
a Circulation supplement (Circulation. 2024;149:AP192). Lead: Victor Wenze Zhong, Shanghai Jiao Tong
University School of Medicine. NHANES 2003–2018 linked to National Death Index. ~20,000 adults.
AHA's own newsroom release carried the "91% higher risk of cardiovascular death" headline. Not peer
reviewed at that point.

### The methodological problem

Eating window was derived from **two 24-hour dietary recalls** and then used to classify a person's
habitual eating pattern over ~8 years of follow-up.

Expert criticism (Science Media Centre, 2024-03-18/20):
- Prof Kevin McConway (Open University): researchers "classified people into different dietary patterns
  on the basis of what and when they reported they ate in just two days, over a study period averaging
  8 years"; "We don't know whether their eating times over those two 24-hour periods was typical of the
  times they usually ate."
- McConway on reverse causation: "It could even be that there's some sort of reverse causation. People
  who were already worried about their cardiovascular health, perhaps for some good reason such as
  signs of illness or previous unhealthy activity, might be more likely to decide to restrict their
  eating times."
- Dr Duane Mellor (Aston Medical School): "It is unclear if it included how healthy diet pattern was or
  even what people ate"; questioned adjustment for smoking, physical activity, alcohol.
- Prof Tom Sanders (KCL): uncertainty about "whether statistical adjustment have been made for
  confounding factors (smoking, physical activity, alcohol intake, social class)."
- McConway: "It's unlikely to have been through the full peer review process yet."

### IT WAS EVENTUALLY PUBLISHED — and the numbers changed

Chen M, Xu L, Van Horn L, Manson JE, Tucker KL, Du X, Feng N, Rong S, Zhong VW.
"Association of eating duration less than 8 h with all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality."
Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome: Clinical Research & Reviews. 2025. doi:10.1016/j.dsx.2025.103278
Received 17 June 2025; revised 20 July 2025; accepted 4 August 2025.

Abstract verbatim:
> "Methods: Adult participants who reported usual intake from two valid 24-h dietary recalls were
> included from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey in 2003–2018 (n = 19,831).
> Mortality status as of December 2019 was obtained through linkage to the National Death Index.
> Average eating duration was categorized as <8, 8–<10, 10–<12, 12–14 h (mean duration), >14–16, and
> >16 h. Multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) were derived.
> Results: During a median follow-up of 8.1 years, compared with eating duration of 12–14 h, eating
> duration <8 h was robustly associated with higher cardiovascular mortality (HR, 2.35 [95 % CI,
> 1.39–3.98]), **but not with all-cause and cancer mortality.** The positive association with
> cardiovascular mortality remained consistent across 8 subgroups stratified by race/ethnicity,
> socioeconomic factors, and smoking status, and survived 14 sensitivity analyses. However, **the
> association with all-cause mortality did not survive many sensitivity analyses.**
> Conclusions: Although a positive association was observed between eating duration <8 h and
> cardiovascular mortality, further research is required to understand whether this risk is attributed
> to the short eating duration itself or residual confounding resulting from its contributing factors."

KEY CROSS-CALIBER PROBLEM: the famous number is "91% higher" (HR ≈1.91) from the unreviewed abstract;
the peer-reviewed paper reports **HR 2.35** for CVD mortality — a LARGER relative effect — while
simultaneously reporting **no association with all-cause mortality**. The public number and the
published number are different numbers, and the more decision-relevant endpoint (all-cause death) is
null. Both the alarm and the reassurance in circulation are miscalibrated.

Methodological detail worth recording: eating duration = clock time from first to last eating occasion
>5 kcal, averaged over the two recall days. Adjustment went to Model 5 including demographics, SES,
food security, smoking, drinking, physical activity, HEI-2015, BMI and BMI², self-reported CVD/cancer/
dyslipidemia/hypertension/diabetes, and breakfast skipping. So the confounding critique was partly
addressed. The residual-confounding worry the authors themselves flag is that a <8 h eating window in
NHANES is a marker of chaotic/constrained life circumstances (shift work, poverty, illness), not of
deliberate TRE. Nothing in NHANES distinguishes intentional TRE from skipping meals because you can't
afford them.

### THE AUTHORS' OWN GAP STATEMENT (superb quote for the gaps section)

From the introduction, verbatim:
> "All published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) studied the effects of TRE regimens on changes in
> risk factors within 1 year. **Long-term RCTs investigating the effects of 8-h TRE on hard clinical
> endpoints are currently unavailable and will be challenging to conduct in the future.**"

And: "Eight-hour TRE is the most commonly studied, but its cardiometabolic benefits may primarily stem
from caloric restriction rather than the timing of food intake."

## 6. Fasting-mimicking diet (Longo / L-Nutra)

### Wei 2017 Sci Transl Med 9(377):eaai8700 (PMID 28202779)

100 randomized, **71 completed** three FMD cycles. Randomized crossover: Arm 1 (n=48) normal diet 3
months then crossed to FMD; Arm 2 (n=52) FMD immediately. FMD = 5 consecutive days/month × 3 cycles.
Results: "Three FMD cycles reduced body weight, trunk, and total body fat; lowered blood pressure; and
decreased insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1)."
The broader biomarker panel came from a POST HOC analysis (verbatim): "A post hoc analysis of subjects
from both FMD arms showed that body mass index, blood pressure, fasting glucose, IGF-1, triglycerides,
total and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and C-reactive protein were more beneficially affected
in participants at risk for disease than in subjects who were not at risk."
CONCLUSION VERBATIM: "Cycles of a 5-day FMD are safe, feasible, and effective in reducing markers/risk
factors for aging and age-related diseases."
Note the conclusion is scrupulously about MARKERS. The tier-3 promotion happens downstream.
Caliber problem: abstract gives directions, not effect sizes with CIs. 29% attrition.

### Brandhorst 2024 Nat Commun 15:1309 (PMC10879164) — the "2.5 years younger" paper

WHAT IT IS: "secondary and exploratory analysis of blood samples from a randomized clinical trial"
(NCT02158897), plus a second secondary analysis (NCT04150159). Both are re-analyses of already
published RCTs, not new trials.
- NCT02158897: N=100 randomized two arms; control n=48 "continued their normal diet for 3 months";
  FMD n=52; control arm then crossed over, n=32 completed 3 cycles.
- NCT04150159: n=44 FMD vs n=40 Mediterranean-diet comparator; analysis used "baseline as well as
  values after the 3rd FMD cycle to match the time points."
FMD composition: day 1 ~4600 kJ (11% protein, 46% fat, 43% carb); days 2–5 ~3000 kJ (9% protein, 44%
fat, 47% carb). 3 monthly cycles.

BIOLOGICAL AGE MEASURE: **Klemera-Doubal method (KDM)**, built from 7 biomarkers: albumin, alkaline
phosphatase, serum creatinine, C-reactive protein, HbA1c, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol.
Not a methylation clock. These are ordinary clinical chemistry values that move acutely with diet.

THE EFFECT — AND THE CATCH:
- NCT02158897 (n=52): "median biological age decreased by nearly 2.5 years (Fig. 3A, p = 0.0007)"
- NCT04150159 (n=34): "median decrease in biological age after 3 FMD cycles was 2.7 years"
- Pooled (N=86): "median decrease in biological age of 2.6 years"
**All of these are WITHIN-GROUP pre-post comparisons in the FMD arm.** The 2.5-year figure is not a
randomized between-group contrast against the control arm, even though both parent trials had control
arms. This is the single most important thing to get right about this paper: a randomized trial's
control group exists precisely to tell you what would have happened anyway, and the headline number
does not use it.

WHY THAT MATTERS SPECIFICALLY HERE: KDM is built from CRP, HbA1c, SBP, total cholesterol — all of which
fall transiently during and shortly after 5 days of severe energy restriction. A within-arm pre-post
drop is close to guaranteed and tells you about acute physiological response, not aging rate. General
methodological literature makes this point about cross-sectional biological-age predictors: observed
shifts may reflect acute physiological responsiveness to dietary inputs rather than altered aging
trajectories (see BMC Med Res Methodol 2024;24:articles on KDM assumptions — the "identical-association
assumption" is untestable cross-sectionally).

AUTHORS' OWN LIMITATIONS (verbatim, on the life-expectancy simulation):
> "There are major limitations that need to be acknowledged in regards to our simulation: First, the
> models used to estimate the effects of 3 cycles of FMD on Biological age were based on results from
> two clinical trials and include only 86 participants. Moreover, trial participants generally shared
> advantageous social, economic, behavioral, and health characteristics and therefore their results may

### THE HYPE ARTEFACT — "11 years younger"

Newsweek headline: "Research Suggests Diet Plan May Reduce Biological Age by 11 Years."
The article's own text shows where 11 comes from: "A simulation of the effect of 3 FMD cycles for
twenty years assuming continued efficacy of this dietary intervention suggests a potential decrease in
biological age of about 11 years." The measured result was 2.5 years (within-group). The 11-year figure
is a 20-year extrapolation of a within-group short-term biomarker shift, promoted to a headline.
Record ONLY as an example of hype.
Chain of promotion: within-group pre-post biomarker change (2.5y) → 20-year simulation (11y) →
headline number. Three tier-jumps in one press cycle.

## 7. THE CENTRAL GAP — no hard clinical endpoint anywhere

I ran a systematic scan of ClinicalTrials.gov (API v2, 2026-07-28) over all registered studies matching
"caloric restriction" OR "calorie restriction" OR "intermittent fasting" OR "time-restricted eating" OR
"fasting-mimicking": **1,472 studies scanned.** I regex-matched primary outcome measures for mortality,
death, all-cause, survival, MACE, or incident cancer/diabetes/CVD/stroke/dementia.

**9 hits. Every one is in a disease population, not a healthy-aging population:**
| NCT | Study | N | Primary |
|---|---|---|---|
| NCT02750826 | Breast Cancer WEight Loss (BWEL) | 3177 | Invasive disease-free survival |
| NCT05384444 | FMD in colorectal cancer | 602 | Disease-free survival |
| NCT07391267 | CKD/MAFLD (not yet recruiting) | 90 | Composite renal + death |
| NCT01754350 | Ketogenic diet + fasting, reirradiation glioma | 50 | Progression-free survival |
| NCT03709147 | Metformin ± FMD, LKB1-inactive lung cancer | 64 | Progression-free survival |
| NCT01645852 | Low-calorie diet before liver resection | 65 | Blood loss/complications/mortality |
| NCT04627688 | OptiSurg, pre-surgical diet | 40 | Death/MI/stroke/re-intervention |
| NCT00645931 | (mis-registered; BMT in children) | 40 | — |
| NCT06603155 | TRE in metastatic HNSCC | 0 | WITHDRAWN |

=> **No registered CR, fasting, or TRE trial in a healthy or general population anywhere in the world
has mortality or incident age-related disease as a primary endpoint.** Not one. The entire human
longevity case for CR/fasting rests on surrogate endpoints: weight, lipids, blood pressure, insulin
sensitivity, DNA-methylation clocks, and clinical-chemistry-derived "biological age." The largest
studies with hard endpoints (BWEL, n=3177) are oncology weight-loss trials, which test a different
question in a different population.

This is not an accident of my search terms — it reflects the design economics the Chen 2025 authors
name explicitly: such trials "will be challenging to conduct in the future."

## 8. Cross-caliber / number-hygiene issues found

1. "25% caloric restriction" vs achieved 11.7–11.9%. The trial name, the protocol, and most secondary
   citations say 25%; the delivered dose was under half that. Every CALERIE effect size is an effect of
   ~12% CR.
2. CALERIE N is quoted as 218 (Kraus, Villareal), 220 (Waziry), and 238 (registry actual enrollment).
   The 218/220 figures are randomized-and-analyzed; 238 is enrolled. Any claim should state which.
3. "91% higher CVD death" (unreviewed abstract) vs HR 2.35 (peer-reviewed). Two different numbers for
   the same analysis; the widely circulated one is the unreviewed one. And the all-cause result — null —
   almost never travels with either.
4. FMD "2.5 years younger" is within-group pre-post, not randomized between-group; "11 years" is a
   20-year simulation. Neither is a controlled effect estimate.
5. Colman 2009's headline endpoint (age-related deaths, p=0.03) excluded 16 deaths, 9 of them in the CR
   arm; all-cause was p=0.16 in the same paper. "CR extended monkey lifespan (2009)" is a misquote of
   what that paper established.
6. "2–3% slower pace of aging ≈ 10–15% lower mortality risk ≈ smoking cessation" — the mortality
   translation is imported from DunedinPACE's observational validation cohorts, not measured in CALERIE.
7. Mixing tiers: TRE's rodent literature shows timing-independent-of-calories effects; the human
   isocaloric trials do not. Claims routinely cite the rodent mechanism and the human popularity
   together as if they were one evidence base.
8. Colman 2014 abstract describes onset as "since young adulthood" while Colman 2009 says animals were
   7–14 year old adults. Minor, but the age-at-onset variable is precisely what the 2017 reconciliation
   says matters.
