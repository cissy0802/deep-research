### CLAIM 1
**Grossman-Stiglitz's actual thesis statement — "an equilibrium degree of disequilibrium"**
- **Source:** Grossman, S.J. & Stiglitz, J.E., "On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets," *American Economic Review* 70(3), Jun. 1980, pp. 393–408. Opening paragraphs, p. 393.
- **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED]
- **Caliber notes:** VERBATIM (read from extracted PDF text, not a summarizer): *"We propose here a model in which there is an equilibrium degree of disequilibrium: prices reflect the information of informed individuals (arbitrageurs) but only partially, so that those who expend resources to obtain information do receive compensation."* And the setup: *"Clearly not, for then those who arbitrage make no (private) return from their (privately) costly activity. Hence the assumptions that all markets, including that for information, are always in equilibrium and always perfectly arbitraged are inconsistent when arbitrage is costly."* Population: a 2-asset (one safe, one risky) noisy rational-expectations model with a continuum of ex-ante identical agents; λ = fraction who pay cost *c* to observe signal θ. Note the object of the model is the fraction who **pay for information**, not the fraction who **index** — see CROSS-CALIBER ISSUES.
- **URL:** https://www.pims.math.ca/files/Grossman_Stiglitz1980.pdf

### CLAIM 2
**The self-correcting mechanism, stated exactly (this is what "more passive → higher active returns" rests on)**
- **Source:** Grossman & Stiglitz 1980, p. 394, Conjecture 2 and the two-channel argument preceding it.
- **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED]
- **Caliber notes:** VERBATIM: *"Conjecture 2: The more individuals who are informed, the lower the ratio of expected utility of the informed to the uninformed."* The stated mechanism, verbatim: *"As more individuals become informed, the expected utility of the informed falls relative to the uninformed for two reasons: (a) The price system becomes more informative… (b) Even if the above effect did not occur, the increase in the ratio of informed to uninformed means that the relative gains of the informed, on a per capita basis, in trading with the uninformed will be smaller."* Equilibrium condition, verbatim: *"An overall equilibrium requires the two to have the same expected utility."* **Direction matters:** the paper states the comparative static in the direction of MORE informed → LOWER relative payoff. The "more passive → bigger mispricings → higher returns to remaining active" claim is the contrapositive, which the paper never writes out. It is a legitimate reading of the same equalization condition, but it is an inference, not a quoted result.
- **URL:** https://www.pims.math.ca/files/Grossman_Stiglitz1980.pdf

### CLAIM 3
**What pins down the equilibrium fraction of informed traders — it is the COST of information, not investor fashion**
- **Source:** Grossman & Stiglitz 1980, p. 394, Conjecture 3 and the preceding parameter list.
- **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED]
- **Caliber notes:** VERBATIM: *"The equilibrium number of informed and uninformed individuals in the economy will depend on a number of critical parameters: the cost of information, how informative the price system is (how much noise there is to interfere with the information conveyed by the price system), and how informative the information obtained by an informed individual is."* And: *"Conjecture 3: The higher the cost of information, the smaller will be the equilibrium percentage of individuals who are informed."* This is load-bearing for the article: in G-S, λ is an OUTPUT. An exogenous, preference-driven migration to indexing is not a shock the model contains. Coles-Heath-Ringgenberg (CLAIM 19) is the paper that extends G-S to make that a separate choice — and gets the "no effect" result precisely because informativeness ends up depending only on information cost.
- **URL:** https://www.pims.math.ca/files/Grossman_Stiglitz1980.pdf

### CLAIM 4
**G-S on the LIMIT case: perfect informativeness ⇒ no competitive equilibrium exists**
- **Source:** Grossman & Stiglitz 1980, p. 395 (Conjecture 6) and Section IV "On the Possibility of Perfect Markets," pp. 404–405.
- **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED]
- **Caliber notes:** VERBATIM, Conjecture 6: *"In the limit, when there is no noise, prices convey all information, and there is no incentive to purchase information. Hence, the only possible equilibrium is one with no information. But if everyone is uninformed, it clearly pays some individual to become informed. Thus, there does not exist a competitive equilibrium."* Section IV, VERBATIM: *"We showed that when the efficient markets hypothesis is true and information is costly, competitive markets break down."* And: *"Hence having any positive fraction informed is not an equilibrium. Having no one informed is also not an equilibrium, because then each trader, taking the price as given, feels that there are profits to be made from becoming informed."* **Caliber warning:** this nonexistence result is driven by the noise parameter (Var x\* → 0 or σ²_ε → 0), i.e. by prices becoming TOO informative. It is the mirror image of, not the same as, the "everyone indexes so nobody is informed" scenario. Quoting this as academic support for a 100%-passive breakdown is a misuse.
- **URL:** https://www.pims.math.ca/files/Grossman_Stiglitz1980.pdf

### CLAIM 5
**G-S's own prediction for the high-passive corner: thin markets at BOTH extremes**
- **Source:** Grossman & Stiglitz 1980, p. 395, Conjecture 7.
- **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED]
- **Caliber notes:** VERBATIM: *"Conjecture 7: That, other things being equal, markets will be thinner under those conditions in which the percentage of individuals who are informed (λ) is either near zero or near unity. ### CLAIM 17
**Bond & García explicitly REVERSE Grossman-Stiglitz's strategic structure — substitutes become complements**
- **Source:** Bond & García (2019 draft), Introduction, footnote 3.
- **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED] (published as RFS 2022); text from WP draft
- **Caliber notes:** VERBATIM footnote 3: *"Grossman and Stiglitz (1980) analyze traders' decisions to become informed, taking the set of trading agents as given, and show that information acquisition decisions are strategic substitutes: the incremental trading profits from private information decline as more traders become informed. ### CROSS-CALIBER ISSUES

**1. Gabaix-Koijen is not a passive-investing paper, and it has never been refereed.** Two independent problems that compound. (a) Full-text search of the 123-page paper finds exactly one substantive mention of passive holding, and it is a paraphrase of De Long et al. (1990) (CLAIM 9). The mechanism is *institutional mandate rigidity across sectors*, which would produce a low macro elasticity even in a 100%-active market. (b) It is still an NBER working paper as of 2026-08, five years after issue, while the authors' companion methods paper cleared JPE in 2024 (CLAIM 10). Any article sentence of the form "research shows passive flows move the market 5-for-1" is doing two illegitimate things at once: importing a non-passive result into a passive claim, and citing an unrefereed number as established.

**2. The "$5" is a headline, not an estimate.** The paper's own point estimates are M = 5.3, 5.9, 7.1, 7.7, 10.9 depending on specification, with standard errors around 1.9–2.3 (CLAIM 7). The abstract's "about $5" is at the LOW end of the family. Writers who cite "$5" as conservative are right; writers who cite it as precise are wrong. And van der Beck (2026) implies the quarterly-horizon multiplier overstates the long-run one by ~3× (CLAIM 12), which would put the long-run figure near 2. Hartley-Gerding (2025) reportedly reach a similar short-run/long-run split (CLAIM 13, unverified). **The 12-month update is that the multiplier literature is moving toward "large but transitory," not "large and permanent" — and transitory is fatal to the bubble reading.**

**3. HHL's number changed between working paper and journal, in the debunking direction, and nobody cites the change.** WP 2022: elasticity 15% more inelastic, strategic offset 60%, stylized calibration 12.5%. AER 2025: 11% more inelastic, offset two-thirds (~67%). (CLAIMS 14, 15.) A 15%→11% revision through refereeing is a ~27% haircut on the field's flagship "passive really does matter" number. Expect to find secondary sources still quoting 15%, or quoting 11% while citing the 2022 WP.

**4. HHL measures MICRO elasticity; Gabaix-Koijen measures MACRO elasticity. They are constantly conflated.** HHL's object is *"the demand for individual stocks"* — the Ford-vs-GM margin. GK's object is stocks-vs-bonds at the aggregate level. The index-bubble thesis is a claim about the aggregate market's price LEVEL, i.e. GK's object. HHL's 11% is the best-identified number in the debate and it is about the wrong margin for the thesis it is used to support. Gârleanu-Pedersen (CLAIM 18) is the only peer-reviewed *theory* that puts the damage specifically on the macro margin.

**5. Two peer-reviewed theories disagree on whether the drift to passive is self-correcting, because they model different margins.** G-S: information acquisition is a strategic SUBSTITUTE → self-correcting (CLAIM 2). Bond-García: market participation is a strategic COMPLEMENT → self-amplifying, no restoring force (CLAIM 17). Bond-García call this contrast out explicitly in a footnote. Neither is "wrong." The honest framing is that the self-correction reassurance is a property of one modeling choice, not a theorem.

**6. Gârleanu-Pedersen (4) and (5) point opposite ways, and only (5) ever gets quoted.** Falling *information* costs REDUCE macro inefficiency; falling *passive* costs INCREASE it (CLAIM 18). The last 25 years featured both — collapsing data/compute costs alongside collapsing ETF fees. The model's net prediction for the observed period is therefore ambiguous, and citing only result (5) is selective.

**7. Conflict-of-interest runs in both directions and the sign is not what you'd guess.** For the bubble thesis: Pedersen (AQR, active manager) authors both the anti-Sharpe paper and the co-authored theory predicting passive growth degrades macro efficiency and boosts active alpha — disclosed, but directionally convenient. Against the thesis: the most prominent "no informativeness effect" result (Koijen-Richmond-Yogo, CLAIM 20) comes from an author who is *also* half of Gabaix-Koijen, which cuts against a simple "index-adjacent academics debunk it" story. Coles-Heath-Ringgenberg and Chabakauri-Rytchkov are pure academics with no obvious stake. The FCA-commissioned Bristol survey (CLAIM 22) is the closest thing to a disinterested referee and it declines to call it.

**8. Grossman-Stiglitz's limit result is routinely misapplied.** Conjecture 6 / Section IV proves nonexistence when prices become TOO informative (noise → 0), not when informed traders vanish (CLAIM 4). The "if everyone indexes there's no equilibrium" op-ed move borrows G-S's authority for the wrong limit. G-S's actual high-λ prediction is Conjecture 7: thin markets — a *volume* claim (CLAIM 5). And G-S themselves state they have no general proof of any conjecture outside the CARA-normal example.

**9. Two summarizer fabrications caught in this session — verify before quoting anything not read from extracted text.** (a) A WebFetch summarizer invented quotes for Chabakauri-Rytchkov claiming indexing *raises* volatility and *reduces* price informativeness; the real abstract says indexing **decreases** market volatility and makes no informativeness claim (CLAIM 21). (b) A WebSearch summarizer asserted Gabaix-Koijen find flows drive "over one-third of stock fluctuations"; that string and that magnitude do not appear in the paper, whose actual figure is a 16% R² difference (CLAIM 11). Both fabrications happened to push in the pro-bubble direction. Claims 13, 14, 20 and the Sharpe definitions in 23 remain summarizer-sourced and are marked [UNCONFIRMED WORDING] — they need a PDF pass before any of their strings go into the article.

**10. "Volatility rises" and "price efficiency falls" are separate findings and only the first has rough consensus.** The FCA survey, Coles-Heath-Ringgenberg's theory, and Jiang et al.'s calibration all get higher volatility from more passive; the efficiency result is where the literature splits. But Jiang et al.'s calibrated magnitude for a 90:10 → 40:60 shift is annual volatility 11.13% → 11.84% (CLAIM 22) — a ~0.7pp move, which is a real effect and a rounding error at the same time. Whether that counts as "passive breaks markets" is a rhetorical choice, not an empirical one.
