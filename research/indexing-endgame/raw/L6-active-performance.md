### CLAIM 15 — The counter-rebuttal: Morningstar's Jeffrey Ptak dismantles the IAA paper (June 2026)
- **Numbers/arguments:** "over the 10 years ended Dec. 31, 2024, SPIVA reported that almost 90% of active US equity funds failed to beat their index, but the authors found only 63% lagged after the adjustments" — and even so, "nearly two-thirds of active US stock fund assets failed to outperform over the decade." On the noisiest category: SPIVA reported >61% of active "investment-grade intermediate" bond funds underperformed over 10 years, falling to ~24% after adjustments; but **"nearly one-fifth of the 37-percentage-point improvement … stemmed from replication errors"** and "only two of those adjustments—crediting dead funds and asset-weighting—actually improved the results. The others—the benchmark adjustment and the interaction effect of applying all three adjustments together—made the results worse."
- **Original dead-fund evidence:** of 150 high-yield-bond funds as of Dec 31, 2004, 72 survived 20 years; of the 78 that died, average lifespan 8.3 years; 32 of the 78 outperformed the Markit iBoxx Liquid High Yield index up to their death, but "the dead funds that outperformed survived 2,844 months in aggregate, while those that lagged lived nearly twice as long: 4,518 months."
- **Asset-weighting objection:** "when you asset-weight, you essentially bake in the choices that investors at large have made about which funds to own. … The top 1% of funds in their peer group by asset size—which accounted for 34% of overall assets—were about half the cost of the smallest 25% of funds."

### CLAIM 17 — Harvey & Liu (2022), PRIMARY: the Fama-French test is underpowered

### CROSS-CALIBER ISSUES

1. **The 2025 SPIVA primary is unreachable from this environment.** `spglobal.com/spdji/*` and `indexologyblog.com` return Akamai 403 to WebFetch, curl (multiple UA/header sets), r.jina.ai, and three CORS proxies; the Wayback capture of the YE2025 PDF is itself an archived 403; requisitecm.com only mirrors the 2024 editions; the Chrome MCP extension was not connected. **Every year-end-2025 SPIVA number in this dossier (79%, 55%, 41%, 63%, 76%, 82%, 76%, 31%, "fourth-worst in 25 years") is second-hand from InvestmentNews + marketsgroup.org, which agree with each other.** The mid-year 2025 scorecard, by contrast, is a verified primary PDF and carries all the long-horizon tables. Recommend the article anchor long-horizon claims on Mid-Year 2025 (data to 2025-06-30) and treat the 79% as a 1-year, media-relayed figure.

2. **"90% of active funds fail" and "56% of active assets fail" describe the same year.** SPIVA YE2024: 79% of active US equity funds underperformed the S&P Composite 1500 in 2024, ~90% over 10 years, 92.66% over 20 years (fund-count, equal-weighted, dead = failure, costless index). Cremers-Fulkerson-Riley on the same data: 56% of active US equity **assets** underperformed in 2024, 67.18% over 10 years, 55.31% over 20 years (asset-weighted, partial credit for dead funds, investable passive funds as benchmark). Neither is wrong; they answer different questions ("what share of the menu lost?" vs "what share of the money lost?"). Any single-number version of the passive case has to declare which.

3. **SPIVA and Morningstar disagree in magnitude and, for large-cap, in the harsher direction than you'd expect.** SPIVA (mid-2025): 85.98% of large-cap funds underperformed over 10 years ⇒ ~14% "succeeded." Morningstar (Dec-2025): US Large Blend 10-year success rate **8.1%**, Large Growth 3.6%. Morningstar uses the *investable, net-of-fee* passive composite — the fix the IAA paper demands — and gets a **worse** answer for large-cap, because passive funds themselves survive much better (80.4% vs 62.4% over 10 years in US Large Blend) so the benchmark isn't dragged down by mortality. The "costless benchmark" objection therefore does not have a determinate sign.

4. **Fixed income is where the caliber is weakest and the disagreement is widest.** SPIVA: 71.34% of active fixed-income funds underperformed over 10 years (cross-category average). IAA adjustments: 37.09% — a reversal of the conclusion. Ptak: ~a fifth of the improvement in investment-grade intermediate came from *replication error*, and two of the three adjustments individually made results *worse* in that category. Morningstar's Barometer independently gives corporate bond a 52.2% 10-year success rate and intermediate core bond 41.7% — i.e. Morningstar partly *agrees with the IAA side* on bonds while disagreeing on equities. Any claim about active bond management should be stated as contested, with the benchmark-construction problem named (there is no cheap, fully replicating Agg tracker).

5. **The persistence headline is calibrated against nothing.** "Not a single top-quartile fund stayed top-quartile" is a four-consecutive-period requirement; chance predicts 0.25⁴ = 0.39% (≈2 of 512 funds). The informative comparison is SPIVA's own top-half line: 4.21% of all-domestic and 2.42% of large-cap top-half funds stayed top-half for five consecutive years vs 6.25% under randomness. That is a 2–4pp shortfall — real, but an order of magnitude less dramatic than the "zero!" framing implies. Also: the persistence report's own Reports 5/11 show fourth-quartile funds are merged/liquidated at 25% vs 7% for top-quartile funds over five years, which means (a) SPIVA's dead-fund-equals-failure rule is *approximately* right on average, and (b) the IAA's partial-credit fix systematically favours a population that skews toward losers — Ptak's high-yield count (dead outperformers lived 2,844 fund-months, dead underperformers 4,518) is the only quantification of this I found.

6. **The Grossman-Stiglitz prediction fails in the data, and two of the best pro-active papers predict the failure's opposite.** Pástor-Stambaugh-Taylor: +1pp of active-industry share of market cap ⇒ −40bp/yr gross alpha, so the post-2010 *shrinkage* of active share should have raised gross alpha. Cremers-Ferreira-Matos-Starks: average alpha is higher where explicit indexing is higher. Observed instead: SPIVA large-cap underperformance averaged 61% in 2001–09 vs 68.5% in 2016–25, with the worst readings (87, 85, 82, 79) all after 2010; Unterberg reports net four-factor alpha going from −0.72% to −1.82%/yr and **the industry-level relation between passive share and the active-minus-passive spread flipping sign after 2010**. Two ways to reconcile that the article must not conflate: (i) the *level* of passive ownership degrades price discovery (the bubble thesis), vs (ii) the *flow* into passive creates asymmetric price pressure against off-benchmark positions (Unterberg's mechanism, which self-extinguishes when the transition plateaus). Note also CFMS's own interaction term: the *marginal* return to being more active is **lower** where indexing is bigger — the pro-indexing and pro-active readings of that paper come from adjacent sentences.

7. **"Aggregate alpha = −fees" is a 1984–2006 result and cannot be extended forward casually.** FF2010's gross VW three-factor alpha is +0.13% with t = 0.40 on a sample ending September 2006, i.e. before the passive ramp. Unterberg's reported gross VW alpha of −0.66%/yr post-2010 would, if it survives peer review, mean the *pre-fee* identity has broken — active managers as a group now lose to the benchmark before costs, which Sharpe's arithmetic says requires the non-fund side of the market (direct holders, hedge funds, foreign investors, corporates via buybacks) to be winning. That is a much stronger claim than "fees eat the alpha" and needs the primary paper before it is asserted.

8. **Harvey & Liu (JF 2022) undercuts the strongest-sounding academic sentence in the passive case.** "Few funds produce benchmark-adjusted expected returns sufficient to cover their costs" rests on a bootstrap the same journal later showed "has little or no power to detect the truly outperforming funds," failing even against injected alphas above 10%/yr. The defensible version of the academic claim is the *aggregate* one (Claim 16 Table II, and Berk-van Binsbergen's 57% of funds with negative value added), not the cross-sectional "nobody has skill" one.

9. **Berk & van Binsbergen is quoted by both sides from the same table.** "The average mutual fund generated $3.2 million a year" (AUM-weighted mean, t = 5.74) and "57.01% of funds had negative value added, median −$0.24m/yr" are the same row. Under Berk-Green, net-alpha-≈-0 is the *prediction*, so SPIVA-style evidence is not evidence against skill — it is evidence about who captures it. Whether that matters to a retail investor is a normative question, not an empirical one, and the article should say so rather than adjudicate it with data.

10. **Conflict map, both directions.** *Benefits from the "active is hopeless" finding:* S&P Dow Jones Indices (publishes SPIVA, licenses the S&P 500 — the single most-quoted vendor number in this debate), Morningstar (Barometer + fee study + index business), BlackRock, Vanguard, and passive-advocate practitioners (Swedroe/Buckingham). *Benefits from the "SPIVA understates active" finding:* the Investment Adviser Association's Active Managers Council (funded Claim 14), Cremers personally (director at Ariel Investments, academic advisor to Touchstone), and active managers generally — who are also the constituency that benefits from the index-bubble narrative this article is testing. *Cross-cutting oddity worth a line in the piece:* Cremers' 2016 pro-active-management paper was a runner-up for **S&P DJI's own SPIVA award**, and Morningstar's Ptak — defending SPIVA's methodology — is defending the convention his own competing product uses.

11. **Two fee denominators are routinely mixed.** Active US equity: **0.58%** asset-weighted (Morningstar, mutual funds + ETFs, 2025) vs **0.64%** asset-weighted (ICI, equity mutual funds only, 2025) vs **1.00%** equal-weighted (Morningstar). Index: **0.05%** (ICI, index equity mutual funds, asset-weighted) vs **0.14%** (index equity ETFs, asset-weighted) vs **0.45%** (index equity ETFs, simple average) vs **0.10%** (Morningstar, all passive funds, asset-weighted). The gap the article quotes will be anywhere from 44bp to 95bp depending on which pair is chosen.

12. **The 2001–2024 series is digitized from chart data labels, not a table.** Values in Claim 7 were extracted from the PDF's word bounding boxes and matched to year ticks (max offset 1.1pt), so they are S&P's printed integers. Some earlier SPIVA editions report the same years to one decimal and with occasionally different values after benchmark/universe revisions — if the article prints the 2005/2007/2009 "majority beat the index" fact, cite the Mid-Year 2025 Exhibit 1 specifically rather than "SPIVA data."
