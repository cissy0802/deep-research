### CLAIM 1
**Finding:** Price informativeness for S&P 500 nonfinancial firms ROSE substantially at 3–5 year horizons over 1960–2010. Verbatim: "three-year price informativeness is about 60% higher in 2010 than in 1960, and the increase is highly statistically significant. Five-year informativeness is about 80% higher, and also highly significant." And: "the one-year series show only a mild increase."
- **Source:** Bai, Philippon, Savov, "Have financial markets become more informative?", *Journal of Financial Economics* 122(3), 2016, 625–654 (pp. 634–635)
- **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED]
- **Caliber notes:** Numerator/denominator: the measure is NOT an R²; it is `b_{t,h} × σ_t(log M/A)` — the annual cross-sectional forecasting coefficient times the cross-sectional SD of log(market cap / total assets). Regression: `E_{i,t+h}/A_{i,t} = a + b_{t,h} log(M_{i,t}/A_{i,t}) + c_{t,h}(E_{i,t}/A_{i,t}) + d^s 1_{s,i,t} + ε`, run separately each year 1960–2014, one-digit SIC sector dummies, E = EBIT, all quantities inflation-adjusted (GDP deflator, =100 in 2010). Population: S&P 500 **nonfinancial** firms only. Trend p-values from Newey–West with 5 lags. Series plotted end in 2010 because h=5 last estimate is 2009/2011. ### CLAIM 8
**Finding:** CHR: precisely-estimated ZERO effects on price efficiency. Verbatim: "We find that changes in passive investing did not lead to any significant changes in absolute variance ratios over either the 4 or 8 day horizon." Magnitude and power: "the estimated treatment effect from index assignment is a change of -0.017 to the 4-day absolute variance ratio... the standard deviation of the 4-day AVR in the lower-band sample is 0.413, so the estimated effect is less than one-twentieth of one standard deviation. - **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED]
- **Caliber notes:** Three outcome families, all null: (1) AbsVarRatio = |VarRatio(q) − 1| at q=4, 8 days (Lo–MacKinlay bias-corrected), i.e. WEAK-FORM efficiency only; (2) Stambaugh–Yu–Yuan 11-anomaly mispricing score (sample mean 0.501 vs 0.503 for full CRSP universe — representative); MDES = 1/3 SD (lower band) and 1/5 SD (upper band) = 5 and 3 percentile ranks on 1–100; (3) BetaPEAD = coefficient of CAR(t+3, t+63) on SUE scaled so 1 = EPS surprise of 1% of price; sample mean 4.3% (SE 0.6%), SD 17%. ### CROSS-CALIBER ISSUES

1. **"Passive is over 50%" vs every price-informativeness paper's denominator.** Not one paper in this line uses a passive share anywhere near 50%. Sammon: <1% → ~15% of US equity market cap, 1990–2019. CHR: 2% → 11% of Russell 3000 market cap, 2007–2016; treatment intensity 1.5pp. JVZ: 0.44% → 16% of the US stock market, 1993–2021 (ICI + World Bank). Höfler: mean PO ~9%, reaching >10% by 2021; first stage moves it ~1pp. HHL comprehensive measure: active investor fraction 81% → 59%. KNS: passive = 45% **of institutional equity** by 2017. **The "50%+" figure is a share of FUND AUM; the empirical literature's independent variable is a share of MARKET CAPITALIZATION. These are not the same quantity and differ by roughly 3–4×.** Any sentence of the form "now that passive is over half, the studies imply X" is an out-of-sample extrapolation of 3–5×, and CHR, Höfler, and Sammon all explicitly disclaim external validity to higher levels.

2. **BPS is cited for the opposite of what it says twice over.** (a) Its own first draft (Dec 2013, = the NBER WP most people link) concluded informativeness was FLAT; the sign flipped only after inflation adjustment, delisting correction, and adding the 5-year horizon (footnote 7). (b) The "markets got more informative" headline holds for S&P 500 nonfinancials only; BPS's own footnote 2 says informativeness "appears to decline" outside the S&P 500. Citing "Bai-Philippon-Savov (2016)" as debunking the passive-bubble thesis requires suppressing both facts.

3. **BPS vs FMVV is not a disagreement — it is the same measure on different populations.** FMVV use BPS's exact estimator and reproduce BPS's S&P result (+~50% over 1962–2010) while showing the all-firms trend "eviscerated almost all of the information content in prices." But FMVV then undercut the passive story: "the informativeness of stocks currently in the S&P 500 is similar to non-S&P 500 stocks with similar characteristics... differences in asset characteristics, rather than inclusion in S&P 500 per se, is the source of the divergence." So the divergence pattern most often invoked as passive's fingerprint is, in the source paper, attributed to the economics of data value in large growth firms, not to index membership.

4. **CHR vs Sammon is NOT a contradiction, and the pipeline should refuse to frame it as one.** Same design (Sammon explicitly follows Coles et al. for cohorts and controls), overlapping and extended sample (CHR 2007–2016; Sammon's Russell IV 2007–2019). The measures differ in TIMING, not quality: CHR test weak-form variance ratios, anomaly mispricing, and PEAD — all of which are POST-arrival; Sammon tests how much was in prices BEFORE arrival. Both results together say: passive shifts price discovery from anticipatory to reactive while leaving no exploitable post-event drift. Sammon says this himself. Papers claiming CHR "refutes" Sammon, or vice versa, are comparing different objects.

5. **Sammon's own headline is his weakest estimate.** The abstract's "1/4th of its whole sample mean" is the average of the OLS point estimates. His IV estimates — the ones with causal identification, F > 200 — are 2.5–4× larger. He argues this direction of divergence rules out the obvious reverse-causality bias. But his reduced-form coefficients on the instrument interaction "are not always significant," which is the correct place for an adversarial reader to push. Both the conservative and aggressive readings are supportable from the same paper; state which one you are using.

6. **BDFM's "16%" is a caliber error that has propagated for a decade.** The 2014 WP abstract's "an increase of 16% in daily stock volatility" is, in the paper's own text, "a 20 bps increase in daily volatility, which represents 16% of a standard deviation of the dependent variable" — and only for S&P 500 stocks, where one SD of ETF ownership = 1.44% of market cap. Extended to Russell 3000, the effect is "about 5% of a standard deviation." Separately, the RePEc reproduction of the PUBLISHED JF 2018 abstract does not contain "16%" at all and instead headlines a "risk premium of up to 56 basis points monthly," while a search-engine summary of the Wiley page claims the published abstract does contain the 16%. **Unresolved: someone must open the Wiley PDF.** Do not quote "16%" until then, and never quote it as "16% higher volatility."

7. **Höfler et al.'s effect sizes are ~8× extrapolations and their claimed replication of BDFM is not one.** Their instrument moves PO by "12.5% to 12.8% of one standard deviation... roughly... 1 percentage point," yet every headline is scaled to a full one-SD (~7pp) move: bid-ask spreads +0.9 SD, liquidity beta +0.9 SD, tail risk +19pp on a base of 23–26%, and return variance +12.8pp implying "an average volatility of around 31% to about 47% annually." They describe this as essentially replicating BDFM, whose comparable figure was 20bp of daily volatility. These are not the same caliber. Also: the paper is an unrefereed Nov-2024 working paper, and its variance-decomposition private/public split is acknowledged by the authors as hard to interpret. It is the single most quotable set of numbers in the anti-passive case and the least robustly calibrated.

8. **JVZ's "29%" is 7× their own model.** The reduced-form cumulative estimate for top-50 outperformance (29% over 25 years) is contrasted by the authors against their calibration's 0–4%. Also, the size gradient loses significance by the top 200 (t = 1.64), and the non-top-50 idiosyncratic-volatility main effect is insignificant (t = 1.21) — only the top-50 differential is identified. And note JVZ's mechanism is explicitly NOT informational: "information in our model is symmetric." Using JVZ as evidence that passive "breaks price discovery" misreads it; it is evidence that passive distorts the cross-section of prices without any information channel at all.

9. **"How much do active investors adjust?" has three incompatible answers from three good papers, and the difference is definitional.** CHR: fully (price informativeness unchanged, precisely estimated, mid-cap Russell slices, ~1.5pp shocks). HHL: two-thirds (13F 2001–2020, pass-through 0.33, elasticity −11%). KNS: it depends — the passive-share effect is negative but the institutional-size effect is positive and the two roughly cancel in the observed time series, producing "relatively stable price informativeness." These are not adjudicable against each other because CHR measures a local response to a small shock, HHL measures an aggregate demand-curve slope, and KNS measures an equilibrium learning allocation. Note also HHL's own two answers: 11% (comprehensive active-share measure, 81%→59%) vs 6.3% (narrow passive MF+ETF share, +15pp of market cap). Whichever number is quoted, the denominator must be stated.

10. **The "informativeness" label covers at least six non-substitutable objects.** (i) BPS/FMVV: predicted variance of 1/3/5-year future EBIT from prices — an ALLOCATIVE, long-horizon measure; (ii) CHR: 4- and 8-day absolute variance ratios — weak-form random-walk deviation; (iii) BDFM: 15-second intraday variance ratios — microstructure noise; (iv) Sammon: share of earnings news in prices before the release — anticipatory, idiosyncratic; (v) GNZ / Hu-Liang: contemporaneous earnings response and PEAD — reactive; (vi) BSW: market-model R² (Roll non-synchronicity) and GPIN. A finding on (iii) tells you nothing about (i). BSW's Rsq is the most suspect: index addition mechanically raises comovement, and BSW's own Panel B finds no passive-ownership link on Rsq while GPIN does show one.

11. **Directional-interest declaration cuts both ways and neither dominates here.** The papers in this line are overwhelmingly academic, not vendor: BPS, FMVV, CHR, Sammon, BDFM, JVZ, HHL, KNS, GNZ, Buss-Sundaresan, BSW are all [PEER-REVIEWED] with no commercial stake in the answer, and several report results against their own priors (CHR reporting significant volatility/beta effects; Sammon reporting that his own reduced form is weak; JVZ reporting the model/data gap; BPS reversing their own earlier conclusion). The exceptions to watch: Höfler et al. is unrefereed; the ECB box uses a Bloomberg proprietary liquidity model; and CHR's framing explicitly engages Fraser-Jenkins et al. (2016) — a sell-side active-manager report arguing passive is "worse than a centrally-planned economy" — which is [PRACTITIONER] with a direct commercial interest in the bubble narrative.

12. **Gap I could not close: there is no primary aggregate US market-quality series (spreads, depth) covering the alleged 50% crossing.** SEC.gov blocked both WebFetch (403) and direct curl (rate-limit page), so the SEC's own equity-market-structure statistics on quoted spreads, displayed depth and intraday volatility are unverified here. A search summary attributed to an SEC page the claim that "displayed market depth for the median stock has grown nearly 300% in the past eight years" and that S&P 500 bid-ask cost runs ~4.5bp; **neither is verified and neither should be used.** The best verified aggregate primary datum in this line remains CHR's Figure 5 (Russell 3000 4-day variance ratio 1.16 → 1.10 while index ownership went 2% → 11%, 2007–2016) — which points the opposite way from the micro-level Höfler spread result, and which stops a decade before the period the article is actually about.
