### CLAIM 6
**FIVE candidate explanations (not four) and the authors' verdict.** Verbatim from the conclusion: "In this paper, we consider five explanations why this happened. To sum up, our assessment is that the declining index effect is driven by primarily two factors: an increase in migrations over time from the S&P MidCap Index, and an overall increase in the market's ability to provide liquidity to index changes. We cannot rule out that a third factor, increased predictability of index changes, played some role. Overall then, when demand shocks become regular and repeated, competitive markets adapt over time to minimize price impact, in the spirit of Lo's (2004) adaptive markets hypothesis."
The five, with their stated dispositions:
1. **Changing composition of additions/deletions** — "We quickly rule out that the effects we document are driven by changes in the characteristics of additions and deletions"; composition "cannot fully account" for the change.
2. **Market-wide liquidity improvement** — "value-weighted average bid-ask spreads have fallen by a factor of about 10× between the early 1990s and the late 2010s"; but "not enough to fully explain the change… and moreover the timing of the decline in trading costs predates the disappearance of the index announcement effect."
3. **Migrations (net demand shock smaller than it looks)** — "From the 1990s to the present day, migrations went from about 40% of additions to over 80%." Returns: "In the mid-1990s, migration and non-migration additions had average returns of 6.7% and 6.4%, respectively. By the late 2010s, however, direct additions had returns of 2.2%, while migrations had returns of −2.3%." **ACCEPTED as a primary driver.**
4. **Predictability / front-running by arbitrageurs** — "We find mixed evidence to support this hypothesis." **Cannot rule out.**
5. **Better liquidity provision to index events** — "in the 1990s, in the month before and after the index change, 15% of total volume occurred on the effective date, while in the 2010s that number increased to almost 30%"; and "although index trackers now buy about 7-8% upon index addition, total institutional ownership barely moves around index changes. We interpret this as professional active investors providing liquidity to passive buyers and sellers." **ACCEPTED as a primary driver.**
- Source: Greenwood & Sammon, WP 30748, pp. 3–5 and §3 Conclusion
- Tier: [PEER-REVIEWED]
- Caliber notes: Explanation 5 is the key point for the article's thesis: *active managers are still there, and they are the counterparty.* Explanation 3 means part of the "disappearance" is measurement (offsetting MidCap selling), not pure market efficiency.
- URL: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30748/w30748.pdf

### CLAIM 10

### CLAIM 20

### CROSS-CALIBER ISSUES

**1. Greenwood & Sammon's own headline numbers moved between drafts and publication — and the widely-circulated "0.3%" appears to be fabricated.** The 1990s addition effect is **7.6%** (NBER WP, Dec 2022), **7.3%** (revised June 2023 body text), and **7.4%** (published JF 2025 abstract). The recent-decade addition effect is **0.8%** (both WP versions) and **"less than 1%"** (JF abstract). The deletion effect is **−0.6% / −16.6%** (Dec 2022), **−0.6% / −16.1%** (June 2023), and **"an average return of only 0.1%"** in the published JF abstract — a *sign flip* on the recent-decade deletion number. Separately, a web search summarizer returned "fallen from an average of 7.4% in the 1990s to **0.3%** over the past decade" and "only **0.1%**" for deletions; the 0.3% figure appears **nowhere** in the Crossref-deposited published abstract, which says "less than 1%." **Recommendation: cite 7.4% → "less than 1%" (JF 2025, Crossref-verified) for the headline, and the WP's 3.4/7.6/5.2/0.8 and −4.6/−16.6/−12.3/−0.6 series for the decade table, labelling it as the working-paper version.** Do not use 0.3%.

**2. "The index effect disappeared" is an average over a population that changed shape.** Over 80% of recent S&P 500 additions are MidCap **migrations**, where index-fund buying is mechanically offset by MidCap index-fund selling. Direct (non-migration) S&P 500 additions still earned **5.34% on average 2010–2020**; Russell 1000 direct additions **5.4% in the 2010s**; S&P MidCap additions **4.96%**; S&P SmallCap additions ~**6%, flat for 30 years**. The naive reading — "index demand no longer moves prices" — is false. The correct reading is narrower: *the S&P 500's average add/delete no longer moves prices, largely because the net demand shock is now netted out inside S&P's own index family, and because the market got much better at supplying liquidity to a predictable, pre-announced trade.*

**3. Tesla is measured three incompatible ways.** Greenwood-Sammon: **+5.2% announcement / +4.5% implementation**, market-adjusted, 3-day windows. Research Affiliates: **+57%**, raw, Nov 17 → Dec 18 close (22 trading days). Both are correct; they measure different objects. Greenwood-Sammon themselves flag that Tesla's **32-day announcement-to-effective gap** (vs a typical <10 days) means the long-window return is contaminated by non-index news. Anyone quoting "Tesla ran up 57% because of index buying" is asserting a counterfactual the data does not identify. Also note the required-purchase figure is **$78 billion** in the fetched Research Affiliates PDF but is reported elsewhere as **$94 billion** attributed to the same authors — a ~20% discrepancy in the same firm's own number, probably reflecting different reference dates (announcement-date vs rebalance-date valuation) but unreconciled.

**4. XIV's loss: 84% or 96%?** BIS (fetched, primary): "The value of one of the inverse volatility ETPs, XIV, fell 84% and the product was subsequently terminated." Practitioner and media accounts routinely say "~96%" or "$108 to $4." These are different measurement points: the 84% is the move BIS attributes to Feb 5; the ~96% figures reference the indicative value in after-hours trading and/or the eventual acceleration/termination value. **Use BIS's 84% with its date attached, or state both with their referents.**

**5. August 24, 2015 halt counts come in at least two calibers, from parties with opposite interests.** SEC (fetched, primary): **1,278 LULD halts total; 1,058 in 327 ETPs; 220 in 144 non-ETPs; 80% of ETPs had zero halts.** Circulating alternative: "1,237 individual circuit breaker trading halts… 1,046 were ETPs or 85%" — this figure traces to **BlackRock's comment letter** ("The ETF Stress Test of August 24, 2015"), i.e. the largest ETF issuer in the world, whose framing interest is to attribute the event to market structure rather than to ETFs. Likewise the vivid "IVV fell as much as 26%, some 20 percentage points below fair value" and "the spread between the high and low trading prices for 46 iShares ETPs averaged 31%" are **BlackRock's own numbers about BlackRock's own products**. Prefer the SEC caliber; cite BlackRock's only with the COI stated. The SEC's denominators are also the ones that defuse the narrative: 80% of ETPs never halted, and the halt *rate* was identical (20%) for the fifty largest ETPs and for the other 1,491.

**6. "Passive share" appears with at least three incompatible denominators in this literature.** Greenwood-Sammon's tracker estimate is **~7% of S&P 500 market capitalization** (their lower bound), with sell-side (UBS) at **~13%** of market cap. BIS 2018 used **20% of global fund AUM**. The FCA/Bristol report uses **33% of UK-managed AUM**. The "passive has crossed 50%" headline is share of *US fund and ETF assets*. Chinco & Sammon (JFE 2024) argue the true passive ownership share is "double what you think it is." **The article's central framing claim and its event-study evidence live on different denominators, and any sentence that puts "50% passive" next to "7-8% index-tracker buying on addition" without flagging the switch is misleading.**

**7. The two halves of the official consensus contradict each other and nobody has reconciled them.** ECB (Nov 2024), BIS (2018), Höfler et al. (2025) all find passive ownership *raises* comovement / lowers stock-level market quality. Chen-Singal-Whitelaw (2016), Greenwood-Sammon (2025), and S&P DJI (2021) all find the *mechanical inclusion effect on returns and betas shrank* precisely as passive grew. The Fed's own survey (Anadu et al.) calls the comovement evidence "mixed," and the FCA-commissioned 2025 survey endorses both findings side by side without reconciling them. **The likeliest reconciliation — worth flagging to the writing agent as a hypothesis, not a finding — is that the two literatures measure different horizons: event-window price impact (which arbitrage capital has learned to absorb, and which is now front-run and pre-announced) versus persistent ownership-structure effects on noise, spreads, and correlation (which no arbitrageur is paid to undo).** Greenwood-Sammon's explanation 5 supports exactly this: index trackers buy 7–8% of an added stock, "total institutional ownership barely moves," so active investors are absorbing the flow — the *transaction* is intermediated away, but the *ownership composition* still changes permanently.

**8. Every fragility episode commonly attributed to "passive" was attributed by its official post-mortem to something else.** May 6 2010 → an automated, price-insensitive sell algorithm in E-Mini futures plus ETF market-maker withdrawal under valuation uncertainty (SEC/CFTC). Aug 24 2015 → NYSE's manual opening, stale S&P DJI index calculation using Aug 21 closes, an untriggered market-wide circuit breaker, and LULD band-doubling expiry at 9:45 (SEC). Feb 5 2018 → daily rebalancing of **leveraged and inverse volatility ETPs** holding **~$4bn**, a category the SEC explicitly excludes from "ETPs" in its own analysis (BIS). March 2020 → dealer balance-sheet withdrawal, stale bond NAVs, and MMLF-induced portfolio rotation — with ETF prices leading NAVs, i.e. ETFs doing price discovery (BIS). **Not one of these post-mortems identifies index-fund inflows or outflows as a cause.** The honest version of the fragility case rests on ETF *secondary-market arbitrage* as a transmission channel (BIS's own third flagged issue) and on stock-level ownership effects (Höfler et al.), not on episode narratives.
