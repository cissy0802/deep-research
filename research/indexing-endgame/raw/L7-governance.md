### CROSS-CALIBER ISSUES

1. **"34%" is misattributed in the brief and in general circulation.** Bebchuk & Hirst project **34.3% of votes in ten years (2028)** and **40.8% in twenty years (2038)**. Several search summarizers returned "40% within two decades" and the task brief said "34% within two decades." Any article using "34% in two decades" is wrong by a decade. Separately, the 2022 update quietly revised the *ownership* path down to 27.6% (2028) / 33.4% (2038).

2. **Ownership vs. votes-cast are routinely conflated.** 20.5% (ownership, mean, 2017), 21.9% (ownership, median, 2021), 18.7% (ownership, value-weighted, 2018, S&C), 24.9%–27.6% (votes cast, 2021), 25.4% (votes cast, mean, 2018) are five different statistics. The votes-cast series is always ~4–6pp higher and depends entirely on the assumption that non-Big-Three turnout stays at ~73%. If retail turnout rises (e.g., via broker-vote reform or pass-through voting), the votes-cast projection collapses while ownership is unchanged.

3. **Bebchuk & Hirst (2022) reports two irreconcilable numbers for the same quantity.** Text/abstract: Big Three held "24.9% of the votes cast" in 2021. Same paper, Section I.A and Table 2: "27.6%" (text) / 27.5% (table total = 9.8+12.0+5.7 sums of rounded medians). Neither is flagged as different from the other. An article should pick one, state the caliber (mean vs sum-of-medians), and note the spread.

4. **The airline price effect has three different headline numbers from the same authors.** 3–7% (panel regression), 10–12% (BlackRock-BGI IV), ~0.5% (the direct effect of the BGI merger itself), and 3–12% in their own CPI trade-press summary. The published JF abstract contains **no** price number at all. Commentary quoting "up to 10-12%" is quoting the IV counterfactual against total-separate-ownership, a very strong counterfactual.

5. **The common-ownership literature is not settled, and the two "review" papers each have an author with skin in the game.** Gerardi/Lowry/Schenone (Atlanta Fed 2023) says "relatively little evidence that common ownership causes lower competition" — Gerardi and Schenone wrote the JF replication. Azar & Vives (ECGI 827/2022) says effects are confirmed — Azar wrote the original. The ASt "Refutation" (2022) and the DGS "Surrebuttal" (Dec 2022) are both unpublished SSRN papers; the exchange has no referee-adjudicated endpoint as of 2026-08. The honest statement is: **the airline result did not replicate under placebo tests, and the dispute over why has never been resolved in a refereed venue.**

6. **Backus/Conlon/Sinkinson decompose the measure itself, which is a deeper problem than the airline dispute.** If "between a third and a half of the variation in the profit weight measure comes not from overlapping ownership... but instead from relative investor concentration," then MHHI-based results across *all* industries — not just airlines — are measuring something partly other than what their authors say.

7. **"88% / 90% of S&P 500 companies" is a rank statistic from law firms, using December 2018 data, still quoted as current in 2026.** It means "largest single holder," not "controlling holder," and the same sources put the aggregate stake at 18.7–20% of value. Combining "88% of companies" with "over 50% passive share" produces the "they own everything" impression that neither number supports.

8. **Pass-through voting: eligible vs. enrolled vs. actually voted.** Vanguard's "$3.6 trillion eligible / 22 million eligible investors" (Feb 2026) sits against **82,000 investors who actually participated** in 2025 (~0.8% of the then-eligible ~10 million). BlackRock's $784bn enrolled is 24% of eligible index equity but 11% of total public equity AUM. Both firms and both political camps have reasons to quote the eligible number. Innisfree's independent practitioner read is that pass-through voting "has not meaningfully impacted stewardship voting." Any claim that pass-through voting has defused the Giant Three problem needs the *participation* denominator, not the eligibility one.

9. **Stewardship staffing is measured against unstable denominators.** Bebchuk & Hirst's person-days depend entirely on which allocation scenario you pick — 0.25 to 3.97 person-days for the *same* firms. Their most-quoted line ("less than 4 person-days per $1bn position") is the *most generous* scenario, not the average. Meanwhile BlackRock's team has grown 45 → 60+/65+ while its public equity AUM grew far faster, so the ratio has probably worsened even as the absolute number rose — no source computes this cleanly.

10. **Tesla's "$78bn vs $94bn vs $147bn vs $154bn" is one source contradicting itself in a single endnote,** and the widely circulated "$220 billion" traces to nothing primary. The $78bn = $4.6tn × 1.69% arithmetic is checkable; everything above it includes separately managed accounts and front-running liquidity providers, which is a different population.

11. **The two conflicting official postures on common ownership sit in the same document.** The May 2025 FTC/DOJ Statement of Interest simultaneously reaffirms the 2017 US OECD Submission ("compelling evidence" of anticompetitive common-ownership effects is lacking; across-the-board limits risk "unintended real-world costs... by making it more difficult to diversify risk") **and** argues the Clayton §7 claim against the Big Three should proceed. Anyone citing "the US government now believes common ownership is anticompetitive" is over-reading; anyone citing "the agencies exonerated index funds" is under-reading.

12. **The single biggest 2026 update runs *against* the concentration thesis and is barely covered.** All three managers now vote through multiple independent internal stewardship teams, deliberately structured so each team is "less likely to control more than 5% of an issuer's outstanding shares." Ownership concentration is unchanged; the *unified bloc* premise underlying both Bebchuk-Hirst and the antitrust theories is now partly obsolete by construction. But no one has yet published evidence on whether the split teams actually vote differently — so this is a change in legal form whose substantive effect is unmeasured, and the primary source for it is a proxy solicitor with a commercial interest in complexity.

13. **The index-provider regulatory gap is now a settled non-event, not a pending question.** The SEC's June 2022 request for comment (IA-6050) never became a proposed rule, was not among the 14 proposals withdrawn in June 2025 (it never reached proposal stage), and does not appear on the Spring 2026 Atkins agenda. Index providers remain outside the US Advisers Act while EU/UK benchmark administrators are registered under the EU Benchmarks Regulation. An article should say "the SEC asked the question in 2022 and dropped it," not "the SEC is considering regulating index providers."

14. **SSGA's disappearance below the 5% threshold.** At Apple (2026 proxy) State Street does not appear in the 5%-holder table at all, and BlackRock's figure is from December 2023. Any "current Big Three stake" built from proxy statements is therefore biased *downward* and *stale* in different, non-uniform ways across companies; 13F-based academic series (FactSet/CRSP) and proxy-based practitioner series are not comparable.

15. **Geode is the missing fourth leg.** Fidelity's index funds are sub-advised by Geode Capital Management (~$1.6tn 13F portfolio, Mar 31 2026), which files separately and is excluded from every "Big Three" aggregate. If the concern is "how concentrated is index-fund voting power," the Big Three framing systematically undercounts; if the concern is "are the Big Three specifically a coordinating bloc," adding Geode weakens the coordination story. The choice of frame is doing work that no source acknowledges.
