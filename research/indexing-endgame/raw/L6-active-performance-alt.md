### CLAIM 25 — Pedersen (2018): even the "passive is free" arithmetic is wrong, because index funds must trade
- **Source:** Lasse Heje Pedersen, "Sharpening the Arithmetic of Active Management," *Financial Analysts Journal* 74(1), 2018, pp. 21–36; SSRN #2849071.
- **Tier:** [PEER-REVIEWED] — but Pedersen is a principal at AQR, an active manager. **Caveat: not fetched primary this session; figures via search results, [UNCONFIRMED WORDING].**
- **Caliber notes:** Argument: Sharpe's (1991) "the average actively managed dollar must equal the average passively managed dollar before costs" implicitly assumes the market portfolio never changes. ### CROSS-CALIBER ISSUES

1. **The single most slippery number in this entire debate: "X% of active funds underperform" is a FUND COUNT, not a dollar.** SPIVA's own Report 3 vs Report 4 (CLAIM 5, primary) shows that at mid-2025, asset-weighted All Large-Cap active funds returned 15.48% over 1 year vs the S&P 500's 15.16% and 20.00% vs 19.71% over 3 years — i.e. **the average dollar in active large-cap BEAT the index** in exactly the horizons where SPIVA reports 72.61% and 64.87% "underperformance." Over 10 and 20 years the dollar-weighted shortfall is ~1.2pp/yr, roughly the fee gap, versus an 86–91% "failure rate." Morningstar independently confirms the direction (CLAIM 12: the average active dollar beat the average active fund in 17 of 20 categories; 8 of 20 categories show active winning on a 10-year asset-weighted basis). **Any article using "79%" or "92%" without saying "of funds, counted equally, with dead funds scored as failures" is mis-calibrating by an order of magnitude.**

2. **Survivorship convention does enormous unstated work at long horizons.** Both SPIVA and Morningstar score a fund that closed as an automatic underperformer. SPIVA's own Report 2 (CLAIM 6, primary): only 34.70% of large-cap funds alive 20 years ago still exist; Morningstar's Exhibit 5: 36.5% 20-year active survivorship vs 59.1% for passive. Neither vendor discloses the decomposition of "underperformed" into "closed" vs "survived and lost." Cremers/Fulkerson/Riley claim reversing this one convention (plus asset-weighting plus investable benchmarks) moves domestic equity from 15% of funds outperforming to 43% of assets outperforming over 5 years to 2024 (CLAIM 16). Nobody has reconciled the two.

3. **The Cremers/Fulkerson/Riley numbers do not agree with themselves across outlets.** Yahoo/GlobeNewswire: "43% of assets outperformed" (domestic equity, 5 yrs to 2024) vs SPIVA's "15% of funds outperformed." A search summary of the same paper: "only 56% of assets invested in active U.S. equity funds in 2024 underperformed." WealthManagement.com: "55% of assets underperformed versus SPIVA's 92% of funds." These are different horizons (1-yr 2024 vs 5-yr vs 10-yr) reported without labels. **The SSRN PDF must be fetched and the horizon/population pinned before any of these is used.** Also note the funder: the Investment Adviser Association's Active Managers Council — the trade body whose members are the funds being scored. Conflict is symmetric with S&P DJI's, not smaller.

4. **The Grossman-Stiglitz prediction fails in the raw time series, but the time series cannot identify anything.** SPIVA's own annual chart (CLAIM 7, primary): three sub-50% years in 2001–2024, all of them 2005/2007/2009, followed by **16 straight years (2010–2025) of majority large-cap underperformance** as passive share rose past 50%. That is the opposite of "fewer informed traders → easier alpha." BUT: (a) Morningstar's Exhibit 3 shows US Large Blend success at **58.5% in Dec 2022** — one bear market restored a majority instantly, so regime dominates trend; (b) Pástor-Stambaugh-Taylor (CLAIM 24) predicts the same decline from industry *scale*, which is confounded with passive share; (c) Unterberg (CLAIM 21) argues the causality runs through flow-induced price pressure, not through information economics at all. **Three explanations, one time series, no identification. The honest statement is that the naive G-S prediction is not visible in the data, not that it has been tested and rejected.**

5. **SPIVA's own commentary contradicts the "opportunity set" defense of active.** The Mid-Year 2025 scorecard (primary, verbatim) attributes H1's relatively good 54% to *"higher dispersion"* creating *"greater potential opportunities for stock pickers to shine."* By year-end, with dispersion still above average (per the blocked Indexology post), the number had blown out to 79%. Whatever explains 2025, it is not a shortage of cross-sectional opportunity.

6. **"Passive fees are 0.10%" is not "passive costs 0.10%."** Pedersen (CLAIM 25) shows index funds must trade on reconstitution and share issuance (~7.6%/yr US equity turnover), so the arithmetic-of-active-management case and the fee-savings case both overstate the passive advantage by an unmeasured amount. Conversely, SPIVA's benchmark is a non-investable INDEX (zero cost), while Morningstar's is an investable passive composite (net of fees) — which is one reason Morningstar's "success rates" (32.1% for US Large Blend in 2025) look better for active than SPIVA's implied 21% (100−79%). **These two headline numbers are not comparable and are routinely quoted side by side as if they were.**

7. **The fee-savings welfare case is partly a distribution story, not a passive story.** ICI (primary, verbatim): the decline in average mutual fund expense ratios *"primarily reflects a shift toward no-load funds"* — 92% of gross sales in 2025 vs 46% in 2000. ICI also attributes index funds' low cost partly to fund SIZE ($15.0bn average index equity fund vs $2.8bn active) and to their concentration in cheap-to-manage US large-cap blend — i.e. economies of scale and asset mix, not passivity per se. And Morningstar's headline "$6.8 billion saved" is a **one-year 2bp delta**, not cumulative savings from indexation; the cumulative figure is not published by either vendor and must not be invented.

8. **Publication-date conflict on SPIVA Year-End 2025.** TKer dates its coverage March 3, 2026 (SPIVA's historical release month); MarketsGroup states the report was "published May 19, 2026" (which is the Persistence Scorecard's usual month, and The Idea Farm dates the Persistence Scorecard YE2025 to May 2026). Likely MarketsGroup conflated the two. Resolve before citing a date.
