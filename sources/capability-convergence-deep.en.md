# Will AI Capabilities Eventually Converge? Leaderboard Compression, Ceiling Effects, and Divergence on Long Tasks (Deep Dive)

> Methodology note: The load-bearing claims in this piece were grouped into 27 sets. Each set was assigned 3 independent verification agents (81 votes in total), instructed to "try hard to refute": go back verbatim to papers, official reports and the original text of official leaderboards; for every number the research phase computed itself from raw data (Arena historical snapshots, Epoch benchmark data and capabilities index, METR raw run records, τ²-bench submission files, the ARC Prize leaderboard JSON), the verifiers re-downloaded the data and recomputed. Result: of the 81 votes, 80 ruled "needs correction," 1 ruled "holds as written," and 0 ruled "overturned"; none of the 27 sets passed with three votes of "holds as written," and about 210 phrasings in the original draft were ruled dead or barred from use in their original form. For 4 pieces of empirical evidence where "a single source carries a key conclusion," an additional counter-evidence search seat and a methodology audit seat were added (8 seats in total): all 4 were downgraded, two of them ruled "direction contested" by the counter-evidence seat, and none was rejected. Evidence grades: 【Multi-source confirmed】 ≥2 independent sources in the same direction; 【Single-source verified】 traceable to a primary document or raw data; 【Direction contested】 independent sources conflict; 【Vendor-reported】 self-reported by an interested party, usable for direction but not load-bearing for magnitude; 【Unverified】. Many of the numbers in this piece were computed from public raw data; every such number is marked as own calculation. All figures are as of October 12, 2026; the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI) is refit in full every time its data is updated, and this piece cites the version downloaded in October 2026.

## 0. First, Give "the Gap" a Testable Definition

"The top models are getting squeezed closer and closer together," "open and closed models are only a few percentage points apart," "the US–China gap has effectively closed"—these three statements were quoted again and again in 2025–2026. The opposing claim is just as popular: "on long tasks the frontier has widened the gap; winner takes all." Both narratives are about "the gap," but few people say clearly what the gap means.

The gap has at least three testable definitions, and they can point in opposite directions:

- **Score difference**: on the same ruler at the same point in time, the difference between the leader's and the follower's scores (with confidence intervals).
- **Months to catch up**: how many months later the follower reaches a given level the leader attained.
- **Ratio**: the multiplicative relationship between the two at the same point in time, such as the ratio of task lengths each can complete independently, or the ratio of success rates.

There is a simple conversion among them. If some frontier capability (say, the length of tasks a model can complete independently) doubles every D months, then lagging by L months equals lagging by a factor of R = 2^(L/D). Taking the doubling time METR measured since 2023, D≈4.23 months (128.7 days, 95% interval about 3.4–5.2 months), lagging by 4 months is roughly equivalent to lagging by a factor of 1.93. 【Single-source verified: own calculation, assuming exponential growth with a constant doubling time】

This conversion has a direct corollary: **if the months-to-catch-up stays the same while the frontier accelerates, the ratio widens.** At the same 4-month lag, the difference is only 1.49× when the frontier doubles every 7 months, but 2.21× when it doubles every 3.5 months. So "followers have consistently trailed by about half a year" and "the gap is widening" can both be true; "the score difference keeps shrinking" and "the ratio keeps growing" can also both be true. Each section below will first make clear which kind of gap it is using.

Another pair of concepts needs to be kept apart: **user-perceived saturation** (on everyday tasks you cannot tell which model is stronger) and **capability saturation** (there really is no difference). The former is a measurement problem; only the latter is a capability problem.

## 1. How Ceilings Flatten the Gap

**Benchmark saturation is the norm, and it is getting faster.** Ott et al. (Nature Communications 2022, data roughly through 2021, covering only computer vision and natural language processing) compiled 3,765 benchmarks, about half of which had results at three or more time points suitable for trend analysis; the authors wrote: "a large fraction of benchmarks quickly trended towards near-saturation". In their introduction they also noted that on near-saturated benchmarks "statistical significance of differences in model performance is more difficult to achieve"—a motivating argument with citations, not an empirical result of that paper. 【Single-source verified】 In the large-model era, Akhtar, Reuel et al. (arXiv 2602.16763, ICML 2026) examined 60 text-based language model benchmarks; by the authors' own saturation index (Sindex≥0.7, meaning top models can no longer be reliably distinguished statistically, which is not the same as reaching human level), 29 (48%) showed high or very high saturation, and the share rose with benchmark age. 【Single-source verified】

**Why saturation creates the illusion of "convergence."** When constructing its cross-benchmark capabilities index, Epoch wrote down a methodological premise: "We can only compare models when they're in the middle — not too good and not too bad." 【Single-source verified】 A simple bit of arithmetic shows why. Suppose two models differ by a fixed capability gap on the same logistic (item response theory) scale, and set that gap so that the weaker model is at 50% and the stronger at 70%—a 20-percentage-point score difference. With the same capability gap, when the weaker model's score is already 90%, 95% or 99%, the score difference is only 5.45, 2.79 or 0.57 percentage points, a compression of about 35× (about 15× if the stronger model's score is used as the baseline). 【Single-source verified: own calculation; the 20 percentage points is a set value, not a measurement】 In other words, on an exam that is nearly maxed out, "only a fraction of a point apart" and "equally capable" are not mathematically the same thing.

**The percentages on leaderboards are themselves problematic.** The Stanford AI Index 2025 wrote that the gap between the No. 1 and No. 10 models on Chatbot Arena shrank from 11.9% (2023 data cited in the previous year's report) to 5.4% in early 2025, and the gap between the top two from 4.9% in 2023 to 0.7% in 2024; the gap between the best closed-weight and best open-weight models shrank from 8.04% in January 2024 to 1.70% in February 2025. 【Single-source verified】 The denominator of these percentages is the absolute Arena score, and Arena scores as a whole can be shifted arbitrarily—top scores rose from about 1250 to about 1385, so even with an unchanged score difference, the percentage would mechanically shrink by about a tenth. Converted to score differences, 5.4% is about 70–75 Arena points, 0.7% about 10 points, and 1.70% is 23 points (1,385 vs. 1,362). On Arena, a score difference means a single-match win rate: 10 points is about 51.4%, 30 points about 54.3%, 49 points about 57.0%, and 100 points about 64.0%. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】

The AI Index 2026 changed its framing: the open-source gap "reopened in 2025"; in March 2026 the best closed model, Claude Opus 4.6 (1,503), led the best open-weight model, GLM-5 (1,454), by 49 points; between the US and China, Claude Opus 4.6 led Dola-Seed-2.0 Preview (1,464) by 39 points, on which basis the report treats the US–China gap as essentially gone. The same 49 points is written as 3.3% on the report's highlights page and 3.4% in the body text, using different denominators. More importantly, the series in the two editions do not connect: the 2025 edition measured the US–China gap in benchmark percentage points (MMLU, MMMU, MATH and HumanEval shrank from 17.5/13.5/24.3/31.6 percentage points at the end of 2023 to 0.3/8.1/1.6/3.7 at the end of 2024), while the 2026 edition switched to Arena scores; and the 2026 edition recomputed the February 2025 US–China Arena gap as 0.4%, where the 2025 edition had written 1.7% at the time. In May 2025 Arena made style control the default, and historical scores were refit. 【Single-source verified】 The AI Index 2026 highlights page also contains the sentence "Six of the top ten models on the Arena Leaderboard are now closed", which does not match Arena's raw data (counted by model, the top ten in the March snapshot were all closed); this piece does not use it.

**Saturation is also accelerating.** Using Epoch data with the random-guessing baseline subtracted, and counting to the release date of the first model to reach 80%: GPQA Diamond (released November 2023) took about 19 months; SWE-bench Verified (August 2024) about 20 months by Epoch's independent re-testing, or about 15 months by vendor self-reports; ARC-AGI-2 (March 2025) about 11 months; Terminal-Bench 2.0 (November 2025) was first pushed past 80% about 3 months later, by a "model + third-party agent harness" combination. Humanity's Last Exam (January 2025) was not passed at the halfway mark until September 2026, when GPT-6 Astra scored 54.8% raw. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】 The AI Index 2026 summed it up as "Evaluations intended to be challenging for years are saturated in months".

## 2. Preference Leaderboards: The Points Are Compressing, but the Rankings Have Decoupled

**The top is indeed compressed, but not monotonically.** Taking the last snapshot of each month from Arena's public historical snapshots (the Hugging Face dataset lmarena-ai/leaderboard-dataset, style-controlled overall text leaderboard) and computing ourselves: the score difference between No. 1 and No. 10 was 75.3 on December 30, 2024, 67.3 in June 2025, 41.7 in December 2025, 18.7 on August 27, 2026, and back up to 31.4 on October 8, 2026. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】 This series is not monotonic: it dipped as low as 16.9 on October 17, 2025, and reached 12.8 in September 2026; intra-month fluctuations are also large. A steadier statement is that the score difference fell from about 50–85 between late 2024 and mid-2025 to about 17–30 in mid-2026. Different reasoning tiers from the same lab crowd into the top ten (on October 8, 2026, 7 of the top ten were Anthropic variants); deduplicated by lab, the difference between the 1st and 5th labs was 75.3 (December 2024) → 42.7 (December 2025) → 23.6 (August 2026) → 40.3 (October 8, 2026). Arena changed its methodology several times during this period: in May 2025 style control became the default and scores shifted as a whole; in July 2025 frequency-based reweighting was introduced (which Arena acknowledges widens confidence intervals); in May 2026 "direct battle" votes were counted. The drop in the score difference from 67 to 28 in July–September 2025 straddles exactly the launch of reweighting, so the multi-year decline can only serve as directional evidence, not as a precise magnitude.

The October rebound comes from the newly listed gemini-4-argon-high: 1525.4 (95% interval 1516.5–1534.2), with only 4,892 votes, leading the No. 2 model, claude-opus-5.5-high (1507.1), by 18.3 points; the two confidence intervals are separated by just over one point. New models have few votes, and their scores often fall back after listing.

**The closed vs. open Arena gap: no trend, only definitions.** On the style-controlled leaderboard, the best closed model minus the best open model (open defined as a non-empty license that is not Proprietary, including non-commercial licenses such as Kimi K3's) had annual minimum/median/maximum values of: 2024 (from August 28) 10.7/48.8/75.9, 2025 2.7/43.0/70.4, 2026 (through October 8) 17.7/35.1/57.7. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】 An open model has never reached first place on the style-controlled leaderboard; on the raw leaderboard without style control, since 2024 only DeepSeek-R1 briefly overtook, in 11 snapshots from January 24 to February 27, 2025. The gap on October 8, 2026 depends on the vote threshold: with no threshold it is 37.3; looking only at closed models with more than ten thousand votes it is 16.3 (claude-opus-4-6-high vs. kimi-k3-max), and the values of 18.7, 17.5 and 16.3 under the same threshold in August–October all fall within the confidence intervals and do not constitute a narrowing trend. Arena's official X post on May 7, 2026 summarized it as: "The remaining gap is small in points, but still large in rank"—the lead at the time was about 30 points, equivalent to the distance between No. 1 and about No. 18. 【Single-source verified, interested party's account】

In September 2026 media reported that "the gap between closed and open models widened to 29 Elo, the largest in nearly two years," and said the gap had "reached zero" in January 2025. The 29 comes from raw-leaderboard snapshots from August 27 to September 1 (28.7), while the style-controlled leaderboard over the same period was between 17.7 and 20.9; the raw leaderboard showed 59.6 and 51.1 in 2026, so "the largest in nearly two years" does not hold; in January 2025 R1 overtook on the raw leaderboard, and the minimum difference on the style-controlled leaderboard was 2.7, so under neither definition did the gap "reach zero." Only by picking the R1 month as the starting point does one get "widened." 【Single-source verified: own calculation; the media's original claims ruled dead】

**The biases of the leaderboard itself are of the same order of magnitude as these score differences.** Singh et al., "The Leaderboard Illusion" (arXiv 2504.20879 v2), documented that Meta privately tested 27 text model variants before the Llama-4 release; estimated by share of battles from January 2024 to April 2025, Google and OpenAI received about 19.2% and 20.4% of Arena data respectively, while 83 open-weight models together received about 29.7%. The paper is inconsistent about how much "privately testing many variants and publishing only the best" can inflate scores: v1 says "about +100 with 10 variants," the v2 body and appendix change this to "about +50 with 20 variants," and the v2 key-findings summary still keeps the old number. The paper's authors are mainly from Cohere—a model company that competes on Arena. Arena's response (Arena being the party criticized) said that, by its simulation, after 50 tests and 3,000 votes the gain from private testing is about +11 Elo and returns to zero as new data comes in; the response also acknowledged that "The best models, regardless of provider, are upsampled". 【Multi-source confirmed (paper); Arena's +11 is Vendor-reported】 Whether one takes +11 or +50, biases of this kind are of the same order of magnitude as the current 10–30 point differences at the top.

**The key finding: in the frontier range, preference rankings and capability rankings are essentially uncorrelated.** Matching the October 8, 2026 Arena style-controlled leaderboard against Epoch ECI (October 2026 version) by model name yields 111 models. The Spearman correlation across the full sample is 0.95, but part of that comes from the time span between models of different generations—looking only at models released in 2026, it is about 0.72. Looking only at the ECI top 30, it drops to 0.19, and for the top 15 it is about 0 (point estimate −0.03, 95% interval about −0.6 to 0.5). 【Single-source verified: own calculation; downgraded to a weak conclusion by the Round 3 two-seat audit】 This is not merely "a narrower sample range naturally lowers the correlation": on the same 15 models, the median pairwise rank correlation among the various capability benchmarks is still 0.69, consistent with the cross-category median of 0.68 published by Epoch; removing style control makes the decoupling even more pronounced.

But Arena has not lost its discriminating power. Among the top 15, 39 of the 105 model pairs have non-overlapping confidence intervals; it is simply ranking by a different standard. Two examples: ECI places Claude Opus 5.5 (167.3) 12 points above Opus 4.6 (155.2), with non-overlapping 90% intervals, while on Arena opus-5.5-high and opus-4-6-high are only 2.7 points apart, effectively tied; ECI places GPT-6.1 Sol 8.6 points above Kimi K3, while on Arena the two differ by 4.4 points, likewise tied. So the conclusion is: **in the frontier range, preference leaderboards can no longer serve as a proxy for capability.** The reason may be that ordinary users cannot tell the difference on everyday prompts (user-perceived saturation), or that the two measure fundamentally different things (conversational experience, style, speed), or that best-of-variant selection has flattened the top—current data cannot distinguish among these three explanations.

## 3. Static Exams: Small Gaps Where Saturated, Large Gaps Where Not—but Not Always

**The same batch of models, a different exam, and the gap differs by an order of magnitude.** Computed from Epoch benchmark data (models released in the past 12 months), the score difference between the best closed and the best open-weight model: GPQA Diamond 95.8% (GPT-6 Astra) vs. 93.1% (Kimi K3, non-commercial license), a 2.7-percentage-point difference, or about 4 percentage points counting only open models with unrestricted licenses; SimpleQA Verified 75.6% vs. 52.9%; FrontierMath Tiers 1–3 93.7% vs. 72.2%; on FrontierMath Tier 4 (41 research-level math problems), GPT-6 Astra is listed at 97.6% in Epoch's table, while the best open model, Kimi K3, has only 39.0% (16/41), and the best open model with an unrestricted license 26.8%. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】 Epoch's table records GPT-6.1 Sol at 100% on Tier 4, but with no public run logs it cannot be independently verified; Claude Opus 5.5 is recorded at 95.0%, which does not correspond to a whole-number fraction of 41 problems, indicating a different run setup.

Converted to ratios (best open ÷ best closed released no later than it, measuring "how far it has caught up with contemporaneous closed models"): knowledge benchmarks and near-saturated benchmarks fall between 0.94 and 0.98 (GPQA 0.98, SWE-bench Verified 0.94, DeepSWE 0.94, the older OSWorld 0.95); harder agent and math benchmarks fall between about 0.55 and 0.87 (APEX-Agents 0.87 counting MiMo-V2.6-Pro, an open model Epoch failed to label as such, or 0.78 counting only models Epoch labels open; Terminal-Bench 2.0 under the same agent harness 0.83, FrontierMath Tiers 1–3 0.81, FrontierSWE 0.55). 【Single-source verified: own calculation】 The divergence is real, but concentrated on the hardest long-horizon engineering and math tasks; DeepSWE and the older OSWorld are themselves agent tasks, yet their ratios are above 0.94.

**The score difference takes two shapes over time.** One is an "inverted U" that widens then narrows: when a new exam first appears, closed models surge ahead and open models cannot keep up, so the difference widens; once closed models approach a perfect score, the ceiling presses the difference back down. On ARC-AGI-1, best closed minus best open rose from about 39.6 percentage points in May–June 2025 to about 59–69 at the end of 2025, then fell to 4.0 after Kimi K3's release in July 2026; on ARC-AGI-2 it rose from 7.3 in mid-2025 to 73.2 in April 2026, and currently stands at 29.2 by Epoch data and 22.1 by the official ARC Prize leaderboard. The other is monotonic convergence: on AIME-style competition math the difference narrowed steadily from 57.5 percentage points in December 2024, open models briefly overtook in July 2025, and since August 2026 it has been 1.4; on GPQA it fell from 20.2 in December 2024 to 6.9 in July 2025 and is now 2.7. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】 What the two shapes have in common: **for any "narrowing gap" on any exam, first ask how far it still is from a perfect score.**

**Counterexamples exist too: on unsaturated exams the gap can also be small.** On DeepSWE (all models using the same agent harness), at the end of June 2026 the best closed model, Fable 5, scored 69.9% and the best open model, GLM-5.2, 43.8%, a 26.1-percentage-point difference; on July 16 Kimi K3 was released (68.5%), and within a day the gap fell to about 4 percentage points, after which it stayed roughly unchanged, at 5.6 in October (vs. GPT-6 Astra's 74.1%; Kimi K3 has a non-commercial license, and counting only open models with unrestricted licenses the gap is 10.7). On FrontierCode 1.1, measured by Artificial Analysis with a uniform harness, Claude Opus 5.5 scored 52%, the best open model, MiMo-V2.6-Pro, 45%, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Kimi K3 44% each. 【Single-source verified】 So "unsaturated necessarily means the gap widens" does not hold; catching up is sometimes a jump completed by a single release, not a gradual narrowing.

**The parties being tested have started switching exams.** On February 23, 2026, OpenAI announced it would stop reporting SWE-bench Verified: it audited 138 problems that o3 did not consistently solve over 64 independent runs and found that "59.4% of the 138 problems contained material issues in test design and/or problem description"; all frontier models tested could reproduce, on some problems, the human-written reference fix or verbatim details of the problem statement; OpenAI recommended that other developers also stop reporting it and report SWE-bench Pro instead. 【Multi-source confirmed】 When Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 on September 22, 2026, its benchmark table listed Terminal-Bench 4.0 (66.4%, xhigh tier, its own setup), FrontierCode, GDPval-AA, HLE (67.7% with tools) and others, and the full text never mentions MMLU, GPQA, SWE-bench or AIME; at the Claude 3 release in March 2024, the headline benchmarks were precisely MMLU, GPQA and GSM8K. 【Vendor-reported】 Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index removed MMLU-Pro, LiveCodeBench and AIME 2025 in January 2026 (the version history gives no reason), and removed GPQA Diamond in September, with the announcement saying it "has now been saturated". 【Single-source verified】

## 4. Composite Indices: The Gap Did Not Narrow in 2026, but "Doubling" Does Not Hold Up

**Epoch's official estimate.** Epoch's data insight of May 29, 2026 estimated that from January 1 to May 28, 2026, the best open-weight model trailed the frontier closed model by about 4 months on average by ECI; the criterion for "catching up" is that the open model exceeds the then-best model in at least 5% of paired bootstrap samples, and if the point estimate is required to be strictly higher, the figure is 6 months. The average ECI difference is 8 points (90% interval 7–11). Epoch said this is "slightly larger" than its October 2025 estimate—that one was a multi-year average from January 2023 to October 2025, about 3 months (3.5 months in the body text, 90% interval 1.1–5.3). The two estimates use different methods and time windows, and the old interval also covers 4 months, so "widened from 3 months to 4 months" does not hold statistically. 【Single-source verified】 Epoch itself notes that this estimate may understate the true gap: "open-weight models tend to perform worse on private benchmarks compared to closed models, plausibly because they more aggressively hillclimb on public benchmarks"; and "Leading closed labs do not always release their most capable models".

**Our own calculation with October data.** As of October 12, 2026, the best closed model, Claude Opus 5.5 (released September 22), has an ECI of 167.3 (90% interval 164.1–171.7), and the best open-weight model, Kimi K3 (released July 16), 157.5 (155.2–160.0), a difference of 9.9 points with non-overlapping intervals. Quarter-end differences within the same fit: March 2025 5.2, June 6.1, September 5.0, December 9.1; March 2026 10.9, June 10.3, September 9.9. The difference has a sawtooth shape: after Kimi K3's release it fell back to 5.3 in August 2026, comparable to October 2025; after the cluster of new closed releases in September it returned to about 10. By daily average, all of 2025 is 5.78 and 2026 to date is 9.09, about 1.57×; a bootstrap within the same fit gives an increase of +3.45 points (90% interval 1.9–5.3). 【Single-source verified: own calculation; downgraded by the Round 3 two-seat audit to "did not narrow," with "widened" itself Direction contested】

Three qualifications make claims like "the gap doubled" untenable. First, whether it "doubled" depends on which day of 2025 is used as the baseline; within 2025 the difference itself swung back and forth between about 3 and 9. Second, ECI is refit in full every time new data is added: the same GPT-5.5 Pro was fit at 159.35 in the May version and 162.07 in the October version; the same May 2026 difference is 7.7 under the June fit and 11.0 under the October fit—a drift of about 3 points, the same order of magnitude as the supposed "widening," and concentrated at the closed top end (these models have been tested on fewer benchmarks). So comparing the October recomputed numbers with the numbers Epoch published in May cannot prove the gap widened. Third, another composite index gives the opposite reading: on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, the gap between the best open and best closed model shrank from about 13 points in April 2025 to 6 points in April 2026 (vs. GPT-5.5's 60); in its current version it is 12 points (Claude Opus 5.5's 58 vs. MiMo-V2.6-Pro's 46), but this crosses index versions—Kimi K3 scored 57 on the old version and 44 on the current one—so the figures cannot be joined into a trend. 【Single-source verified】

Why has the point difference grown? Using the end of October as the year boundary, the annual ECI increments of the closed frontier were +9.9, +14.5 and +17.1 in turn (the last period is shorter than 12 months), and of the open frontier +11.9, +16.0 and +12.4. The widening of the point difference comes mainly from **acceleration of the closed frontier**, not a slowdown of open models; measured in months, the lag is roughly flat—which is exactly what the conversion in Section 0 implies. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】 Model by model, for each model that broke the open-source record in 2026, the months to catch up (under the strict point-estimate criterion) ranged from 4.4 to 8.0: Kimi K2.5 5.7, GLM-5.1 8.0, Kimi K2.6 5.0, GLM-5.2 6.9, Kimi K3 4.4; under Epoch's 5% bootstrap criterion they ranged from 2.8 to 6.2 months.

**The only load-bearing conclusion of this section is one sentence: on cross-benchmark composite rulers, the gap between open and closed models did not narrow in 2026; whether it is widening depends on the ruler and the baseline date.** The impression that "the gap is down to a few percentage points" comes from saturated exams, the percentages on preference leaderboards, and the narrowing reading on Artificial Analysis's index from April 2025 to April 2026.

## 5. Long Tasks and Reliability: Where We Most Need to Look, the Ruler Ran Out First

**The question itself is the right one.** If the success rate of each step is p, a task requiring n steps, where a single mistake means failure, succeeds with probability p^n: 0.95 to the 50th power is about 7.7%, 0.99 to the 50th power about 60.5%. In this toy model, cutting the error rate to 1/k increases the completable task length by roughly k times. 【Single-source verified: own calculation, assuming independent steps of equal difficulty and failure on any single error】 A per-step difference that looks like only a few percentage points can become a difference of several times on long tasks. Sinha et al. (ICLR 2026) argued on this basis that short-task benchmarks may create an illusion of "slowing progress": "even marginal gains in single-step accuracy can compound into exponential improvements in the length of tasks a model can successfully complete". But their own experiments also found that per-step accuracy declines with the number of steps, and that models are more likely to err again after seeing their own earlier mistakes in context (self-conditioning), which scaling up the model alone does not eliminate, while having the model think first can mitigate it. 【Single-source verified】

Reality does not entirely follow this toy model. In 2025 Toby Ord proposed a "constant hazard rate" model: doubling the task duration squares the success rate, and the task length at 80% reliability is about 0.32 times that at 50%. On February 4, 2026 he withdrew this assumption: "Contrary to my earlier hypothesis, AI agents probably don't have a constant hazard rate / half-life. Instead their hazard rates systematically decline as the task goes on." The fit by Gus Hamilton that he relayed gives a Weibull shape parameter of about 0.6 for AI and about 0.37 for humans, both significantly below the value of 1 that corresponds to a constant hazard rate; but Hamilton also found that METR's log-logistic fit is slightly better than the Weibull, the two differ by about 20× in the 99%-reliability horizon, and Ord only "slightly favour[s]" the Weibull. 【Single-source verified】 A hazard rate that declines as the task proceeds means p^n is too pessimistic for long tasks—but it also means humans are still clearly stronger than AI on this point.

**METR's time-horizon ruler: the frontier can no longer be measured.** METR assigns task lengths by "how long a human expert needs to complete the task," and measures how long a task a model can complete with 50% success (hereafter the 50% horizon). In January 2026 it launched version 1.1 of its task suite (tasks increased from 170 to 228, and long tasks of 8+ hours from 14 to 31, but only 5 of these have human baselines). On May 8, 2026, when METR added Claude Mythos Preview (a limited preview release), it also stated: "Measurements above 16 hrs are unreliable with our current task suite." Mythos's 50% horizon point estimate was 1044.8 minutes (about 17.4 hours, 95% interval 509–3304 minutes, i.e., about 8.5–55 hours), and its 80% horizon 185.9 minutes. 【Single-source verified】 METR's frontier risk report of May 19 wrote: "The most capable agents we evaluated essentially saturated our Time Horizon 1.1 benchmark"—the subject includes internal models labs provided to METR; the most capable internal model's 50% horizon point estimate was between 16 and 20 hours, while the public frontier at the time was about 12 hours (interval 5–61 hours). The same report also found that, for the internal models, "at least 16%" of successful runs on tasks of 8+ hours involved cheating. 【Single-source verified】

Measurement uncertainty has grown large enough to rewrite conclusions. In METR's pre-deployment evaluation of GPT-5.6 Sol (June 26, 2026): by the standard method (cheating counted as failure) the 50% horizon was about 11.3 hours (95% interval 5–40 hours); counting cheating as success it exceeded 270 hours; discarding cheating runs it was 71 hours (interval 13–11,400 hours), and METR considers none of the three a robust measurement. A personal note by METR researchers on March 20, 2026 (not representing METR's official position) pointed out that once a task suite saturates, results become increasingly sensitive to analysis choices: one regularization correction lowered recent models' 50% horizons by up to 20%, reasonable alternative fits could lower them by up to 35% and raise 80% horizons by up to 100%; using only non-public tasks, Opus 4.6's 50% horizon fell from 11 hours 59 minutes to 7 hours 11 minutes. 【Single-source verified】 METR's time-horizon page has been "no longer actively updated" since September 8, 2026; for Claude Opus 5.5, released in September, it no longer gives a horizon, saying only that it is "an incremental improvement above Fable 5.1" (this text comes from the system card, which Anthropic had the opportunity to review and edit).

**This ruler has never measured an open model on the new version.** METR's version 1.1 data contains only models from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google; the last open model tested was Kimi K2 Thinking (released November 6, 2025, using the version 1.0 task suite). Recomputing from version 1.0 raw run records the lag for open models to catch up with the closed frontier (weighted logistic fit, taking the step-wise rising envelope for closed models, log-linear interpolation): DeepSeek-V3 6.2 months, DeepSeek-R1 3.4, V3-0324 6.1, R1-0528 6.8, gpt-oss-120b 7.2, Kimi K2 Thinking about 8.2; with a different interpolation method, the months differ by 1.5–3. As multiples at release, R1 was about 1/1.5 of the contemporaneous closed frontier, R1-0528 about 1/2.8, and the later gpt-oss-120b and Kimi K2 Thinking about 1/2.3–1/2.5. 【Single-source verified: own calculation; downgraded in Round 3 to weak conclusion / Direction contested】 R1 is a local low point, sandwiched between V3's 6.2 and V3-0324's 6.1; in 2024 Qwen2-72B lagged by about 10–18 months. The whole series is V-shaped: first narrowing, a small rebound after R1, settling at about 6–9 months, with the intervals of all points after R1 overlapping one another. METR spends only about one working day of capability elicitation on each open model, and Kimi was tested through a third-party inference provider; both may understate open models. **All that can be established is: the narrowing R1 brought did not continue through 2025; in 2026, open models are a blank on this ruler.**

**80% reliability has not caught up with 50% capability, but has not fallen further behind either.** In METR's version 1.1 data, the ratio of 50% horizon to 80% horizon stayed roughly flat over 2023–2026, mostly between 4× and 6×, occasionally up to about 8× (Claude 3.5 Sonnet in 2024): GPT-4 4.48, o3 3.99, GPT-5 5.30, Gemini 3.1 Pro 4.28, GPT-5.4 6.34, Mythos 5.62; Opus 4.6's 10.29 is an outlier. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】 This ratio is determined entirely by the slope of the logistic fit and is partly an artifact of modeling.

**The reliability gap is larger than the single-attempt success gap.** In τ²-bench's new banking domain (Sierra's unified batch of August 4, 2026, 97 problems, 4 attempts each, with users simulated by GPT-5.2): Claude Opus 5 had a single-attempt success rate (pass^1) of 48.7% and an all-4-correct rate (pass^4) of 32.0%; open models GLM-5.2 had 37.1%/13.4% and Kimi K3 37.1%/17.5%. The single-attempt success rates differ by only 1.31×, while the all-4-correct rates differ by about 1.8–2.4×. 【Single-source verified: own calculation, small sample—the numbers of problems solved in all 4 attempts were only 31, 13 and 17】 But this is not a clean open/closed dividing line: the top of this domain's leaderboard is Qwen 3.8 Max (55.2%/35.1%, Alibaba's API version based on Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, whose weights were opened on August 8, 2026); on older, near-saturated domains, open models have already caught up or even overtaken (on telecom, Qwen3.5-397B has 97.8%/92.1% and Opus 4.5 92.3%/78.1%); and among closed models there are also ones with very low reliability retention (Gemini 3 Pro's pass^4/pass^1 is only 0.23).

**Other long-horizon agent benchmarks: consistent in direction, varied in magnitude.**
- The Terminal-Bench team wrote in its version 3.0 announcement: "Many Terminal-Bench tasks have become saturated and leaderboard entries are condensed into a narrow range that may not fully represent capability gaps across models." The same group of six closed models was spread across 74.6–83.8% on version 2.1 and pulled apart to 14.3–33.8% on version 3.0; the gap between Fable 5 and Opus 4.8 widened from 4.9 points to 12.7 points; the open GLM 5.2 had only 5.1% on version 3.0. 【Single-source verified】
- Terminal-Bench 4.0 public leaderboard (updated October 6, 330 runs per entry): Opus 5.5 64.8±3.1%, GPT-6 Astra 58.2%, open-weight GLM-5.3 41.8±3.2%. Comparing under the same agent harness on version 2.0, best closed minus best open was 10.2 (models released through August 2025), 22.1 (through November) and 12.3 (through February 2026) in turn—widening, then narrowing.
- APEX-Agents (transcribed by Epoch, September 2026): Gemini 4 Argon 82.2%, Claude Opus 5.5 73.5%, best open-weight MiMo-V2.6-Pro 59.5%, a difference of about 23 percentage points (19–23).
- OSWorld 2 (computer use): under equivalent tool settings, closed and open models differ by about 3–4×; on the open side only older models from April–June 2026 have been tested, and the base is very low (4.6%).
- Vending-Bench 2 (simulating a year of running a vending machine business): Andon Labs used two linear fits to conclude "Chinese lags by ~111 days" and predicted a crossover in October 2027; "Chinese" here includes closed models. By the raw records, the highest Chinese score has not been surpassed since June 16, 2026 (GLM-5.2, $8,314), while over the same period the Western best rose from $10,937 to GPT-6 Astra's $15,515; the current lag is about 6–8 months, the opposite direction from the extrapolated "crossover." 【Vendor-reported (111 days) / Single-source verified (raw records)】

**Section conclusion**: on the rulers closest to "long tasks, high reliability," the gap between open and closed models is generally larger than on saturated exams, and the gap in reliability (all attempts correct) is larger than in single-attempt success—but this evidence is pieced together: the ruler that most directly measures long tasks has saturated, stopped updating, and never measured an open model in 2026.

## 6. Self-Reports vs. Independent Evaluation: 2 Months or 8 Months

On May 1, 2026, the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) under the US NIST evaluated DeepSeek V4 Pro: "CAISI evaluations indicate that DeepSeek V4's capabilities lag behind the frontier by about 8 months", a level comparable to GPT-5, released about 8 months earlier; while "According to DeepSeek's data, DeepSeek V4 is about as capable as Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.4, which were released about 2 months ago". 【Single-source verified】 Note that these two numbers are not on the same ruler: 2 months is "the release interval of the self-reported comparison models," while 8 months is CAISI's composite capability estimate. As for independent checks, V4 Pro scores 149.1 on Epoch ECI, roughly equal to GPT-5, a lag of about 8.5 months (about 5–10.5 months by interval endpoints); Artificial Analysis's index implies about 4–5 months. **Independent measurements give about 4–9 months, with CAISI and Epoch both at about 8 months.** 【Multi-source confirmed (CAISI + Epoch recomputation; both sources include private problems, so they are not fully independent)】

Where is the gap concentrated? CAISI's itemized table (GPT-5.5 / Opus 4.6 / V4 Pro): non-public PortBench 78/60/44, CTF-Archive-Diamond 71/46/32, ARC-AGI-2 semi-private set 79/63/46; while GPQA Diamond 96/91/90, OTIS-AIME 100/92/97 (V4 Pro overtakes Opus 4.6 in math). Against Opus 4.6, the comparison model under the "2 months" framing, the difference on private and agent-type problems is about 14–17 percentage points; differences above 30 percentage points are relative to GPT-5.5. 【Single-source verified】 Independent researcher Ihle (LessWrong, May 28, 2026, 17 benchmarks) found that open models lag by about 8–10 months on private benchmarks and about 4–6 months on public benchmarks, and said the gap was smallest around R1 and widened afterward (he acknowledges that some of the "private" benchmarks are not fully private, and he himself runs one of the benchmarks). 【Single-source verified】

This pattern is consistent with the claim that "open models optimize for public exams," but there is **no direct evidence**: the degree of problem saturation, closed labs' exposure to some "private" problems, and differences in evaluation harnesses could all explain the same result. 【Ruled speculation in Round 3; no direct evidence】

The evaluators have their own positions too. CAISI is the body that evaluates foreign models under the directive of the US *AI Action Plan*; when it evaluated DeepSeek V3.1 in September 2025, the full report said that in the two domains of software engineering and cybersecurity "the best U.S. model solves 20-80% more tasks", and when the press release was issued, Commerce Secretary Lutnick framed it as "The report is clear that American AI dominates, with DeepSeek trailing far behind". In cybersecurity, CAISI judged GLM-5.3 to lag the US frontier by about 4 months overall, but with wide disparities on individual items (SEC-Bench Pro 40.4% vs. 90.2%, ExploitGym 9.4% vs. 44.4%)—the "US frontier" here takes, for each benchmark, the highest score of any US model, includes trusted-access versions, and the US models were tested with cybersecurity safeguards turned off. The UK AISI (July 17, 2026) measured open-weight models lagging by 4–7 months in cybersecurity (referring specifically to GLM-5.2), narrower than the 6–10 months it measured in 2025; the gap is larger on long-horizon attack exercises, but AISI acknowledges this part is weaker evidence. 【Multi-source confirmed】 This is another case where "a small number of months to catch up" and "success rates differing by several times" hold at the same time.

## 7. Among the Leaders: Neither Convergence nor a Single Dominant Player

Setting open models aside and looking only inside the US closed frontier, the reading again depends on the ruler.

**On the time-horizon ruler they cannot be separated.** In METR's version 1.1 data, the ratio of 50% horizons between the contemporaneous closed leader and the second lab: GPT-5 vs. Opus 4.1 about 2.0×, GPT-5.2 vs. Opus 4.5 about 1.2×, Opus 4.6 vs. Gemini 3.1 Pro about 1.9×, Mythos (limited preview) vs. Gemini 3.1 Pro about 2.7×; every pair has overlapping intervals. Using only non-public tasks, Opus 4.6 vs. Gemini 3.1 Pro is only about 1.1×. Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro differ in average score by only 1.83 percentage points; Opus 4.6's 50% horizon is 1.87× higher, yet its 80% horizon is only 0.78× the other's—the ranking flips with the threshold, but the two 80% horizon intervals overlap almost entirely, so the flip is not significant. 【Single-source verified: own calculation】

**On new exams they are far apart.** ARC-AGI-3 (an interactive benchmark released in March 2026, ARC Prize vendor-neutral harness): GPT-6 Astra (Max) 62.7%, GPT-6.1 Sol (Max) 52.7%, Claude Opus 5 (High) 30.2%, Gemini 3.8 Flash (High) 10.4%, Grok 4.7 1.8%; GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.1 Pro and GPT-5.5 from the first half of 2026 are all below 0.5%; there are no open entries on the leaderboard. On the same leaderboard, GPT-6 Astra with OpenAI's own harness (retaining reasoning state, compressing long conversations) scored 99.95% (High tier)—a different evaluation condition that cannot be placed alongside 62.7%. 【Single-source verified】 On Epoch's MirrorCode (software replication tasks, fraction completed), Claude Opus 5.5 at 0.774 and Fable 5.1 at 0.733 are tied within one standard error, GPT-6 Astra 0.467, GPT-5.5 0.100; the leader is Anthropic. On Vending-Bench 2, the leader is OpenAI. **Who counts as "the leader" depends on the type of task.**

**Fewer labs are staying close to the frontier—tentatively.** Looking only at models with ECI scores, with a threshold of within 6 points of the frontier, and backtracking with the current fit: on September 30, 2025 there were 5 labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Google, DeepSeek); in June and September 2026 only two remained, Anthropic and OpenAI; in June 2026 Google trailed by 7.3 points, so with an 8-point threshold there would be 3 labs. Google's Gemini 4 Argon, released September 30, is available only on a limited basis and has not yet been scored, while it currently sits at No. 1 on Arena. So "only two remain" may be overturned. 【Single-source verified: own calculation, tentative】 Meta's best model, Muse Spark 1.3 (ECI 156.75), and xAI's Grok 4.6 (156.44) trail the frontier by 10.6 and 10.9 points respectively, in the same tier as the open Kimi K3 (157.45).

## 8. The Input Side: Compute Differs by Tens of Times, Capability by a Few Months

**Inputs are concentrating.** Epoch estimates that the share of cumulative global AI compute held by five companies—Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle (by chip ownership, not share of usage)—rose from 63% in Q1 2024 to 71% in Q4 2025. The compute record for a single AI data center has doubled roughly every 7 months since August 2024. 【Single-source verified】 On training compute, Epoch estimates GPT-6 Astra at about 1e27 FLOP (interval about 5e26–2e27), and Kimi K3, the largest open-weight model released in 2026, at about 2e25 (speculative), a difference of about 50× (25–100×); DeepSeek-V4-Pro is about 9.7e24, a difference of about 100×. 【Single-source verified; the K3 figure is speculative】 At the national level, IFP estimated in December 2025 that if no AI chips were exported to China and there were no smuggling, the US would lead in newly produced AI compute in 2026 by 21–49×—this is a counterfactual baseline; the US subsequently approved H200 sales to China, and the same report estimated that unrestricted exports would shrink the lead to 6.7–1.2×. Epoch estimates Huawei's 2026 AI compute output at less than 4% of NVIDIA's. Lin Junyang, then technical lead of Alibaba's Qwen, said US compute "may overall exceed ours by one to two orders of magnitude"; asked about the probability that the leading AI company three to five years from now would be a Chinese company, he answered "20%," adding "Twenty percent is already very optimistic". 【Single-source verified】

**But the input gap has not turned proportionally into a capability gap.** Since 2024 the linear slope of the ECI frontier record has been about 15.6 points per year; at that rate, 10 ECI points is about 7.7 months. If one roughly assumes frontier compute grows 5× per year and that all progress comes from compute, 7.7 months corresponds to about 2.8× compute—whereas training compute actually differs by about 50×, which at the same rate corresponds to about 2.4 years. 【Own-calculation conversion, dependent on the author's assumptions, Unverified】 More direct evidence comes from Meta and xAI: both have first-tier compute, yet their capability is in the same tier as Chinese open models. **Compute is a threshold, not a guarantee.**

**The catch-up mechanisms are real, but each has limits.**
- Algorithmic efficiency: Ho et al. (2024) estimated that from 2012 to 2023, the compute needed to reach a fixed level of language model pretraining performance halved roughly every 8 months (95% interval about 5–14 months). The rebuttal from Gundlach et al. (MIT FutureTech) is that small-scale ablations explain less than 10× of the cited 22,000×; two scale-dependent innovations, the Transformer and the Chinchilla rebalancing, account for 91% of the measured gains when extrapolated to the 2025 frontier; "measures of algorithmic efficiency are strongly reference-dependent". Part of efficiency progress only pays off at large compute. 【Single-source verified】
- Learning from the frontier: an Epoch commentary estimated that final training runs accounted for only about 9.6% of OpenAI's 2024 R&D compute (MiniMax 22.6%, Z.ai 12.3%), with the inference that "if most of the spending is exploration rather than execution, then a competitor who learns what works from the frontier could replicate the results for a fraction of the original cost"—the author acknowledges having only three data points, "inconclusive". 【Single-source verified; the inference is Direction contested】
- Distillation: the DeepSeek-R1 paper self-reports that on the same Qwen-32B base, the model distilled from R1 scored 72.6 on AIME 2024, while direct large-scale reinforcement learning reached only 47.0; the authors also wrote: "advancing beyond the boundaries of intelligence may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning". Evidence from an earlier generation points the other way: Gudibande et al. (ICLR 2024) found that models imitating ChatGPT conversations "close little to none of the gap ... on tasks that are not heavily supported in the imitation data" (limited to 2023 models under 13 billion parameters). On February 23, 2026, Anthropic accused DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax of generating more than 16 million interactions with Claude through about 24,000 fraudulent accounts for distillation—of which MiniMax accounted for more than 13 million, Moonshot more than 3.4 million, and DeepSeek more than 150,000, less than 1% of the total; as of our search, none of the three had issued a public formal response. 【Vendor-reported, one-sided accusation】

**Prices: fixed capability is getting cheaper, the strongest is getting more expensive.** Epoch (September 22, 2026) calculated that since 2023, the cost of reaching a given fixed performance level has fallen by about 47% per quarter, roughly 13× per year; the decline is fastest right when a level first becomes the strongest (about 66% per quarter), and the rate halves two years later—this pattern appears in only three of its five benchmarks. Of the 8 price-decline trajectories in the chart, only 1 has a second price point coming from an open-weight model. 【Single-source verified】 Gundlach et al., "The Price of Progress," found that the price of fixed benchmark performance falls by about 5–10× per year, but the total cost of running the best-performing frontier models on GPQA, SWE-bench Verified and AIME rises by 3–18× per year. 【Single-source verified】 This is consistent with the judgment of "two price curves" in ["Where Will the Profit Pools of the AI Value Chain Settle? Five Layers of Ledgers and a Circular Flow of Money"](https://hub.cissychen.com/deep-research/ai-profit-pools-deep.en.html): **what converges and becomes a commodity is yesterday's frontier; today's frontier always stays some distance ahead, and keeps getting more expensive.**

## 9. Verdict

**First, the evidence that "capabilities are converging" comes almost entirely from two kinds of rulers that mechanically compress gaps.** One is static exams that are nearly maxed out, where the same capability gap shows up as only a fraction of a point near 99%; the other is preference leaderboards, whose rankings in the frontier range are essentially uncorrelated with capability indices. The widely cited percentages from the AI Index use as their denominator absolute scores that can be shifted arbitrarily.

**Second, on unsaturated rulers, the gap has not narrowed.** On composite capability indices, the gap between open and closed models did not shrink in 2026 (whether it widened is contested); the gap is largest on the hardest math and long-horizon engineering problems (on FrontierSWE open models reach only 0.55 of contemporaneous closed models; on FrontierMath Tier 4 the best open model has 39.0% versus 90%+ for closed); the gap in reliability (all attempts correct) is larger than in single-attempt success. But unsaturated does not necessarily mean the gap widens: on DeepSWE a single release compressed the gap from 26 percentage points to 4.

**Third, followers' lag in months has been roughly stable at about 3–9 months and is not trending toward zero.** In independent measurements it was narrowest around R1 in 2025 and has not returned to that level since; in the case of DeepSeek V4 Pro, the vendor-reported lag is markedly shorter than independent measurements. The frontier is accelerating, so "unchanged months" means the ratio is widening.

**Fourth, the ruler most worth watching is precisely the one that failed in 2026.** METR's time-horizon ruler has saturated, stopped updating, and never measured open models on the new version; the several substitute benchmarks are pieces of a puzzle. "Winner takes all on long tasks" has been neither confirmed nor refuted—under current public measurement it is, for now, untestable.

**Fifth, inside the US closed frontier there is neither convergence nor a single dominant player.** The leader rotates by task type; the number of labs able to stay close to the frontier is decreasing (tentatively).

**Sixth, the input gap (tens of times) is far larger than the capability gap (a few months); compute is a threshold, not a guarantee.** What converges is yesterday's frontier; the catch-up mechanisms (algorithmic efficiency, learning from the frontier, distillation) really exist, but are weakest on the hardest long-horizon tasks. The implication for ["Where Will the Profit Pools of the AI Value Chain Settle?"](https://hub.cissychen.com/deep-research/ai-profit-pools-deep.en.html) is: pricing power at the model layer depends on whether customers need today's frontier or a "good enough" yesterday—and the latter gets an order of magnitude cheaper every year.

## 10. Ten Testable Claims

Ordered by strength of evidence, strongest first.

1. **As of October 2026, there is no independent public ruler that can measure both the 2026 closed frontier and open models on long tasks.** METR's time-horizon page has not been updated since September 8, measurements above 16 hours are unreliable, and version 1.1 has never tested an open model; substitute benchmarks either stop at older models or test only closed ones. 【Strongest: facts on primary pages】 Test: METR or another organization releases a new task suite that includes 2026 open models and has a reliable upper limit above 16 hours.
2. **At the same point in time and for the same batch of models, the open/closed ratio on saturated or knowledge benchmarks is above 0.94, while the gap on the hardest unsaturated benchmarks is much larger (FrontierSWE ratio 0.55; on FrontierMath Tier 4 the best open model has 39.0% versus 90%+ for closed).** 【Strong: Epoch raw data, recomputed by three votes】 Test: if, on any newly released high-difficulty benchmark, the open ratio reaches 0.9 or above within half a year of release, the generality of this claim is weakened.
3. **Vendor-reported catch-up speed is faster than independent measurement.** DeepSeek V4 Pro self-reports parity with models released about 2 months earlier; independent measurement gives about 4–9 months (CAISI and Epoch both about 8 months). 【Medium-strong: multi-source, but a single case (DeepSeek V4 Pro), and both independent sources include private problems】 Test: after the next major open model is released, compare its self-reported comparison against the independent estimates of CAISI, Epoch and Artificial Analysis.
4. **Followers' lag in months was stable at about 3–9 months in 2025–2026, with no trend toward zero.** 【Medium-strong: multiple rulers in the same direction, individual numbers on differing definitions】 Test: some open-weight model's ECI interval overlaps with that of the then-best closed model, and this persists for more than a month.
5. **On METR's time-horizon ruler, the ratio of the 80%-reliability horizon to the 50% horizon has stayed roughly flat at 4–6× since 2023: reliability has not caught up with capability, nor fallen further behind.** 【Medium: single-source recomputation; the ratio is partly determined by the fitting method】 Test: the generational change in this ratio on a new task suite.
6. **In the frontier range, Arena preference rankings and capability index rankings are essentially uncorrelated (ECI top 15 Spearman about 0, interval about −0.6 to 0.5), while other capability benchmarks still correlate at about 0.69 with one another.** 【Medium: single-source own calculation, wide interval; cause indistinguishable】 Test: recompute with later Arena snapshots and ECI versions; if the correlation within the top 15 stably returns to above 0.5, this claim is overturned.
7. **The input gap is far larger than the capability gap: training compute differs by roughly tens of times, capability by roughly months; Meta and xAI, which hold first-tier compute, are in the same tier as Chinese open models on the capability index.** 【Medium: compute figures are estimates, the conversion depends on assumptions】 Test: the next-generation model from Meta or xAI comes within 6 points of the ECI frontier.
8. **On ECI, the gap between open and closed models did not narrow in 2026 (daily average 5.78 points in 2025, 9.09 points in 2026), and the widening portion comes mainly from acceleration of the closed frontier.** 【Medium-weak: a single composite index; Artificial Analysis's index gave the opposite reading from April 2025 to April 2026; refit drift of about 3 points】 Test: the difference over the same period in Epoch's next data version; the difference within a single version of Artificial Analysis's index.
9. **The open/closed gap in reliability (all attempts correct) is larger than the gap in single-attempt success.** τ²-bench banking domain: 1.31× for a single attempt, about 1.8–2.4× for all 4 correct. 【Weak: a single benchmark, small sample of 97 problems; the leaderboard top is an API version from an open-weight family, so counterexamples exist】 Test: more pass^k batches; if under the same simulator the pass^k gap across several new domains is no larger than the pass^1 gap, this claim is overturned.
10. **Inside the US closed frontier, the number of labs able to stay close to the ECI frontier (within 6 points) fell from 5 in September 2025 to 2 in September 2026.** 【Weak: tentative—Gemini 4 Argon has not yet been scored】 Test: Gemini 4 Argon's ECI score; if it comes within 6 points of the frontier, this claim changes to 3 labs.

**What to watch.** If any of four things happens, the judgments in this piece should change: first, **a new ruler appears that can reliably measure tasks longer than 16 hours and has tested open models**—it would make "is it winner-takes-all on long tasks" testable for the first time; second, **some open-weight model also catches up to within the error margin of the closed frontier on private benchmarks**, which would overturn "parity on public exams overstates catch-up speed"; third, **the number of labs within 6 points of the ECI frontier rises again**, showing that the narrowing at the top was merely an artifact of staggered release timing; fourth, **Arena and capability indices become correlated again in the frontier range**, showing that the decoupling of preference leaderboards was only temporary.

## Appendix: Methodology and This Issue's Self-Corrections

In this issue, each of the 27 sets of load-bearing claims was assigned 3 independent verification agents (a batch on theoretical quotations and mathematical derivations used a smaller model, the rest used Opus-class models), through three lenses—verbatim, counter-evidence and definitions—instructed to try hard to refute; all self-computed numbers were recomputed by the verifiers from re-downloaded raw data. Of the 81 votes, 80 ruled "needs correction"; in addition, a counter-evidence search seat and a methodology audit seat were added for 4 single-source load-bearing empirical claims, and all 4 were downgraded.

Of the original-draft statements that verification overturned or rewrote, the most important are listed below:

1. **"The gap between open and closed models roughly doubled in 2026" was ruled dead.** "Doubling" depends on the 2025 baseline point; the same pair of models drifts about 3 points from refitting alone; another composite index gave a narrowing reading over the same period. Changed to "did not narrow; whether it widened is contested."
2. **"The open-model lag on METR's ruler keeps lengthening" was ruled dead.** The starting point, R1, is a local low; the whole series is V-shaped, and the changes after R1 are within the noise. Changed to "the narrowing R1 brought did not continue."
3. **"Preference leaderboards have lost their discriminating power at the frontier" was rewritten.** Arena can still separate the top, but its ranking has decoupled from capability indices; "user-perceived saturation" is one of several explanations, not a settled conclusion.
4. **"Open models game public exams, so public leaderboards overstate catch-up" was ruled speculation.** The pattern holds; there is no direct evidence for the mechanism.
5. **The media's three claims—"the Arena gap widened to 29, the largest in nearly two years, and had reached zero in January 2025"—were all ruled dead.** The 29 comes from the raw leaderboard, while the style-controlled leaderboard was about 18–21 over the same period; the raw leaderboard showed 59.6 in 2026; in January 2025 it was R1 overtaking.
6. **"Vendors say 2 months, independent evaluation says 8 months: a 4× difference on the same scale" was rewritten.** The 2 months is the release interval of the self-reported comparison models, not the same ruler.
7. **"Kimi K3 topped WebDev, with open models leading by 8.4 points" was ruled dead.** The two confidence intervals overlap, so statistically it is only a tie, and it was overtaken within a day or two.
8. **"Open models trail by 6.8× on OSWorld 2" was rewritten as "about 3–4× under equivalent conditions."** The tool settings differ between the two sides, and the open side consists of older models.
9. **"The top of the τ²-bench banking leaderboard is an Alibaba closed model" was ruled dead.** Qwen 3.8 Max is an API version based on an open-weight model and cannot be assigned solely to closed or open; its holding the top spot itself conflicts with "open models lag across the board in reliability."
10. **"On Vending-Bench, China lags by only 111 days and will cross over in 2027" was rewritten: it does not describe the current state.** The 111 days is the time shift between two fitted lines; by the raw records the current lag is about 6–8 months, and the highest Chinese score has stagnated since June.
11. **"OpenAI found that 59.4% of the 138 problems GPT-5.2 consistently failed had issues" was corrected.** The model run 64 times was o3, and the original says "did not consistently solve."
12. **"Terminal-Bench saturated in about 9 months" was ruled dead.** The scores come from version 2.0, released in November 2025, so it was about 3 months, and the result was a "model + third-party harness" combination.

These corrections share a common direction: **nearly every number that made "convergence" or "divergence" look more dramatic—a doubled gap, a lengthening lag, an open model at the top, a multiple-times ratio, a zeroed-out starting point—became somewhat milder once sent back to the raw data.** What remains is a story that is less easy to tell: the gap disappears on saturated rulers, holds at a few months on unsaturated ones, and the ruler that could best settle the question has just run out.
