When People Are Provided For: What UBI Trials Measured, Labor's Lost Bargaining Power, and How People Who Don't Work Can Live Well (Deep Dive)

This is the deep-dive edition · read the plain-language edition →
TL;DR
Suppose AI really does push governments into paying people at scale; this issue asks only what happens after the money arrives. As of November 2026, primary evidence shows: no trial so far is universal, permanent and large enough to live on, so every conclusion carries an extrapolation gap. Work: in low-income countries cash barely reduces labor and often shifts people from wage work to self-employment; in rich countries unconditional cash cuts hours by about 1–2 a week (the US ORUS study, the German trial and lotteries agree), while ORUS's employment effect is −2.3 to −6.6 percentage points depending on the data and Germany, Alaska and Finland show roughly zero; means-tested schemes that claw back benefits as earnings rise suppress work more (Barcelona: −10.2 points). Wellbeing: in rich countries stress relief fades fast and mental health shows no difference by year two; lottery wins raise life satisfaction for more than a decade but not daily mood; the German trial's gain in meaning is an exploratory, non-preregistered result. Meaning: unemployment lowers life satisfaction with almost no adaptation, and benefits don't close the gap; but retirees and people outside the labor force are hurt far less, and long-term unemployed men recover part of the gap when they retire, by mechanisms that can't be separated. Bargaining power: the oil curse narrows to "after the 1970s, blocking democratization in autocracies," cash did not depoliticize recipients, yet Alaska's dividend is set by politics every year; the intelligence curse is an argument only. Tech leaders' statements carry no amounts and no timelines. Nine testable claims close the piece.

Methodology note: This issue was split into 7 research lines (US cash trials, international trials and universal programs, lotteries and retirement, unemployment and the non-income functions of work, rentier states and bargaining power, vendor and advocate claims, and reviews and measurement), collecting 187 claims from 139 primary sources. From these, 22 sets of load-bearing claims were drawn, and each set was assigned 3 independent verification agents, through three lenses—verbatim, counter-evidence and definitions—instructed to try hard to refute: quotations were checked against the primary originals, derived numbers were recomputed, and versions were matched to the formally published version. Of the 66 votes, 4 ruled "holds as written," 62 ruled "needs correction," and 0 ruled "overturned" at the set level. At the sub-claim level, 2 points were overturned: the German trial's "the increase in meaning in life appeared with a delay" (what was delayed was actually job satisfaction), and a quotation attributed to Andersen & Ross (not found in the primary source). In addition, "about 80% of people would keep working even after winning a big jackpot" could not be found in any primary source, and is not used in this article. "0 overturned at the set level" is a warning sign, so for 5 pieces of empirical evidence where a single source carries a key conclusion, an additional counter-evidence search seat and a methodology audit seat were added (10 audits in total): all 5 were downgraded or split, and 34 statements in all were rejected and barred from carrying weight. Two limitations of the run need to be disclosed: in Round 1, 4 lines were interrupted because the computer went to sleep, of which 3 were re-dispatched and 1 was resumed; from late Round 2 through Round 3, the web-search quota shared by the subagents ran out, so some counter-evidence searches were switched to fetching originals directly by DOI and to citation tracing, giving narrower coverage than planned; every angle that was not searched is recorded in the working notes. The 3-vote system and the two-seat audits were all carried out by independent subagents; this issue is not a degraded run. Data as of November 2026. Every number in the text is followed by an evidence grade: multi-source confirmed / single-source verified / direction contested / vendor claim / unverified.

0. Three Questions, One Premise

The next issue on this site, #20 (forthcoming), asks about the aggregate: whether AI will make the labor share keep falling, whether demand will fall short, and whether "money will no longer matter" has any basis. This issue is its sequel, assuming that piece's pessimistic scenario comes true and governments really do start handing out money at scale. We only ask about what happens after the money arrives:

  1. Work: What have existing UBI and cash-transfer trials measured—how much less do people work, do health and well-being improve, and do the improvements last?
  2. Meaning: Of the harm unemployment does, how much is due to lack of money, and how much to losing the things that come with a job (routine, colleagues, identity, purpose)? If it is mostly the latter, money cannot make up for it.
  3. Bargaining power: When a state no longer needs its citizens' labor and taxes, what leverage do citizens have left? How much can the history of oil states and the so-called intelligence curse tell us?

There is also a premise that runs through the whole article: none of today's trials is "universal, permanent UBI after AI." They either pay only some people, or pay for only a few years, or pay too little to live on, or take place in economies where labor is still needed. Each time this article moves from the trials to the AI scenario, it spells out the extrapolation gap in that step.

The conclusions up front:

  • Handing out money does not make people give up, but in rich countries it makes them work a bit less. In low-income countries, cash barely reduces labor, and often shifts people from working for others to working for themselves; in high-income countries, hours fall slightly (one to two hours a week), and the effect on the employment rate is between 0 and −7 percentage points, depending on which data you use. What determines the size is design: whether the money replaces existing benefits or comes on top of them, and whether it is clawed back more the more you earn (the withdrawal rate) or not at all.
  • What money mainly buys is people's "evaluation" of their lives, not so much their "mood" or "meaning." In rich countries, improvements in stress and food insecurity fade quickly, and by the second year mental health shows no difference; a permanent windfall (a lottery win) raises life satisfaction persistently for more than a decade, but has no measurable effect on day-to-day emotion or on mental-health scales.
  • Part of the harm of unemployment cannot be made up with money, but it is not entirely "not working" itself. The life satisfaction of the unemployed hardly recovers on its own, and benefits cannot fully offset the loss; yet people who choose not to work, and retirees, are harmed much less, and long-term unemployed men recover part of their satisfaction once they retire. This piece looks more like an identity cost of "being defined as someone who should work but doesn't"—whether it would disappear when "nobody works," no data today can answer.
  • "Citizens who are not needed will lose bargaining power" is a historically grounded worry, but the evidence is much narrower than the popular version. The oil curse narrows to "after the 1970s, obstructing democratization in authoritarian states"; handing out money has not in itself depoliticized people; the leverage that history has actually shown to work is "the state needs your body" (mass mobilization). AI making this leverage disappear is mechanistic reasoning, not something that has already happened.

1. Setting the Ruler First: Six Dimensions of Trial Design

Trials may all be called "basic income trials," but if any one of the following six things differs, the results cannot be directly compared:

Among the evidence in this issue, only two cases are truly "universal + permanent," both are quasi-experiments, and both have serious flaws: the Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend (about $1,000–2,000 per person per year, with a single state as the only treated unit) and Iran's 2011 universal cash transfer (compensation for removing energy subsidies, eroded by inflation to below 3% of GDP per capita three years later). All RCTs are time-limited or partial.

2. Work: How Much Less Do People Work After Getting Money?

2.1 Low- and Middle-Income Countries: Almost No Reduction, Often a Shift to Self-Employment

  • Kenya GiveDirectly long-term UBI (Banerjee, Faye, Krueger, Niehaus, Suri, September 2023 working paper; randomized by village, 295 villages; the long-term arm pays each adult about $22.5 a month for 12 years): after two years, there was no net change in total household labor supply—hours in agricultural wage work fell significantly, and hours in non-farm self-employment rose slightly more. In the authors' words: "Overall there is no evidence of UBI promoting 'laziness,' but evidence of substantial effects on occupational choice." [Single-source verified; self-reported hours; no formally published version yet; the implementer, GiveDirectly, is an advocacy organization; the study was preregistered (AEA 1952)]
  • Reanalysis of government cash transfers in seven countries (Banerjee, Hanna, Kreindler, Olken 2017): pooling 5 RCTs with baseline data, the employment coefficient is −0.003, i.e., zero. [Single-source verified; mostly conditional cash transfers]
  • Reviews: Baird et al. 2018 and ODI 2016 (165 studies) point the same way: cash transfers without employment goals produce "almost no change" in adult labor in LMICs; where there are effects, most are increases, and decreases are concentrated among the elderly and caregivers. [Multi-source confirmed (LMIC)]
  • Iran 2011: the universal transfer was about 29% of median household income; apart from a fall in hours among young people aged 20–29, no decline in labor supply was found. [Single-source verified; quasi-experiment; confounded by sanctions and inflation]

A detail that is easily misread: wages in the Kenyan villages went up (village elders reported agricultural wages up about 19% and construction wages up about 40%, covering only about 60% of villages that had a prevailing wage). This is often told as "recipients felt secure enough not to take wage jobs, which pushed wages up," but the authors' own attribution is "predominantly due to higher labor demand"—the consumption expansion brought by the cash pulled up demand for labor, and respondents' self-reported reservation wages rose only about 3%. [Single-source verified; corrected by the definitions lens in Round 2] This mechanism depends precisely on there still being people locally who need to hire, which runs opposite to the AI scenario in which "labor is no longer needed." Likewise, the local multiplier of about 2.5, with prices rising only 0.1%, measured by Egger et al. (Econometrica 2022), is explicitly said by the authors to depend on spare capacity on the production side.

2.2 High-Income Countries: Hours Fall Slightly, the Employment Rate Is Contested

ORUS (OpenResearch Unconditional Cash Study) is currently the largest US RCT: 1,000 people aged 21–40 with household income no more than 300% of the federal poverty line received $1,000 a month for 3 years, while 2,000 controls received $50 a month; the samples were in northern Illinois and north-central Texas. The study was launched by Sam Altman in 2016 as a call under the YC name, and the paper's acknowledgments say "private donors"; OpenResearch's research director, Rhodes, is both an author of the paper and a board member, and Altman is also on the board. The paper has no conflict-of-interest statement. But the day before the transfers began, the team had already filed 11 pre-analysis plans, and the direction of the results does not favor the funder's narrative. So this article does not treat it as an independent third-party evaluation, nor does it discount it because of its funder.

Labor results in the final version (QJE 2026, NBER w32719 revised August 2026):

MeasureEmployment effectNotes

The methodology audit seat rejected the single-point formulation "−4.2pp is the preregistered primary result": only the after-the-fact combined measure survives multiple-testing correction, so the three measures must be given together, written as "−2.3 to −6.6 percentage points." [Single-source verified (range); R1 methodology seat rating B, direction A−] The media still mostly cite the −2.0pp from the early 2024 version; the change across versions from 2.0 → 3.9 → 4.1 → 4.2pp comes from adding administrative data, not from cherry-picking numbers.

What does the independent evidence say?

  • The direction for hours is multi-source confirmed: the German Mein Grundeinkommen RCT (DIW, €1,200 a month for 3 years, with IAB administrative records) measured self-reported hours at −1.35 hours/week (p=0.19), almost the same as ORUS; the Swedish lottery (Cesarini et al., AER 2017, average marginal propensity to earn about −0.08) and the US lottery (Golosov et al., QJE 2024, each extra $1 of non-labor income reduces household labor income by about $0.50) point the same way. [Multi-source confirmed (direction); magnitudes cannot be placed side by side across studies—the denominators of the three are, respectively, monthly transfers, one-off wealth, and an annualized income stream]
  • The magnitude of the employment-rate effect is contested: Germany −0.3pp (95% interval about −5.2 to +4.6; the sample has only 107 recipients, wide enough to cover −4.2), Alaska no change in the employment rate, Finland about zero. Within the high-income-country literature, ORUS is one of the studies with a larger participation effect. [Direction contested]
  • Alaska is not a "zero effect": Jones & Marinescu (AEJ: Policy 2022) synthetic control shows no change in the employment rate and a part-time share +1.8pp; an independent team, Bibler, Guettabi, Reimer (JPAM 2023), exploiting variation in payment timing and amounts, found a small decline in women's hours (more pronounced for those with young children) and a rise in men's employment. [Single-source verified (each)]
  • The 1970s negative income tax experiments: Burtless 1986, summarizing four experiments, found husbands' (primary earners') hours about −7%, and wives' and single female household heads' about −17%; after checking against unemployment insurance administrative data, the decline in wives' earnings almost disappeared. [Single-source verified (summary)]

2.3 Design Matters More Than "Money or No Money"

  • Finland 2017–18 (2,000 unemployed people compulsorily selected at random, receiving €560 a month in place of their existing unemployment allowance, with job-search obligations removed): in the first year (the preregistered primary outcome), days in employment +1.5 days (95% CI −2.3 to 5.4), i.e., zero; in the second year, +6.6 days, significant, but this coincided with the 2018 "activation model" reform, which affected the two groups asymmetrically, and the authors say explicitly that it cannot be attributed to basic income. [Single-source verified; register data] Note: this was a replacement, not extra money; the unemployed people's income barely changed—what changed were the incentives and the job-search obligations.
  • Barcelona B-MINCOME (means-tested guaranteed income RCT, in the ten poorest neighborhoods, average withdrawal rate 59%, 23 months): the probability of working was 10.2 percentage points lower than in the control group (control 47%, 95% CI −3.5 to −16.9; a relative decline of about 22%), and a robustness check with social security registration data gives −11.0pp. [Direction multi-source confirmed—Spain's national minimum income IMV (AIReF 2025, administrative data, observational design) −3pp, France's RMI (Bargain & Doorley 2011, regression discontinuity) −7–10%; magnitude single-source verified] The methodology audit seat rejected two popular sub-claims: "the negative effect doubles at a 100% withdrawal rate" holds only in the social security data and has no multiple-testing correction; "caregivers respond most strongly" is called tentative by the authors themselves and is not robust across data sources. In addition, it was not preregistered, and a decline in employment does not mean people stopped working—the benefit is calculated on declared income, so moving work out of sight is also an explanation.
  • Is the withdrawal rate the key moderating variable? Direction contested: the randomized comparison within B-MINCOME is significant in only one data source; after Spain's IMV introduced an earnings disregard in 2023 (equivalent to lowering the withdrawal rate), the negative effect for new enrollees did not ease.

The conclusion of this section: both "handing out money breeds idlers" and "handing out money doesn't affect work" are too simple. The evidence from low-income countries as a whole supports the latter; in rich countries, unconditional money leads people to work an hour or two less a week, and means-tested assistance with a withdrawal rate suppresses employment more clearly.

2.4 Where Does the Freed-Up Time Go?

ORUS's smartphone time diaries show that the time freed by working less mainly goes to leisure, and they can rule out even small increases in childcare, exercise, job search, or self-improvement. The methodology audit seat downgraded this to a weak conclusion: the q-values for the rise in leisure in the diaries are only 0.16–0.24, and the time diaries have the highest differential attrition of all the data sources. The German trial only partly echoes this (time with friends, family and partners +3.8 hours/week, about 1 hour more sleep), while job-search time actually fell significantly. [Weak conclusion; single-source] "Given money, people will go study, start businesses, or care for family" is not supported by existing high-income-country trials; neither is "people will give up"—what is observed is a small, ordinary increase in leisure.

3. Health and Well-Being: What Did the Money Buy?

First, separate the three rulers. The OECD 2013 Guidelines on Measuring Subjective Well-being divide it into three categories: evaluation of life (life satisfaction, "overall, how satisfied are you with your life"), experience of life (affect, whether you were happy or sad yesterday), and meaning and purpose (eudaimonic). Mental-health scales (such as the GHQ-12 and depression scales) are a fourth thing again. The cash-transfer literature almost never measures "meaning." For every number in this section, first ask which ruler it is.

3.1 Rich Countries: Improvements Are Short-Lived, Invisible by the Second Year

  • ORUS health: stress and food security improved substantially but briefly; use of hospitals, emergency rooms and dental care rose, with medical spending about +$20/month; there was no improvement in self-reported physical health or blood-test biomarkers; mental health showed no improvement after the first year, and over the pooled period improvements larger than 0.03 SD can be ruled out. [Single-source verified; the health paper is still a working paper]
  • Baby's First Years (1,000 mothers below the poverty line, $333 a month vs. $20, 4 years): all four preregistered primary child-development outcomes were zero; there was no improvement in mothers' depression, anxiety, or children's health; the EEG differences reported at age 1 were not replicated in the age-4 follow-up. [Single-source verified]
  • Stockton SEED ($500/month × 24 months, 131 vs. 200): income volatility and mental distress fell (self-reported), but final retention was about 35% in the control group and about 55% in the treatment group—differential attrition is too large; the often-cited "full-time employment rate doubled" comes from the first-year preliminary report and is not significant in the final paper. [Downgraded]

Conclusion: in high-income countries, time-limited cash transfers improve the stress that comes from being "short of money," and once people adapt to the new income, no difference shows on mental-health scales. [Multi-source confirmed]

3.2 The Only Exception: The German Trial, and Why It Cannot Carry Weight

The German Mein Grundeinkommen trial (€1,200/month × 3 years, analysis sample of 107 recipients vs. 1,580 controls, DIW March 2026 version) reports: mental health +0.31 SD, meaning in life +0.25 SD, life satisfaction +0.43 SD, with about 90% of the composite index effect still present 18 months after payments stopped. It is the only cash RCT in this issue that directly measures "sense of meaning," and its result runs opposite to ORUS.

The methodology audit seat rated it B− and rejected 8 statements; the key ones:

  • The psychological outcomes were not in the preregistration (in the paper's words: "We did not include these survey-based measures in the preregistration"), so they can only be called exploratory results;
  • The main table uses change scores relative to baseline, and the treatment group's baseline was worse; switching to levels, meaning in life is only 0.122 SD (p=0.07, not significant), mental health 0.268, life satisfaction 0.335;
  • "90% retained after payments stopped" is a ratio of two point estimates, and the 95% interval for the 18-month wave is 0.15 to 0.57; it cannot be written as "the effect is lasting";
  • The sample is single-person households aged 21–40 with monthly net income of €1,100–2,600, 91% employed, excluding people on long-term unemployment assistance—this is giving extra money to people who are working, and cannot be used to show that "people who don't work live well when provided for";
  • All outcomes are self-reported with the treatment group aware of its status, so demand effects (answering more positively because you know you are receiving money) cannot be ruled out. The funder, Mein Grundeinkommen e.V., is also an advocate; DIW states that design and analysis were conducted independently, and this article does not discount it for that.

[Direction contested; exploratory single source] The most direct counter-evidence found by the counter-evidence search seat is ORUS itself; the authors also acknowledge that the contemporaneous high-income-country literature contains no evidence of lasting mental-health improvement.

3.3 Permanent Windfalls: Satisfaction Rises Persistently, Emotion Doesn't Move

Lotteries are the closest natural experiment available today to "a permanent, unconditional, large enough sum of money." Lindqvist, Östling, Cesarini 2020 (REStud, Sweden, 5–22 years after winning, N=3,362, preregistered):

  • Each $100,000 in prize money raises life satisfaction by +0.037 SD, with no decay more than a decade later—there is no "complete adaptation" for life satisfaction;
  • The main mediator is financial satisfaction (+0.067 SD);
  • Happiness (0.016) and GHQ-12 mental health (0.013) are both not significant, and "the happiness effect equals the life-satisfaction effect" can be rejected.

[Single-source verified (preregistered)] The same paper also re-evaluated three earlier studies reporting that "winning improves mental health": their statistical power was only about 5–6.5%, so their direction may be wrong and their effects may be exaggerated. The popular Brickman 1978 claim—"lottery winners are no happier than ordinary people, and paraplegics recover to being just as happy"—has problems in both halves: the sample had only 22 winners, and accident victims' current happiness (2.96) was significantly lower than controls' (3.82), so "just as happy" is a misreading; the figures are available only from the compilation in the World Database of Happiness. [Secondhand]

This agrees with the latest consensus on the income–emotion curve: Kahneman & Deaton 2010 found that emotion plateaus above annual income of about $75,000, Killingsworth 2021 found no plateau, and the three authors' 2023 adversarial collaboration reconciled them: the plateau appears only among the least happy portion of people, and the effect of income on life evaluation keeps rising. [Multi-source confirmed]

3.4 Low- and Middle-Income Countries: Effective, but Effects Recede When the Money Stops

McGuire et al. 2022 (Nature Human Behaviour, 45 studies in LMICs, about 117,000 people): subjective well-being d=0.13, mental health d=0.07, with transfer size the strongest predictor. [Multi-source confirmed (LMIC)] The first author is from the Happier Lives Institute, and the paper declares no dedicated funding.

Do the effects decay? It depends on whether money is still being paid at follow-up:

  • The effects of Malawi's unconditional transfer on HIV, teenage pregnancy and early marriage "rapidly evaporated" two years after payments stopped (lasting education effects appeared only for the conditional transfer, and only for girls who had already dropped out at baseline);
  • Uganda's YOP business grants raised income +38% at 4 years; by 9 years the control group had caught up; the 12-year follow-up (during the pandemic) partly reproduced about +22%, only for men and only marginally significant;
  • Kenya, Haushofer & Shapiro three-year follow-up: the often-cited "depression still 0.16 SD lower after three years" is actually a composite psychological well-being index with a within-village comparison, a measure the authors say explicitly is biased upward; switching to a clean cross-village comparison, only assets are significant (+40%), and consumption among non-recipient households in the same villages was actually lower. [Single-source verified; corrected in Round 2]

Permanent UBI is precisely what has no measured data. In ongoing programs, payments are mostly still being made at follow-up, so effects look "non-decaying"; once payments stop, the effects disappear or the control group catches up.

4. Meaning: The Non-Income Functions of Work

4.1 Marienthal and Jahoda's Five Functions

In 1930 the textile factory in the Austrian village of Marienthal closed, and nearly the entire village became unemployed at once. Jahoda, Lazarsfeld and Zeisel's 1933 study pioneered the hour-by-hour time-use sheet; one man wrote in the 10–11 a.m. slot "…meanwhile it gets to be noon" (German original); the conclusion was "the weary community": resignation, loss of the capacity to act, and depoliticization. [Single-source verified (as relayed by the archive); original book not obtained]

Jahoda later built on this to propose latent deprivation theory: besides earning money, work has five latent functions—"time structure, an enlarged social network, participation in collective efforts, definition of social identity, and required regular activity" (Jahoda 1997 as quoted in Paul 2005). [Secondhand quotation] Her opponent Fryer argued that the harm comes mainly from poverty and uncertainty restricting agency. The two have opposite implications for UBI: if Fryer is right, permanent UBI can make up for most of it; if Jahoda is right, it cannot.

Note that Marienthal itself cannot settle this debate: unemployment relief at the time was meager, so income and latent functions were mixed together. In 2025 Kasy & Lehner ran a randomized job-guarantee experiment on the original Marienthal site; its direction supports Jahoda, but it likewise cannot separate income from latent functions. [Unverified: added by the counter-evidence lens in Round 2, not checked verbatim]

4.2 How Big Is the Harm of Unemployment, and Can People Adapt?

  • Magnitude: Paul 2005 (the working draft behind Paul & Moser 2009) cross-sectional meta-analysis: the mental health of the unemployed is worse than that of the employed by d=0.51 (after removing outliers, k=315, n=209,379); in longitudinal studies, distress rises by d=0.19 when the employed become unemployed, and falls by d=−0.35 when the unemployed are re-employed. [Single-source verified (meta)]
  • Almost no adaptation: the German SOEP panel (Lucas et al. 2004; Clark et al. 2008) shows that the life satisfaction of the unemployed recovers somewhat but on average does not fully recover, even after re-employment; in Clark et al. 2008's leads-and-lags model, people adapt to marriage, divorce, widowhood and childbirth, but hardly adapt at all to being unemployed (the published version is restricted to men). [Multi-source confirmed (life satisfaction)]
  • Weaker under rigorous identification: studies that deal with endogeneity find smaller effects (Picchio & Ubaldi 2023 meta); Schmitz 2011, likewise identified through plant closures, found a zero effect on clinical health. So these must be written separately: life satisfaction falls robustly; on mental-health scales the effect is about half a standard deviation in observational studies and small under rigorous identification; clinical health results contradict each other. [Direction contested (causal magnitude for health)]

4.3 How Much Can Money Make Up? The Direction Is Clear; the Magnitude Can't Be Quoted Literally

Winkelmann & Winkelmann 1998, using an SOEP sample of men, estimated that the non-pecuniary cost of unemployment is far larger than the income loss. [Multi-source confirmed (direction)] But the circulating "it takes 7 times the income to compensate" is a misreading—in 0.96/0.13≈7.4, the unit is log income; converted literally it is about 1,600 times. Numbers like this are all affected by attenuation of the income coefficient, which systematically exaggerates non-pecuniary costs; this article does not cite any "N times income" figure.

The buffering role of benefits: Di Tella et al. 2003 found that more generous unemployment benefits make everyone happier, but the gap between the employed and the unemployed does not narrow; Wulfgramm 2014 found that when benefits are meager the harm almost doubles; the McKee-Ryan 2005 meta found that benefit amounts do not moderate the harm. The four pieces of evidence point in different directions, but none shows the harm going to zero. [Direction contested (magnitude); multi-source confirmed (not zero)] Note that these are all time-limited, conditional, stigmatized unemployment insurance, not permanent unconditional UBI.

4.4 The Key Turn: Not Working ≠ Unemployment

If the harm comes mainly from "having no job" itself, then everyone who doesn't work should be equally harmed. The data do not support this:

  • Winkelmann 1998: the negative effect for people outside the labor force (not looking for work) is significantly smaller than for the unemployed.
  • Paul et al. 2023 meta: retirees lack latent functions (routine, social contact, collective goals) almost as much as the unemployed, yet are not nearly as distressed.
  • Knabe et al. 2010 (Berlin and Magdeburg, day reconstruction method): the life satisfaction of the long-term unemployed is far below that of full-time employees (4.58 vs. 7.11), but duration-weighted daily net affect is almost identical (4.24 vs. 4.23)—they spend the time others spend at work on more pleasant activities. [Single-source verified; response rate in the unemployed group 15%; in the US, Krueger & Mueller found the unemployed sadder over the course of a day; the two differ in scales and countries, direction contested]
  • Hetschko, Knabe, Schöb 2014 (SOEP): when the long-term unemployed move into retirement, life satisfaction rises from 6.12 to 6.47 (n=696), while for the employed it is unchanged before and after retirement (7.17→7.16).

The last item is the key pivot this issue uses to extrapolate the unemployment evidence to "society as a whole no longer needs labor," so it received a two-seat audit. The outcome: the fact holds; the mechanism doesn't.

  • The counter-evidence search seat found a same-direction result from an independent team using independent data (Ponomarenko et al. 2017, multi-country European SHARE data, a "catching-up effect"), upgrading it to multi-source confirmed, but with three qualifications: the effect is significant only for men; the rebound may be short-lived (Schmälzle et al. 2018); and the scars of unemployment persist after retirement.
  • The methodology audit seat rated the interpretation "this is an identity/norm effect" D: at the same time, income rose 6.2%, income volatility halved (time-limited benefits were replaced by a lifetime pension), and the job-search obligation was lifted, and none of the three can be separated from "switching into a socially approved category"; moreover, after retirement they still score about 0.7 points below the employed.
  • "The more unemployed people around you, the less unemployment hurts" (Clark 2003) was downgraded: Oesch & Lipps (Germany, Switzerland), Chadi 2014, and Hajdu 2023 (European Social Survey) do not replicate it or find the opposite direction; the regional unemployment rate looks more like a measure of how good the labor market is than of norms.
  • "The stronger the work norm, the more unemployment hurts" (Stutzer & Lalive): direction contested; there is cross-country support, and also counter-evidence directly measuring work norms in 45 countries (Stam et al. 2016).

[Direction: multi-source confirmed (men, possibly short-term); mechanism: unverified]

Retirement research itself does not give a clean answer either: causal studies of retirement's effects on cognition, health and mental health point in different directions; the Filomena & Picchio 2023 meta (85 papers, 308 estimates) gives an average effect that is small and barely significant, with involuntary retirement a small negative effect. [Direction contested] As for "would you keep working if you won a big jackpot," the only survey of actual winners is Arvey et al. 2004: 85.5% kept working, but with only 117 people and a response rate of about 16%. [Single-source verified (weak)]

4.5 This Chapter's Conclusion

The harm of unemployment has at least three pieces: income (UBI can make it up), latent functions (routine, social contact, collective goals; UBI can't make them up, but the retiree data show people can get them elsewhere), and identity and norms (the cost of "should work but doesn't"). The third piece is shown to vary in today's data, but there is no reliable independent estimate of how large it is; whether it would disappear when "all of society doesn't work" would require a natural experiment that does not exist today—regional unemployment rates top out at a dozen or so percentage points, retirement only happens to older people, and the people around them are still working.

Self-determination theory (the three needs of competence, autonomy and relatedness can be met in many domains) leaves theoretical room for "living well without working"; Amodei has written "meaning comes mostly from human relationships and connection, not from economic labor". Both of these are currently theory or statements of position, not measurements.

5. Bargaining Power: Citizens Who Are Not Needed

5.1 The Rentier State: A Proposition Much Narrower Than the Slogan

Rentier state theory starts from a fiscal definition: the state "regularly receives substantial amounts of external rent," rather than relying on taxing its own citizens (Mahdavy 1970; this issue obtained it only as quoted in a book review). Beblawi 1987's original text is: "With virtually no taxes, citizens are far less demanding in terms of political participation."—the parenthetical cites the forward slogan "no taxation without representation"; the widely circulated reversed slogan "no representation without taxation" is not in this text. [Single-source verified (verbatim); source of the reversed slogan unverified]

After twenty years of debate, the cross-national empirical work on the oil curse has narrowed its conclusion considerably:

  • Ross 2001 (113 countries, 1971–97): oil dependence has a negative effect on democracy; but Lall 2017, redoing it with multiple imputation, shrank the coefficient to about one fifth, and adding regional dummies halved it again and made it lose significance.
  • Haber & Menaldo 2011: using within-country time series from 1800 onward, no curse is visible. Andersen & Ross 2014's response: the curse only appeared after about 1980 (original sentence in the abstract: "since about 1980, there has been a pronounced resource curse").
  • Wiens et al. 2014 (166 countries, 1816–2006): resource dependence lowers the probability that authoritarian states democratize, and has "no appreciable effect" on the survival of existing democracies.
  • Ahmadov's meta: average partial correlation about −0.07, with large regional and institutional heterogeneity.

[Direction contested (conditional)] The version that holds up is: after the nationalizations of the 1970s, oil made it harder for authoritarian states to democratize. It does not support "mature democracies will lose bargaining power because of rents." The contrasting cases of Norway and Botswana point to the same moderating variable: whether institutions were already mature when the rents arrived, and who controls the rents.

5.2 Did Taxation Really Buy Accountability?

  • Weigel 2020 (Kananga, Congo; property tax collection randomized across 356 neighborhoods): the tax compliance rate rose from 0.1% to 11.6%, and political participation rose by about 5 percentage points. What it measures is "taxation makes people participate"; you cannot symmetrically infer "not taxing makes people apathetic." [Single-source verified (RCT)]
  • Paler 2013 (Indonesia): tax framing made people monitor incumbents more; but once given spending information, the windfall group cared just as much as the tax group. The treatment was an information frame, not actual taxation.
  • de la Cuesta et al.: in 6 experiments in Ghana and Uganda in 2021, aid and oil revenue did not significantly lower demand for accountability; in 2022 they found that as long as people were given a sense of ownership over oil revenue, demand for accountability was the same as with taxes.

[Direction contested] The individual-level evidence for "no taxes, no accountability" is mixed.

5.3 Historical Leverage: The State Needs Your Body

Scheve & Stasavage, using a century of data, found that progressive income taxes and inheritance taxes rose significantly after mass war mobilization; "In the absence of war, neither the establishment of universal suffrage, nor the arrival of political control by parties of the left is systematically associated with large increases in tax progressivity." [Single-source verified (econometric)] Przeworski 2009 found that class-based extensions of the franchise were "fought for" from below. Acemoglu & Robinson 2000's formal model explains franchise extension as a commitment device by elites to prevent revolution (argumentative).

This is exactly what makes the AI scenario worrying: historically, ordinary people's bargaining leverage was that the state needed to conscript you to fight its wars, hire you to produce, and tax you to fund itself. If none of these three needs ordinary people anymore, where does the leverage come from? This is reasonable mechanistic reasoning. But it is reasoning; no country has experienced it today.

5.4 Does Distributing Rents Directly to Citizens Depoliticize Them?

The Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend is the case closest to "resource rents → universal unconditional dividend." Two studies (Loeffler 2022; James, Rivera, Smith 2025) both find higher turnout in years with larger dividends. The outcome of the two-seat audit:

  • "Dividends don't depoliticize people" upgraded to multi-source confirmed: in addition, adult recipients of Cherokee casino dividends (several thousand dollars a year, permanent and unconditional) show a zero effect on turnout, ruling out ±2.5 percentage points; ORUS has zero effects on political participation, trust in government and support for democracy across the board, and the treatment group actually considered work more important to individuals and society.
  • "Dividends raise turnout" direction contested: the positive effect has only one case, Alaska; the two papers study the same state and the same policy, so they are not two independent pieces of evidence; Loeffler's state-level +8.3 to +18.2pp has only one treated unit, and parallel trends are nearly impossible to test; "a 10% larger dividend, +1.4pp turnout in gubernatorial elections" has p=0.082 after dropping two years; since 1993 the PFD application form has included a voter registration form, which neither paper controls for. The methodology audit seat rated it C+ and rejected 6 statements.
  • Accountability and policy responsiveness were not measured: what reliably shows up in cash programs such as Mexico's Progresa and Uruguay's PANES is "the incumbent who hands out money gains votes," which rewards whoever pays, not a demand for accountability.

There is also a lead running the other way that matters more than turnout: Alaska's dividend is not in the constitution. The constitution only provides that mineral revenues go into the Permanent Fund; in 2016 the dividend was cut by the governor's veto to $1,022; in 2017 the state supreme court ruled that the dividend "must compete for annual legislative funding just as other state programs"; in 2025 the legislature set it at $1,000. [Single-source verified (ruling/official)] The people receiving the dividend voted more actively, yet still could not preserve the statutory formula. The difference between an "entitlement" and a "handout," even in America's only universal permanent precedent, is just a thin sheet of paper.

6. The Intelligence Curse: Mechanism and Testable Signals

Two argumentative works have carried rentier state theory over to AI:

  • The Intelligence Curse (Drago & Laine 2025): "With AGI, powerful actors will lose their incentive to invest in regular people–just as resource-rich states today neglect their citizens because their wealth comes from natural resources rather than taxing human labor."
  • Gradual Disempowerment (Kulveit et al. 2025): "if AI automation leads to citizens becoming primarily dependent on state support rather than contributing through taxes, it weakens the historical 'taxation-representation' relationship that has been crucial for maintaining democratic accountability."

Both quotations were checked verbatim. [Unverified (argumentative)] This article gives only their mechanisms and testable signals, and does not treat their conclusions as fact:

  1. Their empirical premises are narrower than the originals state. "Resource states neglect their citizens" is conditional in the empirical work (after the 1970s, authoritarian states); "dependence on state support weakens accountability" conflicts with Alaska, the ownership-sense experiments, and null results in many countries—the key is the form of distribution: an annual appropriation by the state, or a share held by citizens.
  2. The signal the Intelligence Curse itself proposes is flawed. It says: when a state's fiscal revenue structure looks more like Norway (about 30% from oil), Saudi Arabia (75%) or the DRC than like the OECD average, the curse is taking effect. This signal puts Norway into the "curse" sample and cannot distinguish good outcomes from bad ones; the authors themselves acknowledge that "democracy is likely a necessary precondition".
  3. AI rents differ from oil rents in three ways, which no one in the literature has tested: after the 1970s, oil was mostly state-owned, immobile, and could be paid out directly as dividends by citizenship; AI rents are in the hands of private multinational companies, the tax base can move, and states compete on taxes.
  4. The only critique of Gradual Disempowerment found (Bentham's Bulldog & Halstead, September 2026) attacks the point that "delegation is not disempowerment," and does not respond to the taxation–representation channel; the Intelligence Curse's rentier analogy currently has no formal academic critique.

The testable signals this article can offer are in Section 10.

7. Who Is Talking: Vendor and Advocate Claims

All tech leaders' statements on UBI are treated uniformly as vendor claims: the direction can be taken as a reference, the degree cannot carry weight, and the speaker should be read together with who benefits.

  • Sam Altman (OpenAI): his 2021 "Moore's Law for Everything" proposed an American Equity Fund—an annual levy of 2.5% of market value on companies above a certain valuation (paid in shares) and 2.5% on privately held land—predicting "a decade from now each of the 250 million adults in America would get about $13,500 every year," and describing it himself as "in the spirit of a conversation starter". In May 2024 on the All-In podcast (spoken, per auto-generated captions) he said the future might be "more like Universal basic compute than Universal basic income", "everybody gets like a slice of gpt7 compute". His 2025 blog wrote "giving some 'compute budget' to enable everyone on Earth to use a lot of AI", while also saying "just relentlessly driving the cost of intelligence as low as possible has the desired effect". [Vendor claim] The vehicle for redistribution has shifted from cash and shares to his own company's product.
  • Elon Musk (Tesla, xAI): in 2016 he told CNBC "There is a pretty good chance we end up with a universal basic income, or something like that, due to automation" (a prediction). At VivaTech in May 2024 (spoken, per auto-generated captions of a video uploaded by a third party) he spoke of "Universal High income" in the benign scenario, and estimated that scenario at about 80% likely. In April 2026 he posted on X (per Business Insider via Yahoo; original post around 2026-04-16) that universal high income paid by federal government checks is the best way to deal with AI unemployment, and asserted it would not be inflationary (an advocacy statement). None of the three came with a mechanism, and the money comes from the government, not from Tesla or xAI. [Vendor claim]
  • Mark Zuckerberg: 2017 Harvard commencement speech: "We should explore ideas like universal basic income to give everyone a cushion to try new things."—about a cushion for taking risks, not about unemployment. [Vendor claim]
  • Dario Amodei (Anthropic): in 2024 he described a large UBI as "only be a small part of a solution"; his January 2026 long essay wrote "The natural policy response ... is progressive taxation. The tax could be general or could be targeted against AI companies in particular", and called all the measures "ways to buy time"; the co-founders pledged to give away 80% of their wealth (private philanthropy, not public transfers). He is the only person in this issue to propose taxing his own industry, but he gave no tax rate. The circulating "3% token tax" is Axios's paraphrase; the only words inside quotation marks are "goes to the government and is redistributed in some way." [Vendor claim]
  • Advocates: Andrew Yang's Freedom Dividend is $1,000 a month funded by a 10% VAT, and existing benefit recipients "would not receive both" (which programs can be stacked could not be verified verbatim in this issue); Chris Hughes's plan gives $500 a month to workers (including caregivers and students) with annual income below $50,000, through the EITC channel—it is not UBI; the Economic Security Project is both an advocate and a donor to Stockton SEED; GiveDirectly is both implementer and advocate.

None of the three tech leaders has made a commitment with an amount and a timetable.

The opposing side's primary texts also deserve a clear look: legislation passed in 2024 in Idaho, South Dakota and Iowa bars local governments from running "guaranteed income programs," each defined as "regular, unconditional cash payments", and explicitly exempts programs that require work, job search or training. What is banned is "unconditional," not "giving money." Public attitudes: Pew 2020 asked about "the federal government providing about $1,000 a month to all adult citizens—whether or not they work": 54% opposed, 45% supported (78% of Republicans opposed, 66% of Democrats supported; asked of only half the sample, N=5,501); with the framing "if machines could do many human jobs," Pew 2017 found 60% support. Numbers from different questions cannot be placed side by side.

The institutional economists' warning is the most sober: Hoynes & Rothstein 2019 estimate that giving every adult $12,000 a year would cost about $3 trillion, "about 75 percent of current total federal expenditures"; UBI would shift more money toward childless, non-elderly, middle-income households; and existing pilots "will do little to resolve the major outstanding questions". [Single-source verified]

10. Nine Testable Claims

Ordered by strength of evidence:

  1. In high-income countries, unconditional cash lowers hours by 1–2 hours a week but does not lead most people to leave the labor force. Test: the next preregistered high-income-country RCT with a sample ≥1,000 and administrative data, reporting both the hours and participation effects with their intervals. (ORUS, Germany and lotteries agree in direction)
  2. Means-tested assistance with a high withdrawal rate suppresses employment more than an unconditional transfer of the same amount. Test: randomize the withdrawal rate (0% vs. 50% vs. 100%) within the same trial and compare using administrative employment data. (B-MINCOME saw this in only one data source)
  3. Time-limited trials underestimate the labor effects of permanent schemes. Test: randomize the payment duration (a 3-year vs. a 10-year commitment) and compare first-year responses. (The 1970s experiments give one instance, husbands only)
  4. Cash transfers in rich countries improve stress and food security, but the mental-health effects fade within a year. Test: the formal version of the ORUS health paper and a preregistered replication of the German trial, using the same set of scales.
  5. Money raises life evaluation, not day-to-day emotion. Test: in any new cash RCT, measure all three rulers at once—life satisfaction, day-reconstruction emotion, and sense of meaning—preregistering "the three effects are not equal."
  6. Part of the non-pecuniary harm of unemployment comes from identity and job-search obligations, not only from not working. Test: use institutional discontinuities such as the exemption from job-search obligations above age 58 to separate the effects of "lifting the job-search obligation" and "retiree identity."
  7. When an entire community receives money at once, labor and norm responses differ from those under dispersed sampling. Test: run saturated randomization (whole villages/whole communities) and dispersed sampling in parallel in the same country, comparing participation and satisfaction. (Kenya randomized at the village level, but in an LMIC)
  8. When resource or AI rents are distributed as "shares held by citizens," demand for accountability is no lower than with taxation; when distributed as "annual appropriations," they are more easily cut politically. Test: compare the survival rates of statutory-formula dividends and annual-appropriation dividends under fiscal pressure, and the accountability behavior of beneficiaries.
  9. If the intelligence curse is happening, the first signal to show will be government spending tilting toward capital and security, with public investment in ordinary people declining. Test: track the shares of education and health spending and civil-liberties indices in countries where the share of AI revenue is rising (not all resource-revenue countries), compared with control countries; take care to exclude Norway-style "rents + mature democracy" combinations. (Argumentative, no data yet)

Appendix: Method and This Issue's Self-Corrections

During the research, this issue overturned or narrowed several popular formulations that we ourselves had initially accepted:

  • ORUS "labor participation −2.0pp" (early 2024 version) → three measures −2.3 / −6.6 / −4.2pp, with −4.2 being the after-the-fact combined measure;
  • Kenya "recipients felt secure enough not to take wage jobs, pushing wages up" → the wage increase came mainly from demand expansion, and reservation wages rose only about 3%;
  • "Depression still 0.16 SD lower after three years" → composite index, within-village comparison, which the authors say explicitly is biased upward;
  • German trial "the increase in sense of meaning appeared with a delay" → what was delayed was job satisfaction; the level of sense of meaning is not significant;
  • "It takes 7 times the income to compensate for unemployment" → log units, cannot be quoted literally;
  • "If everyone is unemployed, it doesn't hurt" → does not replicate;
  • "The unemployed recover as soon as they retire, purely because they switched identity" → income security and job-search obligations changed at the same time; the mechanisms cannot be separated;
  • "Oil means the state doesn't need its citizens, so democracy will decay" → after the 1970s, obstructing democratization in authoritarian states;
  • "Alaska proves dividends raise turnout" → single treated unit; all one can say is "no depoliticization";
  • Dauphin "hospitalization rate −8.5%" → a two-way dispute with Green 2022's re-estimate, unresolved;
  • "About 80% of people would keep working even after winning a big jackpot" → no primary source, discarded;
  • Amodei "3% token tax" → Axios's paraphrase;
  • "No representation without taxation" from Beblawi → the original parenthetical has the forward slogan.

Primary documents this issue could not obtain: the original 1933 Marienthal book, the original texts of Jahoda 1981/1982 (the five functions are available only as quoted), the original Morse & Weiss 1955, the original PDF of Brickman 1978, the original chapter of Mahdavy 1970, the original pages of Huntington 1991, Finland's 2020 final evaluation report, the main text of Ridley 2020's 2024 erratum, the decay coefficient in the formal version of McGuire 2022, ORUS's IRS 990 tax forms (funding amounts), the original text of the stacking clause in Yang's campaign FAQ, the original text of Tesla's compensation plan (SEC EDGAR skipped under the network constraints), and Musk's original 2026 X post.
