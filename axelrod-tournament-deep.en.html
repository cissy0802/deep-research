Is Tit for Tat Still the Winner? Axelrod's Tournaments and Their Fate Forty Years On (Deep Dive)

This is the deep-dive edition · read the plain-language edition →
TL;DR
"Tit for tat won twice and took over the population" has to be checked piece by piece. As of October 2026, primary evidence shows: in the first tournament of 15 rules, TFT's 504.5 beat second place by just 0.82%, and changing the mutual-defection payoff from 1 to 2 drops it to fourth — the big gap was between nice rules (471.8–504.5) and the rest (best 401); the second tournament's "average 200 moves" was really five shared game lengths averaging 151, and a 2025 rerun of the original Fortran 25,000 times has TFT winning in only about 63% of seeds; in the ecological simulation TFT had the largest and still-growing share at generation 1000, while "went to fixation" was a 1981 extrapolation Axelrod walked back in 1988. Theory says cooperation can be sustained, but the folk theorem doesn't single out TFT; under noise TFT falls into retaliation echoes and the winners are more forgiving variants (generous, contrite), and in human experiments with noise TFT drops to 7%. Zero-determinant extortion wins every match but is eliminated by generous strategies in large populations. In 2004 Southampton entered 112 of 223 programs and colluded its way to the top three of the standard league. In today's large tournaments TFT sits mid-table, mostly behind its own refinements, and the winners still never defect first. LLM "vendor personalities" were vetoed by the methodology audit. 0. A Story Told for Forty Years

The popular version goes like this: in 1980, the political scientist Robert Axelrod invited experts from many fields to write programs to play the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, and the simplest one, "Tit for Tat" (TFT: cooperate on the first move, then copy whatever the opponent did on the previous move), won both tournaments; entrants in the second tournament knew it had won and set out specifically to beat it, and still lost; when these programs were run in an "ecological" evolutionary simulation, TFT eventually took over the entire population. Axelrod distilled four principles from this—be nice, retaliatory, forgiving and clear—and wrote them up in his 1984 book The Evolution of Cooperation, and so "nice guys finish first" became the most widely cited story in game theory.

There are five things in this story that can be checked against primary documents:

  1. How the two tournaments were actually designed, and by how much TFT won;
  2. Whether "taking over the population" was a simulation result or an extrapolation;
  3. Whether the four principles hold up in theory;
  4. How far later work—noise, zero-determinant strategies, collusive entries, large-scale open-source tournaments—has rewritten it;
  5. What the behavioral evidence from humans, animals, history and large language models supports.

The conclusion up front: TFT really did come first in total score in both tournaments, but it won narrowly and under very specific conditions; "taking over the population" is an extrapolation, and Axelrod himself later changed his wording; what has best survived forty years of testing is not this four-line program but its character of "don't defect first + retaliate conditionally"; in a noisy world, the winners are variants more forgiving than it.

1. The First Tournament: 15 Rules, a Champion by 4 Points

The first tournament had 14 submissions, from 3 countries and 5 disciplines (economics, mathematics, political science, psychology, and one sociologist), from 16 submitters in total (two entries were co-written by two people); the organizer added a rule that moved at random, RANDOM, for 15 rules in all. 【Single-source verified: Axelrod 1980a JCR original】 The format was a round robin: each rule played one game against every other rule, against its own twin, and against RANDOM; each game was exactly 200 moves, with payoffs R=3, T=5, S=0, P=1 (mutual cooperation gives 3 each, unilateral defection gives 5 to 0, mutual defection gives 1 each); the whole round robin was run 5 times, for 120,000 moves in total. 【Single-source verified; number of pairings C(15,2)+15=120, 120×200×5=120,000, Recomputed and consistent】

TFT was submitted by the psychologist Anatol Rapoport and averaged 504.5 points per game; second place, Tideman & Chieruzzi, scored 500.4, a gap of 4.1 points, or 0.82%; third place, Nydegger, scored 485.5. Benchmark: a game of mutual cooperation throughout is worth 600 points. 【Single-source verified】 The top eight were all "nice" (never the first to defect), and the remaining seven were all not nice; nice rules scored 471.8–504.5, while the best non-nice rule scored only 401. 【Single-source verified】 This is the hardest conclusion of the first tournament, harder than "TFT won": the gap between the champion and the runner-up was far smaller than the gap between nice and non-nice rules.

TFT's victory also depended heavily on the environment. Axelrod himself states three things in the original paper:

  • Change one payoff number, and the champion changes. Changing the mutual-defection payoff P from 1 to 2 makes Tideman & Chieruzzi the winner, and TFT drops to fourth. 【Single-source verified: 1980a footnote 3】
  • The ranking was decided by two "kingmaker" rules. The order among the top eight depended mainly on how they scored against two rules, DOWNING and GRAASKAMP: the range of nice rules' scores against RANDOM was only 157, against DOWNING it reached 443, and against GRAASKAMP it was 290. 【Single-source verified; these two decided the relative ranking among the top eight, not whether a rule was nice】
  • Programs that weren't entered could have won. "Tit for Two Tats" (TF2T, which retaliates once only after two consecutive defections by the opponent), placed into the environment of these 15 rules, averages 532 points, higher than TFT's 504.5; REVISED DOWNING (542) and LOOK AHEAD (520) would also have won. Ironically, TF2T was the very sample program the organizer had sent to all potential entrants, yet no one submitted it. 【Single-source verified; this is a counterfactual calculation within the given environment, not a rerun of a tournament】

Three more popular details:

  • "TFT is only four lines of code": holds, measured as four lines of FORTRAN; it was the shortest program submitted, with RANDOM and JOSS at five lines each and DAVIS at six. 【Single-source verified】
  • "TFT never wins a single game": holds, and it does so by construction—TFT only defects after the opponent defects, so its score in a single game can never exceed its opponent's. In the original's example game, TFT vs. JOSS was 236:241, and the five-game average was 225:230, behind by exactly 5 = T−S. 【Single-source verified】
  • "TFT was a dark horse": needs correction. Entrants had received a report on a preliminary tournament beforehand, in which TFT came second, and many people built on TFT to try to improve it. 【Single-source verified: 1980a】

2. The Second Tournament: 63 Rules, One Reused Set of Game Lengths

The second tournament had 62 entries from 6 countries, plus RANDOM, for 63 rules; most entrants were computer hobbyists, but there were also professors of evolutionary biology, physics and computer science, as well as the five disciplines of the first tournament. 【Single-source verified】 All entrants received the detailed analysis of the first tournament, including rules such as TF2T that "could have won." 【Single-source verified】 The popular version says "the entrants all wanted to beat TFT"—the original contains no such sentence; this is an inference by those retelling it. 【Unverified】 Rapoport submitted TFT again and won again; TF2T was submitted this time by the evolutionary biologist John Maynard Smith. 【Single-source verified: Axelrod-Python documentation; the current Wikipedia version attributes TF2T to Axelrod himself, which is wrong】

The biggest change in definitions was the termination rule. The first tournament had a fixed 200 moves, so entrants could defect on the last move; the second instead ended the game after each move with probability 0.00346 (continuation probability w=0.99654), with the aim of making the median game length 200. 【Single-source verified】 But "an average of 200 moves" is wrong: with this w, the median is ln0.5/ln0.99654≈200, while the expectation is 1/(1−w)≈289. 【Recomputed】 Moreover, game lengths were not in fact drawn independently for each game—Axelrod drew 5 game lengths in advance, shared by all pairings: 1980b lists four of them, 63, 77, 151 and 308, and the fifth, back-calculated from the reported average of 151, is 156. 【Single-source verified: the actual average of 151 moves is independently corroborated by the original Wu & Axelrod 1995 text, "averaging 151 moves"】 In other words, all 63×63 games of the second tournament were played at only five game lengths.

1980b also reports two often-cited things for which this issue could not obtain the primary original: all but one of the top 15 were nice; and Axelrod constructed 6 "representative" variant tournaments (each heavily over-representing one class of rules), of which TFT won 5. 【Unverified: only lecture notes and quoted book sentences】

Can this tournament be reproduced? In 2025, Knight et al. rebuilt the second tournament from the Fortran source code Axelrod left behind and reran it 25,000 times (the paper was published in 2026 in Communications AI & Computing). The conclusion comes in two halves: the exact results Axelrod reported cannot be reproduced, but TFT is still first—winning in only about 63% of random seeds, with about 31% taken by k42r instead. 【Single-source verified: the same paper gives 63.3% in the text and 0.6319 in a table; k42r is 31.4% in a table and 35% in the discussion, inconsistent within the paper】 The code of the runner-up, Champion, contained an uninitialized variable; once fixed, it ranks only 12th in the reproduction; the authors cannot say whether this bug affected the results at the time. 【Single-source verified】 Add 1% noise, and the ranking is reshuffled: k42r drops to 10th, yet is already ranked ahead of TFT; the authors' summary is that the original tournament was "especially favorable to TFT." 【Single-source verified】

3. The Ecological Simulation: "Going to Fixation" Is an Extrapolation, Not a Result

This is the strongest sentence in the popular story, and the one this issue rejected most thoroughly.

Axelrod used the 63 rules of the second tournament for a "future generations" simulation: in each generation, the rules' shares were redistributed in proportion to their scores in the previous generation. Axelrod and Hamilton wrote in Science in 1981: "In the long run, TIT FOR TAT displaced all the other rules and went to fixation". 【Single-source verified: Science 211:1390 original】 But note 24 to this sentence cites 1980b, and 1980b, according to secondary citation, reports only up to the 1,000th generation: at that point TFT held about 14.5%, the largest and fastest-growing share of any rule (about 0.05% per generation), "never losing its slight lead". 【Single-source verified: via the citation in Ostrom's 2007 biography; the original 1980b p.401 not seen】 At this growth rate, the 1,000th generation is still very far from "fixation"—"fixation" is a long-run extrapolation of the trend.

Two independent pieces of material point in the same direction:

  • Axelrod himself, in 1988 (with Dion, Science), changed his wording to "TFT quickly became the most common strategy", no longer saying fixation. 【Single-source verified: original】
  • The abstract of Hirshleifer & Martinez Coll 1988 (JCR) directly rebuts it: TFT "does not approach 100% fixation", and interior equilibria arise in which cooperative and non-cooperative strategies coexist. 【Single-source verified】

Two numbers often brought in as support cannot be placed alongside "the 1,000th generation": Binmore's "TFT controls only a little more than 1/6 of the population" comes from a different set of simulations in which Axelrod and Linster changed it to an infinitely repeated version, and after Linster swept over initial conditions, TFT had the largest share in only about 1/4 of cases; Hoffmann 2000's "reciprocal cooperators make up about 75%" counts a whole class of rules, not TFT alone, and gives no citation. 【Direction contested】

The methodology audit seat also pointed out a more fundamental problem: this is closed replicator dynamics over 63 fixed rules, with no mutation and no entry of new strategies, so what it tests is "who reproduces fastest among this batch of opponents," not "who can resist invasion." TFT being an evolutionarily stable strategy cannot be inferred from it. 【Methodology veto: treating "taking over/fixation" as fact, and inferring ESS from the ecological simulation, may not carry weight】

The version that can safely be written: in the ecological simulation of the second tournament's rules, TFT stayed in the lead from start to finish and, by the 1,000th generation, had the largest share and was still growing, but it did not take over the world—what survived was a batch of "nice" reciprocal rules.

4. Where the Four Principles Come From, and What Theory Says

The Evolution of Cooperation contains two sets of formulations that are often conflated: Chapter 2 attributes TFT's success to four properties—nice (doesn't defect first), retaliatory (provocable), forgiving (forgives after retaliating), and clear (understandable to opponents); Chapter 6 gives readers four pieces of advice—don't be envious, don't be the first to defect, reciprocate both cooperation and defection, and don't be too clever. 【Unverified: both sets only from secondary quotations; Science 1981 lists only the first three, without clear】 The original advice in Chapter 6 carries a qualifier—"in a durable iterated Prisoner's Dilemma"—which is almost always dropped in retellings.

Stability. The condition given in Science 1981 is: when the probability w of meeting again is large enough, namely w ≥ (T−R)/(T−P) and w ≥ (T−R)/(R−S), TFT is "evolutionarily stable"; plugging in 5/3/1/0, the two thresholds are 1/2 and 2/3, so w ≥ 2/3. 【Single-source verified; Recomputed and consistent】 But their test ruled out only invaders that score strictly higher, and the book switched to the weaker "collectively stable." This is weaker than the standard evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS): a neutral strategy that scores the same as TFT, such as "always cooperate" (ALLC), can drift in at random. 【Multi-source confirmed】

Theory delivered three corrections in the late 1980s:

  • Boyd & Lorberbaum 1987 (Nature): in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, no pure strategy is an ESS. 【Single-source verified: abstract】
  • Boyd 1989: as long as the probability of error is always positive, a pure strategy can be an ESS—those that meet the conditions are ALLD and a modified TFT, not ordinary TFT. 【Single-source verified】
  • Bendor & Swistak 1995 (PNAS): under a weaker stability concept there are "too many" stable strategies, and stability by itself cannot single out TFT; but ranked by "how large an invasion it can resist," conditional cooperators are the most robust. 【Single-source verified】

More fundamental is the folk theorem: as long as w is large enough, any outcome that gives both players at least the mutual-defection payoff P can be an equilibrium. 【Single-source verified: Hilbe et al. 2018 review】 In other words, theory says "cooperation can be sustained," and never said "TFT is the answer." Binmore put it more strongly in a 1998 book review: that repeated games can support cooperation had been well known for more than 25 years before Axelrod, and retaliation is necessary only in pairwise interactions. 【Single-source verified: Binmore's book review; a reviewer's opinion】

5. Noise: TFT's Achilles' Heel

Axelrod later acknowledged that the original tournaments had no "noise"—no slips of the hand and no misreading of the opponent's move; he called this a "basic flaw" of simple reciprocity. 【Single-source verified】

The damage noise does to TFT can be calculated directly. Two TFTs play each other; one slips and defects once, the other retaliates on the next move, the first retaliates back, and the two fall into an alternation of "you cooperate while I defect, I cooperate while you defect"; unless a second error pulls them out, this echo continues indefinitely. In the long-run average, the two TFTs' score falls to (R+S+T+P)/4, which is 2.25 under 5/3/1/0, compared with 3 for mutual cooperation. 【Multi-source confirmed: Imhof 2007, Wu & Axelrod citing Molander; this 2.25 holds for any error rate less than 1, defined as the undiscounted long-run average; at 1% noise, an error occurs about once every 50 moves, and a single 151-move game does not reach this limit】 Molander 1985 concluded that TFT's performance "deteriorates sharply" at arbitrarily low noise levels, and that the remedy is to add generosity. 【Single-source verified: abstract】

How generous is right? Cooperating with probability q even after the opponent defects, the upper bound on q is q* = min{1−(T−R)/(R−S), (R−P)/(T−P)}; under 5/3/1/0, q* = 1/3, and in the "donation game" it is 1−c/b. 【Single-source verified: Hilbe et al. 2018 attribute it to Molander; Recomputed and consistent】 Nowak & Sigmund 1992 (Nature) observed in a noisy heterogeneous population that a small amount of TFT is a necessary condition for reciprocity to emerge, but it is only the "pivot, rather than the aim", and is subsequently replaced by the more generous GTFT. 【Single-source verified: abstract】

Axelrod's own noisy rerun. Wu & Axelrod 1995 placed the 63 rules of the second tournament into an environment with 1% noise, repeated it 20 times, and then added several new rules and scored them one at a time: GTFT with 10% generosity scored higher than all 63 original rules; "Contrite TFT" (CTFT, which after its own slip accepts one retaliation without striking back) ranked behind only 5 of them; Pavlov scored below 55 of them. In an ecological simulation of 67 rules over 2,000 generations, CTFT became the leader by the 1,000th generation and held 97% by the 2,000th; GTFT succeeded early and declined later; Pavlov fell below one in a million after about 100 generations. 【Single-source verified: author's PDF version; the paper does not give ordinary TFT's rank in the noisy tournament】

"Win-stay, lose-shift" (WSLS/Pavlov). In the evolutionary simulations of Nowak & Sigmund 1993 (Nature), WSLS succeeded unexpectedly: it can correct occasional errors and can also exploit unconditional cooperators, thereby preventing ALLC from drifting in and opening the door to defectors. 【Single-source verified; the abstract's wording is hedged, "seems to be more robust"】 But it has a hard flaw: against always-defect (ALLD), after mutual defection it returns to cooperation, so ALLD can alternately collect T and P. Under 5/3/1/0, 2R = T+P, so WSLS sits exactly on the boundary. 【Single-source verified; this holds only for 5/3/1/0】 This explains why Pavlov did badly in Wu & Axelrod's environment and well in Nowak & Sigmund's—the difference comes from the opponent pool and the payoff settings.

Finite populations. Imhof, Fudenberg & Nowak 2007 compared ALLD, ALLC, TFT and WSLS in noisy finite populations: TFT is never selected as the end state, but it lowers the threshold for WSLS to prevail from b/c>3 (weak-selection limit) to 2, and the authors call it a "catalyst … not the target". 【Single-source verified】

The verdict of this section on noise is clear: in a world where mistakes happen, TFT itself is not the winner; the winners are its variants—more generous (GTFT), contrite (CTFT), or error-correcting (WSLS). What they have in common is that they keep "don't defect first + retaliate conditionally," and leave some slack in the retaliation.

6. Zero-Determinant Strategies: "Extortion" Can Win a Game, Not a Population

In 2012, the physicist William Press and Freeman Dyson published in PNAS "Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma contains strategies that dominate any evolutionary opponent"; the paper was submitted by Press in his capacity as a member of the US National Academy of Sciences. 【Single-source verified】 They discovered a class of "zero-determinant" (ZD) strategies that look only at the previous move: the player using one can unilaterally force the two players' scores to satisfy a linear relation, or pin the opponent's score at a certain value, but cannot pin its own score. Among them, the "extortion" type can guarantee that its own surplus is χ times the opponent's. The abstract says the best response of an "evolutionary" opponent is to "accede to the extortion", and "Only a player with a theory of mind about his opponent can do better". 【Single-source verified】 Note that "evolutionary opponent" here means a single player who adjusts its own strategy, not a population. TFT itself is a special case within the ZD family—the fair one. 【Single-source verified】

Concrete numbers: a χ=3 extortioner facing an opponent who always cooperates earns 3.727 points per move, while the opponent gets only 1.909 (the abstract of Wang et al. 2016 independently gives 3.727 and 1.907). 【Multi-source confirmed; Recomputed and consistent】 But against an opponent who always defects, both get only 1.

The media told it as "Axelrod overturned": Edge wrote "cleverness and unfairness triumph after all", MIT Technology Review headlined it "The Emerging Revolution in Game Theory," and Quanta wrote in 2015 that "the best strategies were selfish ones." 【Single-source verified: all three originals】 On the same Edge page, Sigmund and Nowak responded that strategies able to pin the opponent's score had been known since 1997; Dyson himself said "best for outcome is worst for evolution". 【Single-source verified】

Research in the following years put ZD back into populations:

  • Stewart & Plotkin 2012 (PNAS commentary) added ZD strategies to an Axelrod-style tournament: the generous ZDGTFT-2 had the highest total score, higher than TFT and GTFT; the extortionate Extort-2 won more individual games than any strategy except ALLD, yet had a lower total score. 【Single-source verified】 This is the earliest source of "win every game, lose the tournament."
  • Stewart & Plotkin 2013: extortion succeeds only in very small populations; for all but the smallest population sizes, the robust strategies are generous. 【Single-source verified】
  • Adami & Hintze 2013 (Nature Communications): WSLS loses to ZD in every head-to-head game, yet drives ZD to extinction, because WSLS can cooperate with itself and ZD cannot. A 2014 erratum restricted the conclusion to "only the coercive strategies we examined"—so the accurate statement is that extortionate ZD is unstable, not all ZD. 【Single-source verified】
  • Hilbe, Nowak & Sigmund 2013: extortion can act as a "catalyst" for cooperation in small populations, but is not a stable end state; under their parameters (b=3, c=1), it fails once the population exceeds 10. 【Single-source verified; M>10 holds only for this set of parameters】

How do people deal with extortioners? Hilbe, Röhl & Milinski 2014 had 60 subjects (who did not know their opponent was a computer) each play 60 rounds: the extortionate strategy beat its human opponent in every match, but its total earnings were lower than those of the generous strategy—people cooperated only 34.2% of the time with extortioners and 67.7% with generous players. 【Single-source verified】 People were "punishing" extortion by cooperating less, at the cost of earning less themselves. Wang et al. 2016 found that extortion beat generosity under only one condition: 500 rounds, with subjects knowing their opponent was a computer (2.943 vs. 2.263 per round); under the condition of 60 rounds with uninformed subjects, generous players scored significantly higher. 【Single-source verified】

The review by Hilbe, Chatterjee & Nowak 2018 divides successful strategies into two classes: "partners" (TFT, GTFT, WSLS, Grim—never letting the other's score fall below mutual cooperation) and "rivals" (ALLD, extortioners—never letting their own score fall below the other's). Small populations and short games favor "rivals"; large populations and long games favor "partners." 【Single-source verified】

Verdict: ZD is a real discovery, but what it overturns is a claim no one ever made—that nice players don't lose out in a single game; at the level of populations and tournaments, Axelrod's "nice players come out ahead overall" stands, and the winner is generous ZD, not extortionate ZD.

7. 2004: Half the Entrants Were One Team

To mark the twentieth anniversary of Axelrod's tournaments, the 2004 IEEE Congress on Evolutionary Computation (CEC 2004) reran the competition. The organizer's results page (Wayback archive) shows 223 entered programs in total, of which 112 came from a University of Southampton team, slightly more than half; the "60" reported by Wired and IEEE Spectrum at the time is wrong. 【Single-source verified: row-by-row count of the organizer's results table; consistent with Southampton's own paper, "4 teams × 28 people," and with rivals Slany & Kienreich's "112"】

These programs opened with a pre-agreed sequence of moves to recognize each other: once a teammate was recognized, the "slave" program cooperated throughout, letting the "master" program defect to rack up points; on meeting an outsider, the "slave" defected throughout, dragging down the opponent's score. 【Multi-source confirmed】 As a result, the top three in the standard league (no noise)—StarSN 117,057, StarS 110,611, StarSL 110,511—were all Southampton "masters"; fourth was GRIM (100,611), and TFT ranked 12th (92,962). Southampton's members ranked all the way from 1st to 216th. 【Single-source verified: organizer's results table】 In the league with noise of 0.1, Southampton's lead entry again came first, about 13% ahead of second place (an independent entrant); but this time one of the top three was not from Southampton. 【Single-source verified: the "13% lead" in popular accounts belongs to the noisy league, not the standard league; in the standard league the lead over GRIM was 16.3%】

Team leader Nick Jennings told Wired: "we had far too many -- we would have won with around 20". 【Single-source verified: archived original Wired article of 2004-10-13; reprints lost the context, and the quote is often misattributed to organizer Graham Kendall—overturned in this issue】 The next year (CIG 2005), the organizers actually enforced a cap of 20 per team and added a track limited to "one entry per person." 【Single-source verified: all four teams had exactly 20; the rules' original text is not archived】 Results: the noisy tournament was still won by Southampton; the standard tournament's champion became CosaNostra from the Kienreich team; and in the 50-person one-program-per-person track, the champion was Jiawei Li's APavlov, with Omega TFT second. 【Single-source verified】

What this collusion shows, and what it does not show, both need to be stated clearly. It shows that the tournament's rules decide who wins: without a cap on the number of entries, numbers can be traded for ranking; it also echoes the 2015 finding of Rapoport et al.—that for this 2004 batch of programs, the total-score ranking and the ranking by games won were negatively correlated (ρ=−0.45, significant). 【Single-source verified】 But it does not show that "collusive strategies are better than TFT": Shalizi pointed out at the time that the master–slave structure is not evolutionarily stable, and "slaves" have no reason to stay on the team. 【Single-source verified: a commentator's view】 And once each person could enter only one program, the top two went back to variants of Pavlov and TFT.

8. Today's Big Tournaments: TFT Sits Mid-Table, Its "Character" Still Near the Top

The open-source library Axelrod-Python puts several hundred strategies into the same codebase, so "who wins" can be recomputed against a much larger pool of opponents. Three sets of results are cited most often, but they come from the same research group and use the same strategy library, so they cannot count as three independent replications. 【Methodology audit: same group, same library, not independent】

  • Harper et al. 2017 (PLoS ONE): 176 strategies, with a standard tournament (200 moves) and a 5%-noise tournament each run 50,000 times. In the standard tournament, the top 11 by median score were all strategies trained with evolutionary algorithms and particle swarm optimization; TFT did not win a single one of the 50,000 tournaments, nor a single game. But the authors also found that the top-ranked strategies all never defect first—Axelrod's "nice" is still there. 【Single-source verified; the "reinforcement learning" in the title is actually evolutionary algorithms plus PSO; the trained strategies were trained against the same library, and the paper held out no out-of-sample opponent pool】
  • Glynatsi, Knight & Harper 2024 (PLoS Computational Biology): 195 strategies, about 45,600 tournaments (the same paper elsewhere writes 11,420×4, an inconsistent count). The conclusion is that no single strategy excels in all environments; successful strategies are "nice, provocable and generous, a little envious, clever, and adapt to the environment", and perform best when their probability of cooperating matches the population's overall level of cooperation; "nice" is clearly advantageous only without noise or in long games. 【Single-source verified; the authors themselves acknowledge that trained strategies "tend to overfit"】
  • The standing tournament (v4.13.1, 2024-10-30): 236 strategies, 200 moves, 100 repetitions, a single random seed. TFT ranks 82nd in the standard tournament and 61st in the 5%-noise tournament; ranked ahead of it are its improved versions—Omega TFT 12th, Gradual 15th, Spiteful TFT 19th; the champion, EvolvedLookerUp2_2_2, likewise never defects first. 【Single-source verified: row-by-row check of the CSV. But ranks 70 to 95 differ in score by only about 1%, and ranks 78 to 82 differ by less than 0.0015, so "82nd" can only be read as "upper mid-table, roughly top 35%"】

Rapoport, Seale & Colman 2015 (first author Amnon Rapoport was himself an entrant in the first tournament; the program he co-wrote with Stein ranked sixth) took another angle: using the first tournament's score table, they enumerated all 756,756 two-stage groupings of "three groups of five, round robin within groups, then a final," and TFT won in only 11.0% of the groupings, versus 30.0% for Tideman & Chieruzzi; ranked by games won, TFT came last. The correlation coefficient between the total-score ranking and the games-won ranking was ρ=−0.103, not significantly correlated (p>0.70). 【Single-source verified; 756,756 counts partitions with group labels; unordered partitions number 126,126, which does not affect the percentages】

Verdict: "TFT is the optimal strategy" does not hold in today's large opponent pools; "strategies with TFT's character come out ahead" broadly holds, with the addition of "a bit more generous, and adapted to the environment." Studies from the same group corroborating each other is not independent replication; the strength of evidence for this conclusion is "Single-source verified," not "Multi-source confirmed."

9. The Lab, Animals and the Trenches

Human experiments. Dal Bó & Fréchette 2011 (AER) found that in the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, cooperation does emerge, but under conditions stricter than "cooperation is a subgame-perfect equilibrium," and even stricter than "cooperation is risk-dominant." 【Single-source verified: abstract】 Their 2018 review (JEL) pooled 15 papers, 32 treatments, 2,415 subjects and 157,170 choices: cooperation rates are high only when cooperation is very robust to uncertainty about whether the other side will cooperate. 【Single-source verified: numbers from the 2016 working paper; final version not checked】 Under perfect monitoring, the strategies subjects used most were always-defect, grim trigger (Grim) and TFT—together accounting for more than half in 15 of 17 treatments; WSLS never reached a notable share. 【Single-source verified】

Noise changes people's strategies. Fudenberg, Rand & Dreber 2012 (AER) found that under conditions where actions are flipped with some probability, subjects became "slow to anger and fast to forgive". The breakdown in the review is: in noisy treatments, lenient multi-state strategies account for 67%, TFT only 7%, always-defect 14%, Grim 5%; the often-cited "TFT 15%" comes from the same study's no-noise control. 【Single-source verified: review Table 11】 This is strikingly consistent with the theory in Section 5: once the world is error-prone, people spontaneously become more forgiving than TFT.

Animals. The two most-cited examples both have primary data: blood-sharing among wild vampire bats depends on both kinship and opportunities for reciprocity (Wilkinson 1984); among captive vampire bats, how much a bat received was the strongest predictor of how much it gave, 8.5 times as important as kinship, and 64% of sharing pairs were unrelated (Carter & Wilkinson 2013). 【Single-source verified】 But later research shows it is more like "a relationship that escalates step by step" than round-by-round bookkeeping: unfamiliar bats first groom each other, then progress to costlier blood-sharing (Carter et al. 2020). 【Single-source verified】 Milinski 1987 used a mirror to make sticklebacks believe a companion was approaching a predator alongside them, and reported their behavior as "consistent with TFT," but alternative explanations were soon proposed (Lazarus & Metcalfe 1990). 【Single-source verified; content of the critique not seen in the original】

The mainstream wording at the review level is that firm evidence of reciprocity in animal societies is rare (Clutton-Brock 2009, Nature), with Stevens & Hauser 2004 and Raihani & Bshary 2011 in the same direction. 【Multi-source confirmed】 The other side argues that it is "rare" because TFT is used as the definition of reciprocity: precise round-by-round reciprocity is indeed rare, but looser forms of reciprocity (Carter 2014; Schweinfurth & Call 2019 consider it "might be a widespread phenomenon") may be common. 【Direction contested: the latter is a revisionist view, not consensus】

The WWI trenches. Chapter 4 of The Evolution of Cooperation uses Tony Ashworth's 1980 historical study to present the "live and let live" understandings in the Western Front trenches as a real-world case of the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma. In the same chapter, Axelrod himself acknowledged three things unfavorable to simple TFT: "Ashworth does not put it this way"; retaliation was often "two-for-one or three-for-one"; and the understanding was ultimately ended by raids ordered by high command. 【Single-source verified】 The statistician Gelman argued in 2008 that the trench soldiers were "not generally in a prisoner's dilemma", and that restraint was more like directly reducing their own risk. 【Single-source verified; this issue found no systematic critique by a professional WWI historian】

10. Large Language Models Enter the Game

After 2023, researchers began having large language models play the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma. The results are much more complicated than the popular headlines.

  • Akata et al. (Nature Human Behaviour, 2025): a finitely repeated game of 10 rounds with known length. When GPT-4 faced an opponent that defected once and then cooperated thereafter, it "never cooperates again", like grim trigger; only after being prompted that "the other player may make mistakes" did it resume cooperating. 【Single-source verified】
  • Fontana et al. (2024 preprint): Llama2, Llama3 and GPT-3.5 played 100 rounds against opponents moving randomly at various defection rates. When the opponent's defection rate was below 30%, the models were more forgiving than humans and "tend not to initiate defection"; but Llama2 and Llama3, both from Meta, went in opposite directions. 【Single-source verified】
  • Payne & Alloui-Cros (2025 preprint, still v1): models from OpenAI, Google and Anthropic were entered alongside classic strategies in 7 evolutionary tournaments, and the results were summarized as "strategic fingerprints": Gemini "strategically ruthless", OpenAI "highly cooperative", Claude "the most forgiving reciprocator". 【Single-source verified: original wording】

This last paper spread the most widely, and the methodology audit seat gave it a C−: each condition was run only once, with no random seeds and no confidence intervals; the evolutionary rule amplified a gap of 0.07 points per move into an outcome of "one family goes extinct, one holds 16 seats"; the three vendors' sampling settings were asymmetric (OpenAI and Claude were set to temperature 0.7, Gemini used the API default); whenever an API error or a format-parsing failure occurred, the code silently recorded it as "cooperate"—92/3923 of Claude's decisions came about this way; "Claude is the most forgiving" comes from only one tournament and one claude-3-haiku. 【Methodology veto: the family labels, "Claude is the most forgiving," and the evolutionary outcomes may not carry weight】

Independent research only partly corroborates it. A 2026 preprint from Nowak's group (five models) likewise found Claude leaning "forgiving" and Gemini the only one that is suspicious on the first round (still cooperating 41 out of 50 times); but GPT-4o opened as grim trigger, and GPT-5 defects on a known final round; with "competitive" wording most models trended toward always-defect, and with "altruistic" wording toward always-cooperate; the authors conclude that "none of them exhibit full consistency over all tasks". 【Single-source verified: preprint】 Willis et al. 2025 had models write complete strategies and then ran evolution: GPT-4o produced the most aggressive equilibria, Claude 3.5 Sonnet the fewest. 【Single-source verified】 Two other studies (Li & Shirado, EMNLP 2025; CoopEval, ICML 2026) found that models with stronger reasoning cooperate less in one-shot dilemmas. 【Single-source verified: abstract】

Verdict: "vendor personalities" can only be written as tendencies of specific versions under specific prompts, not as stable fingerprints. The only thing that recurs across studies is a phenomenon isomorphic to Axelrod in Section 5: when a model is unforgiving, explicitly telling it that "the other side may make mistakes" makes it more lenient.

What remains at the core after forty years: don't defect first, retaliate conditionally, and in an error-prone world give a little more forgiveness. These three hold up in the original tournaments, noise theory, population studies of zero-determinant strategies, modern large tournaments, and human laboratories; what does not hold up is "this one TFT program is the optimal solution" and "niceness will always take over the world."

12. Ten Testable Claims

Ordered by strength of evidence:

  1. In tournaments with no cap on the number of entries, collusion can buy ranking. Test: in any public tournament that allows one person multiple entries, measure the relationship between the number of programs from the same source and that source's best rank. (The 2004 organizer's results table already provides one example)
  2. With each step up in noise, the winner's forgiveness increases. Test: in Axelrod-Python, fix the opponent pool, sweep noise from 0% to 10%, and record the champion strategy's probability of cooperating after the opponent defects. (Wu & Axelrod 1995 and FRD 2012 point the same way)
  3. Champion strategies "never defecting first" continues to hold in noiseless large tournaments. Test: after each update of the standing tournament, check whether the top 10 are all nice.
  4. TFT's rank is sensitive to the random seed; a single ranking cannot be taken as a precise rank. Test: switch the standing tournament to 100 seeds and report the interval of TFT's rank.
  5. Extortionate strategies are eliminated by generous strategies in large populations. Test: vary the population size N in replicator dynamics and record the critical size at which extortionate ZD disappears. (Stewart & Plotkin and Hilbe 2013 give the direction)
  6. People facing extortioners will sacrifice their own payoff to punish them. Test: repeat the Hilbe 2014 design with a larger sample, preregistering "cooperation rate with extortioners lower than with generous players."
  7. The advantage of trained strategies shrinks against out-of-sample opponent pools. Test: rerun the champion strategies of Harper 2017 against an independently written strategy pool outside Axelrod-Python.
  8. Prompt framing affects large models' cooperation rates more than vendor differences do. Test: fix the model, sweep "competitive/neutral/altruistic" framings, and compare the effect size with cross-vendor differences.
  9. Telling a large model that "the other side may make mistakes" raises its forgiveness rate. Test: replicate Akata's mistake-prompt manipulation across multiple models and report confidence intervals.
  10. Animal reciprocity mostly appears as "relationships that escalate step by step" rather than round-by-round bookkeeping. Test: in more species, separate the explanatory power of two predictors, "return in the most recent round" and "long-term relationship strength."

Appendix: Method and This Issue's Self-Corrections

During the research, this issue overturned several popular formulations that we ourselves had initially accepted: the second tournament's "average of 200 moves" (actually five shared game lengths, averaging 151); the ecological simulation's "TFT went to fixation" (an extrapolation); Southampton's "60 programs" (112); "would have won with around 20" coming from the organizer (actually Jennings); "entrants in the second tournament all wanted to beat TFT" (no primary basis); "zero-determinant strategies are unstable" (restricted to the extortionate type after the erratum); and "TFT accounts for 15%" in human experiments (the no-noise control figure; the noisy treatments give 7%).

Primary documents this issue could not obtain: the full text of Axelrod 1980b (TFT's score in the second tournament, the 6 variants, the original sentence on 14.5% at the 1,000th generation), the original text of the 1984 book (the original wording of the four properties and four pieces of advice, the Chapter 2 figure of the ecological simulation), the original Fudenberg-Rand-Dreber 2012 (noise rate), the main texts of Molander 1985 and Boyd & Lorberbaum 1987, and the main text of the 2004 organizers' book chapter. The related claims have all been downgraded to secondary or "Unverified."
